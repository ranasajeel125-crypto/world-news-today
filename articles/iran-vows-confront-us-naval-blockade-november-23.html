Iran Vows to Use 'All Available Tools' to Confront US Naval Blockade as Tankers Gather at Kharg Island

world · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Oil tankers at anchor near Kharg Island, Iran's main crude export terminal, where more than a dozen vessels have gathered as Tehran weighs a challenge to the US naval blockade.

Iran will use all available tools to confront the US naval blockade, its Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said at a weekly news conference on Monday, in Tehran's sharpest public challenge yet to the American operation that has stalled its oil exports for months.

The blockade on Iran-related oil exports was imposed on 13 April, lifted in June after Washington reached a draft understanding with Tehran, and then restored on 14 July after the agreement collapsed, according to Reuters. Baghaei did not say what specific steps Iran would take, but his warning came as signs grow that Tehran is preparing to test the blockade at sea.

More than 14 tankers have gathered near Kharg Island, Iran's most important crude-oil export terminal, which handles roughly 85 to 90 percent of the country's crude exports, Euronews reported on Friday, citing satellite imagery and ship-tracking data. Crews have been assigned to the vessels and Iranian flags have been raised ahead of a possible convoy toward the Sea of Oman, according to the report. Iranian media linked to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps have discussed sending a convoy of tankers loaded with crude to China, the destination for almost all of Iran's oil before the war.

Whether any convoy will actually sail is far from settled. Maritime intelligence firms have cautioned that claims of an imminent convoy remain unconfirmed, and TankerTrackers, a shipping intelligence firm that monitors Iranian oil exports, pushed back firmly against the idea that a challenge to the blockade was under way. “Wishful thinking. That's definitely not what's going on,” the firm said in a social media post. The distinction matters: a group of tankers sitting at anchor is not the same as a convoy under way, and so far no such sailing has been confirmed.

Washington has moved in recent days to close the routes Iran has been using to keep some oil moving. US Central Command issued a maritime advisory last Wednesday warning that ships receiving cargo at sea from vessels carrying goods loaded at Iranian ports could themselves be treated as violating the blockade, even if they never enter Iranian waters. American forces said such vessels could be stopped, boarded, diverted or captured, or ordered to return their cargo to its Iranian port of origin.

The warning followed reports of a ship-to-ship transfer involving about 200,000 barrels of Iranian crude in the Persian Gulf. The sanctioned Suezmax tanker Olym was reported positioned alongside the Hao Fan, a medium-range tanker that was allegedly spoofing its location to appear near Oman, with the transfer identified by United Against Nuclear Iran. Bloomberg separately reported that a cargo of Iranian crude had been transferred from a sanctioned Suezmax tanker to a medium-range vessel not under sanctions, according to information from United Against Nuclear Iran and vessel tracker Kpler. Such transfers are one of the main ways sanctioned oil can be moved onto a vessel that is not itself under restrictions.

The blockade has already dealt a heavy blow to Iran's oil trade. In March, despite the war, Iran was exporting about two million barrels per day of crude oil and condensates, almost all of it to China. Since July, those exports have dried up completely, according to Kpler, and Iranian production has fallen to 1.8 million barrels per day, half its level before the war. US Central Command said on Saturday that the blockade had reached 130 consecutive days, and it credited American forces with preventing the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps from exporting more than $25 billion worth of crude oil.

The standoff is also reshaping the wider oil market. Brent crude has dropped by more than 20 percent since the start of the month to just over $90 a barrel, Reuters reported on Monday, as recovering Middle East exports and fading fears of a major escalation have driven the decline. But the recovery is uneven: plentiful crude supplies are masking a stubborn diesel shortage, with global seaborne diesel exports averaging just 6.2 million barrels per day so far this year. Oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz slowed to around 9.5 million barrels per day in the 10 days through 19 November, from an average of 12.2 million barrels per day in October, according to Kpler. The Middle East disruption has resulted in the loss of nearly three billion barrels of oil, and inventories have fallen by a cumulative 462 million barrels since the start of the war, according to the International Energy Agency.

In Washington, meanwhile, the debate over what comes next appears to be tilting toward economic pressure rather than new strikes. President Donald Trump reportedly favours intensifying the economic campaign against Iran over renewed major military strikes, according to a report by Israel Hayom cited by Breitbart, which said the president was presented with four options by his advisers: negotiating an agreement, launching another major offensive, conducting limited strikes against nuclear and military facilities, or intensifying the economic campaign. The report, citing three diplomatic sources, said the economic option had support from Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and that it included pressing Gulf governments to help shut down Iranian oil-smuggling routes through Iraq. The report has not been confirmed by the White House, and Trump has repeatedly signalled reluctance to resume major strikes, describing another offensive as “highly unlikely” and saying he is in no rush to decide.

The economic campaign is already widening beyond the sea. US Central Command announced Task Force Iron Gate two weeks ago to disrupt illicit revenue, weapons shipments and sanctioned materials moving across Iran's land borders and the Caspian Sea, extending the interdiction effort to the overland routes Tehran might use as alternatives to its blocked ports.

The confrontation has its roots in the war that began in late February, when Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz and US-Iran negotiations collapsed. The blockade announced on 13 April targeted all maritime traffic entering and exiting Iranian ports, with a reported legal basis in the San Remo Manual on International Law Applicable to Armed Conflicts at Sea. A ceasefire in early April failed to restore commercial transits or freedom of navigation through the strait, and the draft understanding reached in June, which briefly lifted the blockade, collapsed within weeks, bringing the restrictions back on 14 July.

What happens next turns on whether the tankers at Kharg Island move. If a convoy does sail toward the Sea of Oman, US forces would face the choice of intercepting it, with all the escalation risk that carries, or letting it pass and weakening the blockade. Fars News Agency, which is linked to the Revolutionary Guard Corps, has warned that any American interference with Iranian oil shipments could trigger retaliatory attacks against energy infrastructure across the Persian Gulf, a threat that hangs over every decision in the region. If the tankers stay put, the blockade continues to squeeze an Iranian economy that has already lost its main source of income, and Tehran's vow to use “all available tools” will be tested against the reality that its oil has nowhere to go.

Why it matters: the blockade is the sharpest economic weapon in the US-Iran confrontation, and it is now being tested at sea. Iran's exports, once two million barrels a day, have effectively stopped, its production has halved, and more than $25 billion in crude has been kept from the market, according to US figures. The gathering of tankers at Kharg Island, the warnings from both sides, and the tightening of the rules on ship-to-ship transfers all point to a moment when the standoff could either harden into a longer economic siege or flare into a direct confrontation in the Gulf. The oil market, for now, is betting on the siege: Brent has fallen sharply as fears of escalation fade, but the diesel shortage and the sheer scale of the oil already lost show how quickly that calm could break if the tankers at Kharg Island start to move.
