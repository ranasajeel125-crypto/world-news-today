India Ordered Website Takedowns on Fake Court Orders, Reuters Finds, as Police Launch Probe

technology · 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read · By World News Today (Technology Desk)

The two fake orders bore blue court seals with India’s national emblem and were published on the telecoms department’s website; both remained there as of Wednesday, Reuters reported.

India's government ordered internet providers to block 129 illegal betting websites this year based on two state high court orders that Reuters has found were fake, findings that have triggered at least one police investigation.

India's Department of Telecommunications issued two website-blocking directions, on 21 July and 2 September, both published on its website as part of routine uploads. Each came attached with emailed copies of purported court orders from the high courts of Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh ordering the removal of dozens of websites.

A Reuters review found neither court order appears in the courts' online registration records. The Rajasthan High Court's registrar said in an email, after questions from the news agency, that the order bearing its name "appears to be fraudulent" and did not exist on its server.

In Uttar Pradesh, the Allahabad High Court found its order was fake and filed a police complaint on 6 November, prompted by Reuters' findings, according to police case documents, which also showed an investigation against unknown persons has been initiated for alleged forgery and cheating. "It is fake. It is not a genuine order from High Court of Allahabad," senior state cybercrime official Ghanshyam Yadav told Reuters by telephone on Tuesday. He said no arrests had been made.

Between them, the two orders affected 129 websites featuring gambling, which is illegal under Indian law. Reuters could not ascertain who created the fake orders, which bore blue court seals with India's national emblem, typical of genuine court orders. Both remained on the ministry's website as of Wednesday.

The forgeries are the latest sign that fake orders are slipping past India's official content-removal mechanism, considered one of the world's strictest, and could affect the online presence of any organisation operating in the country. Court precedents generated using artificial intelligence have caused concern among judges around the world; India's Supreme Court in July set aside a tribunal's orders that relied on non-existent judgments. The telecoms department, which issued the two takedown directives, did not respond to Reuters' queries.

The fake Rajasthan order arrived first. The telecoms department received the purported order by email at 8:59 p.m. on Sunday, 19 July, signed by "Aryan Chaudhary", described as an advocate-on-record. A Reuters query to the email used by Chaudhary bounced back as "Recipient not found". Two days later, the government told internet service providers to remove 34 betting websites. The Rajasthan High Court has since confirmed the order was fake and that no such document existed on its servers, and the court is considering legal action of its own.

The fake Allahabad order, dated 14 August, carried the court's logo and seal and a QR code that led to a random webpage displaying a one-line case number, "Writ -- A 30480/2026". It was sent to Indian officials on 22 August by an individual who identified himself as advocate Kartikey Pandey, and was signed under the name of "Justice Garima Prashad", whose office has confirmed that "no such order has been passed by Her Ladyship". On 2 September, an official told internet service providers to block access to 95 betting websites. The order was spotted on the telecoms department's website on 30 October by a Reuters reporter, who emailed the Allahabad High Court to verify it. The court could not find the order, and an internal investigation confirmed no such order was ever passed — a conclusion also confirmed by Justice Prashad's private secretary.

The court's own inquiry was thorough. After the matter was brought to the attention of the Chief Justice of the Allahabad High Court, the Registrar General and the court's computer section were tasked with verifying the document. System Manager Arindam Lahiri examined the court's digital servers and databases and submitted a report on 3 November 2026, which found that the case cited in the purported order — Writ Petition No. 30480/2026 — did not exist in the court's records and had never been registered, and that no such order had been uploaded to the court's portal for 14 August 2026, the date on which it was allegedly issued.

The fallout spread quickly. India has launched an inquiry into how telecoms officials blocked the websites based on the forged orders, while police have sought ministry records as part of the criminal investigation, officials said on 12 November. The telecoms ministry's inquiry will review its procedures and consider additional checks before officials act on court orders seeking website takedowns, a government official with direct knowledge of the matter said. The ministry plans to revoke the blocking directives issued on the basis of the forged orders and remove related documents from its website, and is trying to identify those behind the email addresses used to send them. As of that Thursday, correspondence with the alleged fraudsters and copies of the forged orders remained available online. Then, on Saturday, Prime Minister Narendra Modi's office summoned Telecom Secretary Amit Agrawal to explain how officials ordered the takedown of more than 100 websites based on fake court orders, cutting short Agrawal's trip to another state. On Friday 13 November, the department unblocked 95 of the 129 websites with immediate effect, instructing all internet licensees to restore access to the listed domains and marking the order to the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology.

The episode is part of a wider pattern. Elon Musk's X, which has in the past challenged the government's expanding blocking powers, last week itself got embroiled in fake orders issues. The platform told an Indian court that police directives it sent to two free speech groups warning them about removal of their accounts were found to be fake. Separately, the Internet Freedom Foundation reported receiving a fake legal notice purportedly sent on behalf of the RSS, the Hindu nationalist organisation. The case comes amid growing concern over fake and AI-generated legal material: India's Supreme Court in July struck down a tribunal ruling that relied on non-existent judgments, while a US judge in June disqualified lawyers over fabricated legal citations. It was not clear how the fake Indian orders were generated or whether AI was used.

What makes the case alarming is not the target — online betting is illegal in India, and few will mourn the blocked sites — but the method. India's website-blocking mechanism, built around directions issued under the Information Technology Act, is one of the most powerful content-removal systems in the world, and it operates largely on trust: when a document arrives bearing a court's seal and a judge's name, officials act on it. The Reuters investigation shows that trust can be exploited by anyone with a forged seal, a fake email address and a plausible case number — and that, had the targets been a news outlet, a charity or a political group rather than betting sites, the same machinery could have silenced them before anyone checked. The government's response — an internal inquiry, a criminal investigation, the unblocking of the 95 sites and a summons from the Prime Minister's Office — suggests officials now understand the scale of the gap. Whether the new checks they promise will close it is the question that will decide whether the next forged order is caught before, or after, the websites disappear.
