India Ordered Website Takedowns on Fake Court Orders, Reuters Finds, as Police Launch Probe

technology · 11 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (Technology Desk)

The two fake orders bore blue court seals with India’s national emblem and were published on the telecoms department’s website; both remained there as of Wednesday, Reuters reported.

India’s government ordered internet providers to block 129 illegal betting websites this year based on two state high court orders that Reuters has found were fake, findings that have triggered at least one police investigation.

India’s Department of Telecommunications issued two website-blocking directions, on 21 July and 2 September, both published on its website as part of routine uploads. Each came attached with emailed copies of purported court orders from the high courts of Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh ordering the removal of dozens of websites.

A Reuters review found neither court order appears in the courts’ online registration records. The Rajasthan High Court’s registrar said in an email, after questions from the news agency, that the order bearing its name “appears to be fraudulent” and did not exist on its server.

In Uttar Pradesh, the Allahabad High Court found its order was fake and filed a police complaint on 6 November, prompted by Reuters’ findings, according to police case documents, which also showed an investigation against unknown persons has been initiated for alleged forgery and cheating. “It is fake. It is not a genuine order from High Court of Allahabad,” senior state cybercrime official Ghanshyam Yadav told Reuters by telephone on Tuesday. He said no arrests had been made.

Between them, the two orders affected 129 websites featuring gambling, which is illegal under Indian law. Reuters could not ascertain who created the fake orders, which bore blue court seals with India’s national emblem, typical of genuine court orders. Both remained on the ministry’s website as of Wednesday.

The forgeries are the latest sign that fake orders are slipping past India’s official content-removal mechanism, considered one of the world’s strictest, and could affect the online presence of any organisation operating in the country. Court precedents generated using artificial intelligence have caused concern among judges around the world; India’s Supreme Court in July set aside a tribunal’s orders that relied on non-existent judgments. The telecoms department, which issued the two takedown directives, did not respond to Reuters’ queries.

Elon Musk’s X, which has in the past challenged the government’s expanding blocking powers, last week itself got embroiled in fake orders issues. The platform told an Indian court that police directives it sent to two free speech groups warning them about removal of their accounts were found to be fake.

The telecoms department received the purported Rajasthan order by email at 8:59 p.m. on Sunday, 19 July, signed by “Aryan Chaudhary”, described as an advocate-on-record. A Reuters query to the email used by Chaudhary bounced back as “Recipient not found”. Two days later, the government told internet service providers to remove 34 betting websites. The fake Allahabad order, dated 14 August, carried the court’s logo and seal and a QR code that led to a random webpage displaying a one-line case number, “Writ -- A 30480/2026”. It was sent to Indian officials on 22 August by an individual who identified himself as advocate Kartikey Pandey, and was signed under the name of “Justice Garima Prashad”, whose office has confirmed that “no such order has been passed by Her Ladyship”. On 2 September, an official told internet service providers to block access to 95 betting websites.
