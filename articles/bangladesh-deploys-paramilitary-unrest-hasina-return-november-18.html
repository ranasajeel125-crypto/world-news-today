Bangladesh Deploys Paramilitary Troops as Unrest Grows Over Hasina's Planned Return

world · 18 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Supporters of the banned Awami League have taken to the streets as former prime minister Sheikh Hasina, in exile in India since 2024, presses to return to Bangladesh in December.

Bangladesh stepped up security and deployed hundreds of paramilitary troops across the country on Wednesday as the planned return of former prime minister Sheikh Hasina set off clashes, bomb blasts and arson attacks blamed on supporters of her banned party, Reuters reported.

Hasina has lived in exile in neighbouring India since she fled Dhaka following violent protests demanding her resignation in August 2024. She has requested a new passport and identity card, saying she plans to return in December. But the government has so far rejected her request, leading members and supporters of her Awami League party to take to the streets in protest.

Last week, the party announced a week-long programme of protests and rallies to mobilise support for Hasina's return, culminating in a nationwide lockdown on November 19. The deployment of paramilitary forces is the government's answer to a campaign that has already spread well beyond the capital.

Police have recorded dozens of crude bomb blasts and incidents of vehicles being torched in at least 28 districts over the last 10 days, with several police personnel and civilians injured, local media has reported. The geography of the violence — spread across more than a third of the country's districts rather than confined to Dhaka — is what has pushed the authorities to a nationwide security response.

Late on Tuesday, an activist of the ruling Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) was allegedly killed by unidentified people during a protest near Hasina's home district of Gopalganj, police said. On the same night, a crude bomb exploded outside the Dhaka residence of Cultural Affairs Minister Nitai Roy Chowdhury, and the house of a BNP lawmaker and shops belonging to BNP supporters in Gopalganj were set on fire, police added.

On Wednesday morning, a bus company helper was burnt to death in the southern district of Pirojpur after two buses were set on fire, authorities said. The killing, far from the political centres of Dhaka and Gopalganj, underlines how the unrest has reached ordinary workers and public transport, not only party offices and politicians' homes.

Tuesday marked the day on which Hasina was sentenced to death a year ago by a local court that found her guilty of ordering a deadly crackdown on the student-led uprising against her in 2024. The anniversary appears to have been the trigger for the latest wave of action: the Awami League's protest programme was timed around it, and the violence has intensified since.

At least 37 Grameen Bank branches and offices have been targeted in arson attacks or attempted fires since November 10, with 14 more attacked since midnight on Tuesday, the bank said. The bank was founded by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus, who led Bangladesh's interim government after Hasina's ouster and has faced hostility from Hasina's supporters. Attacking the bank is a symbolic choice as much as a practical one: it strikes at the institution most closely associated with the man who governed Bangladesh after her fall.

Dhaka Metropolitan Police have arrested at least 71 members of banned organisations, including the Awami League and its affiliated outfits, in the last 24 hours, police spokesperson Niaz Mehedi said. The scale of the arrests, coming alongside the troop deployment, shows the government treating the unrest as an organised campaign rather than a series of isolated incidents.

On Tuesday, Home Minister Salahuddin Ahmed said Awami League leaders abroad were inciting supporters to destabilise Bangladesh and torch property, adding that those involved in violence would be identified and prosecuted. Awami League leaders, most of whom are in hiding, could not be reached for comment.

A return two years in the making: Hasina's request for a new passport and identity card, made through her lawyers at London-based Matrix Chambers, was the clearest step yet towards her plan to come home in December, more than two years after a deadly uprising ended her long rule. Her lawyers wrote to Preeti Rahman, acting high commissioner at Bangladesh's embassy in Delhi, asking for the applications to be processed expeditiously on the basis of her Bangladeshi nationality. Bangladesh's war-crimes court sentenced her to death in November 2025 in her absence for ordering the deadly crackdown on the student-led uprising, though she has denied the charges from exile. She has told Reuters she plans to return to surrender before a court.

Her Awami League party was the country's largest before it was banned following her fall. Many of her party colleagues have either been jailed or are in self-imposed exile in India or elsewhere. Having ruled Bangladesh for 20 years as prime minister across multiple terms, Hasina still enjoys support among sections of the population, and a return could sharpen political divisions as the government in Dhaka seeks to restore stability after more than two years of upheaval.

On the other hand, it could improve Bangladesh's strained ties with India, which deteriorated sharply after New Delhi gave her refuge. The two neighbours have been at odds over her presence in India ever since she fled, and any move by Dhaka to let her return — or to keep her out — carries a diplomatic cost in one direction or the other.

What happens next: the immediate test is the nationwide lockdown called for November 19, which will show how much street strength the Awami League can still muster and how far the government is willing to go to stop it. Beyond that, the standoff turns on the passport request: as long as Dhaka refuses to issue the documents, Hasina cannot travel home legally, and her December return plan remains a statement of intent rather than a journey. If she does return, she faces a death sentence imposed in her absence and has said she will surrender before a court — a confrontation that would put Bangladesh's judiciary, its politics and its fragile stability to the test at once.

Why it matters: Bangladesh is a country of more than 170 million people that has spent two years trying to rebuild its politics after an uprising toppled a leader who had ruled for two decades. The violence of the past ten days shows that the old order has not gone away — it is organised, it can reach 28 districts at once, and it is willing to burn buses, banks and homes to force its leader's return. How Dhaka handles the lockdown and the December deadline will decide whether the country moves towards a settlement of its unfinished 2024 reckoning, or slides back into the cycle of street violence that brought Hasina down in the first place.
