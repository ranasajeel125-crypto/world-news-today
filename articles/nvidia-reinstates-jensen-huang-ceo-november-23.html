Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO in Extraordinary Reversal as Shares Soar

business · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

Jensen Huang, who built Nvidia from a video-game chip designer into the dominant supplier of AI processors, is back as chief executive 11 days after resigning.

Nvidia said on Monday it was reinstating Jensen Huang as chief executive, effective immediately, in an extraordinary reversal under pressure from US President Donald Trump, its employees, its shareholders and its business partners. Nvidia shares surged more than 31% in premarket trading on the news.

The market reaction was emphatic. In early trading, Nvidia stock leapt 33%, while the Nasdaq rallied 6% and the S&P 500 rose 4%, making up for Friday's plunge, as investors had ditched AI-exposed shares, in part driven by uncertainty at Nvidia itself. Tech shares led gains across most major indices, with Europe's STOXX 600 trading up 1.5%.

The about-face is striking because it comes less than a week after the board said the opposite. Board chairman Harvey Jones said last week that the company would not rehire Huang despite the growing campaign for his return. On Monday, the board said it was satisfied with Huang's candour and leadership ability and, after receiving input from senior US government officials, saw no national security risk from his return. Nvidia also said Huang had been reinstated as a director.

President Trump welcomed the decision on Monday and said Huang's return poses no risk to US national security. Trump said last week that he wanted to see Huang back in the top job, and the White House confirmed that the president had called into an Nvidia board meeting. The board's decision to bring Huang back highlights his deep influence over Nvidia and raises questions about how dependent one of the world's most valuable companies remains on its co-founder.

Huang resigned on 12 November following revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with a businesswoman whom US authorities suspect of spying for China, an allegation Beijing denies. His departure ended 33 years of founder-led control and reverberated from Silicon Valley to Taipei and Washington.

The pressure to reverse course built quickly. A public petition calling for his reinstatement drew more than 180,000 signatures, including one from Elon Musk, underscoring the unusually strong support for an executive who had stepped down amid controversy. Employees, shareholders and business partners joined the campaign, and the president added his voice last week.

Huang built Nvidia from a video-game chip designer into the dominant supplier of artificial-intelligence processors, playing a central role in its chip roadmap, major customer relationships and ties with Washington. That centrality was the heart of the case for his return: the deals, the diplomacy and the strategy that made Nvidia the bellwether of the AI boom were, in large part, his.

Since his resignation, the company had been run by interim chief executive Colette Kress, Nvidia's longtime chief financial officer. The company said on Monday that Kress would continue as chief financial officer, returning to the role she held before she stepped in to steady the company.

The reversal comes six days after Nvidia reported record quarterly revenue of $115.8 billion and the board used the same day to say it did not intend to bring Huang back. The investigations around his departure continue: the US Securities and Exchange Commission is probing Nvidia after the resignation, US intelligence agencies are trying to determine the extent of the alleged spy's ties to China's Ministry of State Security, and Huang has agreed to meet US lawmakers on 1 December to answer questions about chips and national security.

Why it matters: Nvidia is the company whose fortunes are read as a verdict on whether the trillions being poured into data centres are paying off, and Monday's surge shows how much of that verdict still rests on one man. The board spent less than two weeks insisting its founder was gone for good, then brought him back under pressure from the White House, its own workforce and its shareholders — a sequence that settles the succession question for now but leaves open the deeper one: whether the world's most valuable chipmaker can ever be run by anyone else.
