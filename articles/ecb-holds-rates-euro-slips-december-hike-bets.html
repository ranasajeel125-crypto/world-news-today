ECB Holds Rates Steady as Energy Shock Lifts Inflation Risks — Euro Slips, December Hike Bets Firm

business · 29 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

The ECB kept its deposit rate at 2.50% on Thursday, saying energy prices have continued to rise and pose upside risks to inflation, while the euro fell to its lowest since last May.

The European Central Bank left interest rates unchanged on Thursday in a well-telegraphed move, and said inflation risks were on the rise from surging energy costs, a warning that could solidify market bets for a December rate hike, Reuters reported.

The Governing Council kept the three key ECB interest rates unchanged: the deposit facility at 2.50%, the main refinancing operations rate at 2.65% and the marginal lending facility at 2.90%, the ECB said in its monetary policy statement.

"Energy prices have continued to rise, posing upside risks to the inflation outlook and downside risks to the growth outlook," the ECB said. "While the full effects of the energy shock are still unfolding, underlying inflation has remained broadly stable so far and wages do not show a material response at this stage."

The central bank added that financing conditions had tightened since its last meeting, which is likely to weigh on both inflation and growth, and said it would follow a data-dependent, meeting-by-meeting approach, without pre-committing to a particular rate path.

The euro fell to its lowest since last May, trading around $1.11, while rate-sensitive two-year government bond yields fell sharply. Germany's 10-year bond yield, the euro zone benchmark, was down about 6 basis points at 3.32%, and European stocks edged higher, with the broad STOXX 600 index up around 1% on the day. The market read the ECB's message as slightly more dovish than expected, with the currency moving lower following the statement, even though the central bank was warning about inflation.

The decision came on the same day ECB President Christine Lagarde announced her resignation, effective 7 February 2027, a separate development covered in World News Today's earlier report, leaving markets to weigh both the policy outlook and the race to succeed her. Lagarde revealed that a rate hike had been actively discussed at the meeting before the Governing Council unanimously opted to wait for additional data. She said the next six weeks would bring fresh inflation readings, new staff projections, GDP data, wage indicators and labour market reports, and she listed what would arrive before the December decision: new staff projections, two inflation releases and two unemployment releases.

The ECB has already raised rates twice since June, with 25-basis-point increases in June and September, as policymakers seek to prevent an oil-price shock linked to the Iran conflict from feeding into other prices and wages, and money markets are pricing roughly three further rate increases, which would take the deposit rate to 3.25%. Futures on the day priced a better than 80% chance of a 25 basis point hike in December and about 75 basis points of tightening over the next 12 months. A market summary noted that markets had priced only a 2% chance of a 25 basis point move at this meeting, but an 83% chance at the following meeting on December 17.

The ECB is the central bank for the 20 countries that use the euro, and its main job is to keep inflation at 2% over the medium term. Its Governing Council, which includes the governors of the national central banks of the euro area and the members of the ECB's Executive Board, meets every six weeks to set interest rates. The deposit facility rate, the rate the ECB pays banks on overnight deposits, is its main policy rate, and it shapes borrowing costs across the euro area, from mortgages in Spain to business loans in Germany.

The reason the ECB is worried is energy. The war in the Middle East has pushed up the price of oil and gas, and those higher energy prices feed through into almost everything else: transport, heating, food production and manufacturing. The ECB's concern is not just the direct effect of higher energy prices on inflation, but the "second-round effects", the risk that higher energy prices lead to higher wage demands and higher prices for other goods and services, embedding inflation in the economy even after energy prices themselves stabilise.

The ECB now faces a more complicated policy trade-off than it did in September. On the one hand, the energy shock has intensified, and the increased magnitude and duration of the shock are likely to push inflation higher while weighing on growth. On the other hand, financing conditions have tightened substantially since September, with long-term government bond yields rising sharply across advanced economies, and that tightening is itself doing some of the ECB's work, by cooling borrowing and spending. The question is whether the two hikes already delivered, and the tightening of financial conditions since September, are enough to lean against the further increase in energy inflation risk, and the ECB's answer, for now, is to wait and see.

The leadership question adds another layer of uncertainty. Lagarde's resignation is the bigger story than the widely expected decision to leave rates on hold, according to analysts, and markets will be watching the succession closely as pressure on French government debt persists, with different candidates having different attitudes to non-standard policy tools such as the transmission protection instrument, the ECB's tool for preventing disorderly rises in borrowing costs in individual euro area countries. The ECB is heading for a significant reshuffle in the first half of 2027, with Isabel Schnabel, Lagarde and Philip Lane, the ECB's de facto chief economist, all set to depart imminently. Analysts do not expect a major pivot in the ECB's thinking or actions next year as a result of the changes, but how the new leadership communicates with the markets will be crucial.

Attention now turns to the Bank of Japan, which concludes its two-day meeting on Friday, and to the December meeting of the ECB itself, where new staff projections will give policymakers a clearer picture of the inflation outlook. The most likely path, according to analysts, is a 25 basis point hike in December, taking the deposit rate to 2.75%, if October and November inflation stay above 3.5% and the new projections lift the energy effect. But if credit contraction and weaker growth come to the fore, the ECB could leave the hike to February, Lagarde's final meeting. Either way, the October pause should be interpreted not as the end of the tightening cycle but as a tactical delay while policymakers assess whether higher energy prices are beginning to generate broader inflation pressures.
