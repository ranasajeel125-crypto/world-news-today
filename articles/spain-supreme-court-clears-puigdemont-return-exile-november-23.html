Spain's Supreme Court Clears the Way for Puigdemont to Return After Nine Years in Exile

politics · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (Politics Desk)

Former Catalan president Carles Puigdemont, who fled to Belgium in October 2017 after the independence referendum, can now return to Spain without fear of arrest.

Spain's Supreme Court has shelved the criminal case against Catalan separatist and former regional leader Carles Puigdemont, removing the last legal hurdle for him to return to Spain after nearly a decade of self-imposed exile. The ruling, handed down on Monday, closes a judicial saga that began in the autumn of 2017 and has shadowed Spanish politics ever since.

Puigdemont fled to Belgium in October 2017 after holding an independence referendum that the judiciary deemed illegal. The failed vote triggered a deep crisis between Madrid and the northeastern region, which has a distinct language and culture, as well as a significant secessionist movement that peaked in support that year. For nine years he has lived outside Spain, beyond the reach of the Spanish courts, while the case against him remained open.

Monday's decision was the final step in a chain of rulings that has unfolded over the past two months. The Constitutional Court ruled last month that the amnesty law for Catalan separatists also applied to embezzlement charges against Puigdemont, and the Supreme Court then lifted the warrant for his arrest. With the criminal case itself now shelved, there is no longer any outstanding legal obstacle to his return.

The Supreme Court's Criminal Chamber definitively closed the case against Puigdemont and two of his former ministers, Toni Comin and Lluis Puig, who had remained prosecuted in absentia for the crime of embezzlement. The decision came two years and five months after the amnesty law came into force in July 2024, and it carries out the mandate the Constitutional Court imposed on the Supreme Court on 6 October, when it concluded that the court should have granted amnesty to the former pro-independence leaders convicted or prosecuted for this crime.

The case the court has now closed was opened on 31 October 2017, just hours after Puigdemont and several members of his government left Spain for Belgium, after learning that the Attorney General's Office was going to file a complaint against them for rebellion. For years afterwards, the Supreme Court pressed Belgian justice, and for a time also German and Italian justice, to hand Puigdemont over to be tried in Spain, as happened in 2019 with 12 other former leaders of the independence push. The document formalising the definitive closure is signed by the court that tried those leaders, of whose seven members only three remain active today: Andres Martinez Arrieta, Manuel Marchena and Antonio del Moral.

The legal path to this point was long and contested. In 2024, the socialist government introduced a controversial amnesty for dozens of Catalan politicians and activists involved in the secession drive, but the Supreme Court said Puigdemont and several of his colleagues should be excluded, arguing that this was because of the charges against them of alleged financial crimes around staging the referendum. The Constitutional Court overruled that interpretation, and the Supreme Court then rejected an appeal by the far-right Vox party against lifting the national arrest warrants for Puigdemont, Comin and Lluis Puig, paving the way for their return. Spain has also withdrawn its request for Puigdemont's extradition.

Puigdemont's own reaction to the ruling was characteristically combative. Following the decision, he posted on X a poem by Catalan author Jaume Pons Alorda containing foul language and directed at his “dearest enemies”. The post made clear that, whatever the courts have decided, he does not see his exile as something he owes anyone an apology for.

The amnesty that made Monday's ruling possible was itself a political bargain. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez's government pushed it through under a 2023 agreement between his Socialist Party and two Catalan parties, including Puigdemont's Junts, in exchange for their support for Sanchez to be re-elected as premier. The move unleashed intense social and political opposition from the government's critics at the time, and it has remained one of the most divisive decisions of Sanchez's time in office. In a recent interview with newspaper El Mundo, Puigdemont said his party had taken advantage of what he described as Sanchez's ambition in order to “defeat” Spain.

For all that, Puigdemont is in no hurry to use the freedom the courts have now given him. He said he had no plans to return to Catalonia during the campaign ahead of the general election on 29 November, but was considering coming back in December, on a date yet to be determined. His party, Junts, said he will hold a press conference in Brussels on Wednesday, where he is expected to set out his position in person for the first time since the final ruling.

The timing is striking. Spain is preparing for a snap general election, and Puigdemont's return — or his continued absence — will be read in Madrid as a statement about the amnesty and about the price Sanchez paid to stay in power. A Junts official has said Puigdemont is unlikely to take part in the campaign itself, but his presence in the country, or his decision to stay away until after the vote, will be watched closely by both his supporters and his opponents.

The story of the last nine years is, in outline, a familiar one in modern Spain. In October 2017, the Catalan government held a referendum on independence that the Spanish courts had declared illegal, and the regional parliament then declared independence. Madrid responded by imposing direct rule on Catalonia and calling fresh regional elections. The leaders of the independence push were prosecuted: those who stayed in Spain were tried in 2019, while Puigdemont and a handful of colleagues left the country and fought extradition from abroad, first in Belgium and later in Germany and Italy as well.

The political landscape shifted decisively in 2023, when a general election left Sanchez needing the support of Catalan pro-independence parties to form a government. The price of that support was the amnesty law, passed in 2024, which pardoned those involved in the independence push. The Supreme Court's resistance to applying the amnesty to Puigdemont himself, on the grounds of the embezzlement charges, kept the question open for another two years, until the Constitutional Court settled it in October and the Supreme Court finally complied on Monday.

What Monday's ruling settles is narrow — the criminal case is closed, the warrants are gone, and Puigdemont can come home without fear of arrest. What it does not settle is the politics around it. The amnesty remains deeply unpopular with a large part of the Spanish public, and the opposition has argued from the start that it was a political deal dressed up as reconciliation. Puigdemont's own words, that his party used Sanchez's ambition to “defeat” Spain, will be quoted back by his critics as proof of that argument. His supporters, by contrast, will present the ruling as the end of what they regard as the judicial persecution of a democratically elected leader.

What happens next depends first on Puigdemont himself. His press conference in Brussels on Wednesday will be the first indication of how he intends to use his freedom — whether he sets a date for December, as he has suggested, or keeps his plans vague until after the election. Then comes the election on 29 November, which will decide whether Sanchez remains in office and whether the political arrangement that produced the amnesty survives. Either way, the return of the man at the centre of Spain's deepest constitutional crisis in decades, after nine years away, is a moment that will shape the campaign and its aftermath.

Why it matters: the definitive closure of the case against Puigdemont draws a line under the legal consequences of the 2017 independence bid, the event that brought Spain to the edge of a constitutional breakdown. It vindicates, in legal terms, the amnesty bargain that kept Pedro Sanchez in power, and it removes the last formal barrier between Puigdemont and the country he left nine years ago. But the political argument is far from over: the amnesty was always a transaction, not a consensus, and Puigdemont's return — in December, on his own timetable, and on his own terms — will test whether Spain has genuinely moved past 2017, or whether the deepest divisions of that year are simply about to be reopened in a new form.
