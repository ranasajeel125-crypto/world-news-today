Cyclone Akvan Bears Down on India's East Coast as Odisha Evacuates Tens of Thousands

climate · 20 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Cyclone Akvan is the third severe cyclonic storm to threaten India's east coast in less than a month, and officials say the pattern echoes the Bay of Bengal's stormy autumn of 1987.

India's eastern state of Odisha evacuated more than 47,000 people by Thursday as Cyclone Akvan bore down on the coast, the third severe cyclonic storm to threaten the region in less than a month, Reuters reported.

Weather officials said Akvan was likely to cross the coastal states of Odisha and southern neighbouring Andhra Pradesh by noon on Friday, with heavy rain already falling on Thursday. Authorities ordered the closure of all schools and kindergarten centres in some districts, and Indian media reported on Friday morning that the evacuation total had risen above 120,000, with more than 300 rescue teams deployed along the coast.

Indian media reported that landfall was expected on the Odisha coast between Puri and Gopalpur on Friday afternoon. Rail services were suspended across the coastal districts as a precaution, and emergency teams were put on standby along the low-lying shoreline, where a storm surge could push seawater into villages.

In Andhra Pradesh, no evacuations had been ordered by Thursday, state disaster management official Prakhar Jain told Reuters, adding that the storm had reduced in intensity as it approached the coast. Officials there were watching the storm's track closely, ready to move coastal residents if the forecast changed.

Akvan is the third storm in a rapid succession that has tested the region's disaster defences. Andhra Pradesh evacuated thousands of coastal dwellers earlier this month ahead of severe cyclonic storm Murasu, while Cyclone Arnab in late October killed at least six people.

The pattern has echoes in history. Mrutyunjay Mohapatra, India's top government weather expert, told Reuters that the pattern of three cyclones within a month in 2026 is mostly similar to that of 1987, when three cyclonic storms struck the Bay of Bengal in quick succession after the monsoon.

The Bay of Bengal is one of the most cyclone-prone stretches of water in the world, and the weeks after the summer monsoon are its most dangerous season. Warm sea temperatures in October and November feed storms that form over the bay and drive them towards the densely populated coasts of eastern India and Bangladesh, where millions of people live in low-lying delta country.

Odisha has spent a generation building one of the world's most practised cyclone defences. The state was devastated by a super cyclone in 1999 that killed around 10,000 people, and since then it has built a network of cyclone shelters, early-warning systems and community evacuation drills. Mass evacuations ahead of major storms have become routine, and the state is now held up internationally as a model for how to move people out of harm's way before a storm arrives.

That machinery is what Akvan is now testing for the third time in a month. Repeated evacuations are exhausting for the families who must leave their homes and for the officials who must organise the moves, and each storm in the sequence has left less time for shelters to be restocked and for damaged embankments and power lines to be repaired.

For coastal communities, the immediate dangers are the storm surge that can push seawater into villages, the heavy rain that can flood farmland and the winds that can tear roofs from houses and bring down power lines. The full scale of the damage will only become clear once the storm has passed and assessment teams can reach the worst-hit areas. Those teams usually have to wait for the winds to drop before they can travel into the worst-hit districts, so a full picture of the damage can take a day or two to emerge.

What happens next: once Akvan makes landfall on Friday it is expected to weaken as it moves inland, and the focus will shift to relief -- restoring power and water, clearing blocked roads and checking on the families who were moved to shelters. Officials will also be watching whether a fourth storm follows, and whether the 1987 parallel Mohapatra pointed to holds through the rest of the season.

Why it matters: a single cyclone is a test of any disaster system; three in a month is a test of endurance. Odisha's evacuations have saved countless lives over the past two decades, but the clustering of storms in 2026 is a reminder that the Bay of Bengal's cyclone season can deliver its threats in rapid succession -- and that the margin between a well-managed evacuation and a disaster can be measured in hours.
