Israel to Close West Bank Crossings Ahead of Tuesday's Election

politics · 25 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe & Middle East Correspondent)

Israel votes on Tuesday 27 October, and ahead of the ballot the army is closing West Bank crossings and restricting the movement of Palestinian workers, according to BBC News reporting and an announcement from the Israel Defense Forces.

The military said that all land crossings between Israel and the occupied West Bank would be closed on election day. In a statement posted on Telegram, the army said the political leadership had approved a ban on the entry and exit of Palestinian workers throughout the West Bank and the Jordan Valley, including industrial zones, for the whole of election day. From Sunday, Palestinians would also not be permitted to enter Israeli communities in the West Bank and the Jordan Valley for work, the army said, using the terms Judea and Samaria for the territory.

The announcement came as Israel prepared for its first national election since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023, which killed around 1,200 people in southern Israel and set off the war in Gaza. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Israel's longest-serving prime minister, is facing a tight race for a new term, with his security-first reputation badly damaged by the failure to prevent the 2023 attack. Several prominent rivals are seeking to end his long hold on power, and the result will set the direction of Israeli policy for the years ahead.

Election day is a national holiday in Israel, and most polling stations are open from 7am to 10pm. Voters are choosing the 120 members of the Knesset, Israel's parliament, under a system of proportional representation in which parties win seats in line with their share of the national vote. Because no single party usually wins an outright majority, the days and weeks after polling day are normally spent in coalition negotiations, with the president asking the candidate most likely to be able to form a government to try to do so.

The roughly three million adult Palestinians in Gaza, the occupied West Bank and occupied East Jerusalem have no vote in the government that controls their borders, movement and legal status, a point noted in Al Jazeera's live coverage of the election. For them, the closure is not an abstract security measure but a direct interruption to daily life: tens of thousands of Palestinians normally cross into Israel and Israeli settlements for work each day, and a closure means lost wages for workers and lost labour for the employers, farms, building sites and factories that depend on them.

Closures around Israeli elections are a long-standing security practice, and the army presents them as a standard precaution during national votes. But they carry a heavy economic cost, and this closure comes at a particularly tense moment. The army said the restrictions followed an attack in which Palestinian gunmen killed two Israeli soldiers in the territory, and it pledged a harder line on militancy. Over Friday and Saturday, the army said it operated in 40 areas throughout the territory, with local reporting that soldiers announced evacuations over loudspeakers late at night.

The announcement also came a day after Israeli forces pushed into the occupied West Bank city of Hebron and displaced around 2,000 residents, according to the AFP reporting carried by several outlets. Hebron, the largest city in the West Bank, has long been a flashpoint, with a small Israeli settler community living in the heart of a Palestinian city under heavy military protection, and movement restrictions there are among the strictest anywhere in the territory.

The West Bank has seen an escalating crackdown in recent years, with frequent military raids, settler violence and a sharp rise in the number of Palestinians detained. The election campaign has largely been fought over security, the cost of living and the aftermath of the Gaza war, while the situation in Gaza itself has remained largely absent from the campaign, even as an Israeli strike on a university in Gaza City killed three people, according to staff and a local hospital, with the civil defence agency reporting four more deaths elsewhere in the territory.

For Palestinian workers, the practical effect of the closure is straightforward. Those with permits to work in Israel or in settlements will be unable to reach their jobs on election day, and the ban on entering Israeli communities for work from Sunday onwards extends the disruption. Workers who are paid by the day lose income outright, while those on monthly contracts may still find their pay docked or their shifts rearranged. Employers in construction, agriculture and services, sectors that rely heavily on Palestinian labour, face a day without a large part of their workforce.

The crossings are expected to reopen and the work ban to be lifted after the election, according to Israeli media reports. But the episode underlines how closely the lives of Palestinians in the West Bank are tied to decisions made in Jerusalem, in an election in which they cannot vote. The closures also underline the central place that security has taken in this campaign, with Netanyahu and his rivals competing over who can present the tougher response to attacks and the more credible plan for keeping Israelis safe.

What happens after Tuesday matters as much as the vote itself. If no party or bloc emerges with a clear path to a majority, Israel could face weeks of coalition talks, and in the past such stalemates have led to repeated elections. The country has held five elections in less than four years before, between 2019 and 2022, and politicians and voters alike are wary of another cycle of instability. The result will also be watched closely abroad, in Washington, in European capitals and across the Middle East, where governments are weighing what a new Israeli government would mean for the Gaza war, for the West Bank and for the wider region.

For now, the immediate reality for Palestinians in the West Bank is a closed crossing and a day without work. For Israelis, it is a polling day that will decide who leads the country through the aftermath of the worst attack in its history and a war that has reshaped the region. The closures are a reminder that in this election, as in so many before it, security and the occupation are never far from the ballot box.
