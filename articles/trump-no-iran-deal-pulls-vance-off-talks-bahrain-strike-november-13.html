Trump Says US Doesn't Need an Iran Deal, Pulls Vance Off Talks as Iran Targets US Base in Bahrain

world · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Iran said its Revolutionary Guard targeted a US early-warning radar in Bahrain early on Friday; US Central Command said two Iranian drones were shot down and there were no injuries or damage.

President Donald Trump said on Thursday that the United States no longer needs a deal with Iran and ordered Vice President JD Vance — who had been leading the negotiations with Tehran — to turn his attention to domestic issues instead. A day later, Iran launched drones toward a US military installation in Bahrain in its first strike on an American facility in a Gulf state in more than two months, CNN reported on Friday, leaving the eight-month-old war in a state that neither side says it is willing to accept indefinitely.

“We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed!” Trump posted on Truth Social on 12 November. “We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!” He said he had asked Vance to focus his “complete and total attention” on rooting out corruption and crime in Democratic-run cities and states, USA Today reported.

The move was a sudden reversal. Vance had been the administration’s top Iran negotiator for months, and the Wall Street Journal reported the day before Trump’s post that the vice president was considering face-to-face negotiations with Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran’s top security body, after opening a communications channel with him. US officials were at the early stages of discussing in-person meetings aimed at ending the conflict, and Trump had told top aides in recent days that he was open to Vance restarting talks and had empowered him to negotiate, the Journal reported.

Vance had reason to believe a deal was within reach. He helped orchestrate a tentative peace deal with Iran in June that briefly paused the war and allowed shipping through the Strait of Hormuz to resume, only for it to fall apart within weeks after Iran failed to follow through on several of its commitments, USA Today reported. Vance had said the June memorandum of understanding brought the two sides “very close” to a permanent deal. He is also widely seen as the most sceptical figure in the administration about the war, and his removal from the talks takes the administration’s most prominent voice for a negotiated settlement out of the room.

The Bahrain attack came on Friday morning. Iran said its Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps aerospace forces had targeted the early-warning radar of the US military in Bahrain in retaliation for what it described as an American attack on a communication tower in Sirik, on the Iranian coast. “A few hours ago, the IRGC’s aerospace forces, in response to the enemy’s aerial aggression against a communication tower in Sirik, targeted and destroyed the early warning radar of the aggressor US military in Bahrain,” the Guard said, according to the semi-official Tasnim news agency, adding that “this is a serious warning” to Washington.

US Central Command gave a different account. It said later on Friday that American forces shot down two Iranian drones launched toward Bahrain, reporting no injuries or damage, and said the claim that Iran had attacked a radar system was false. The strike was nevertheless a break in the pattern of the last two months: Tehran had largely refrained from attacking American military installations in Gulf states since early September, focusing instead on disrupting shipping through the waterway, while the US has continued striking Iranian targets around the Strait of Hormuz. The US last attacked Iran a week ago to destroy an anti-aircraft system on Qeshm Island, also in the strait, according to The Times.

The conflict began in February with US airstrikes, USA Today reported, and the war has upended the global supply of oil from the Middle East and resulted in the deaths of 23 US service members. Trump has long said his main goal is to ensure Iran does not have nuclear capability, and he warned that the US military is “locked and loaded” and keeping an eye on Pickaxe Mountain, a facility linked to Iran’s nuclear programme, for signs of activity, the Associated Press reported.

Trump has in recent days said repeatedly that neither a new offensive nor a deal is needed. On 9 November he told reporters it “won’t be necessary” to escalate the months-long war, saying the ongoing US blockade on Iranian ports and economic sanctions are working and that he intends to keep those policies in place. “Iran, militarily, is effectively over. We’re just keeping it the status quo,” he said. In a “60 Minutes” interview taped on 6 November, he said the US military can continue escorting ships through the Strait of Hormuz and argued that unspecified countries in the region would help cover the costs.

The politics at home help explain the shift. In the midterm elections on 3 November, Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in a sweeping repudiation of Trump’s second term, with high petrol prices caused by the war a central issue. In a post-midterm analysis on X on 4 November, Vance blamed the war above all for the Republicans’ losses. “The reason we lost is the war,” he wrote. “If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so. Argue it was a worthwhile cost to pay. Agree or disagree, that’s at least honest.” Trump has acknowledged that the war and the global economic disruption it caused were partly to blame for the Republican losses, the Associated Press reported, while maintaining that the war itself was the right thing to do.

Iran, for its part, has warned that it will not accept a stalemate. President Masoud Pezeshkian said on Monday that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had instructed his government “to end the state of neither war nor peace,” telling him to “finish” the war with the US. “We can negotiate with dignity, and can finish it (the war) with dignity,” Pezeshkian said. Tehran has warned that any new escalation would prompt it to dramatically expand its attacks, potentially targeting US interests as far away as Europe, as it comes under intense economic pressure from punishing US sanctions and a naval blockade.

The pressure on shipping through the strait, the world’s most important oil chokepoint, has reached a record high. October recorded 37 vessel attacks, Lloyd’s List reported on Friday, the highest monthly total of the conflict, and at least 14 more were confirmed in November. Despite the attacks, oil has continued to flow through the waterway, with Middle East exports recovering faster than global oil demand. The International Energy Agency said on Friday that the oil market is “showing tentative signs of rebalancing,” and that global oil supplies are surpassing demand for the first time since the outbreak of the Iran war.

Pressure for a return to war is also coming from Israel. The director of the Israeli spy agency Mossad, Roman Gofman, was in Washington on Friday, on a visit that comes as Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has recently sought to push the Trump administration into a return to war with Iran, CNN reported. Two Israeli sources say Netanyahu has even considered a unilateral Israeli strike on Iran, though he would still need approval from Trump to carry it out. Gofman was expected to meet CIA Director John Ratcliffe; his visit was first reported by Axios. Netanyahu has lost his governing majority, and his main rival, Gadi Eisenkot, demanded and received a security briefing from the prime minister.

What happens next is now genuinely uncertain. Trump has held off on a renewed offensive and claimed his economic pressure campaign has been sufficient in weakening Tehran, but he also told a Veterans Day audience on Wednesday: “We won. Now the question is when we want to end. We must have a total victory, not a 93% victory. It’s going to be total.” In recent days he was considering three options, the Wall Street Journal reported: resuming airstrikes, maintaining the blockade on Iranian trade, or making a deal. He chose to tighten the blockade while allowing Vance to pursue an agreement — and has now closed that channel too, leaving the war in the limbo that both capitals say they want to end, with no talks scheduled and no deal on the table.

Why it matters: the decision removes the one senior American official who had both Tehran’s ear and a public record of blaming the war for his party’s election defeat, and it does so at the moment Iran has resumed striking US forces directly. With the Strait of Hormuz recording its worst month of shipping attacks, a naval blockade squeezing Iran’s economy, and Israel pressing for a return to war, the absence of any diplomatic process raises the risk that the next incident — a drone over Bahrain, a strike on Qeshm Island, an attack on a tanker — escalates before either side has a route back to the table. For the region, for oil markets, and for the 23 American families who have already lost someone in this war, the question is no longer whether a deal is needed, but what, if anything, takes its place.
