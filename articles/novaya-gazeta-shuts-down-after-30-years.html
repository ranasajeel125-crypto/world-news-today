Novaya Gazeta to Shut Down After 30 Years, Citing Relentless Pressure From Russian Authorities

world · 30 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Novaya Gazeta, which has existed for more than three decades, said it would turn off its lights and shut down its servers on 9 November.

world · 30 Oct 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Independent Russian newspaper Novaya Gazeta said on Friday that it was shutting down after what it described as a relentless campaign by the authorities which saw state prosecutors pursue some of its senior journalists and left it without financial support and an office.

The newspaper, which has often been highly critical of the authorities, has existed for more than three decades and has faced serious problems in the past. Six of its journalists, including investigative journalist Anna Politkovskaya, have been murdered, in varying circumstances. The six were Igor Domnikov, killed in 2000, Yuri Shchekochikhin, who died in 2003, Anna Politkovskaya, murdered in 2006, Stanislav Markelov and Anastasia Baburova, killed in 2009, and Natalia Estemirova, also killed in 2009. Politkovskaya exposed systemic human rights abuses, widespread corruption and war crimes committed by Russian forces in Chechnya, and in October the newspaper commemorated the twentieth anniversary of her murder.

But like other media outlets not run by the state, Novaya Gazeta has come under increasingly intense pressure from the authorities after Russia launched its full-scale war in Ukraine in 2022 and sweeping new censorship rules were introduced which mandated long jail terms for discrediting the Russian army or disseminating what the authorities deem to be fake news. The newspaper was the last major independent newsroom openly critical of the Kremlin still operating within Russia, and it was in the crosshairs of the authorities ever since Vladimir Putin came to power in 2000.

"On November 9th, we will turn off the lights in the home where we have lived for 30 years, shut down our servers, and leave. The newspaper no longer exists. The Russian authorities and the Russian security services have forced our editorial team to cease its operations," Novaya Gazeta said in a statement. "More than 30 years of our life shared with our country and its citizens will end, leaving us homeless, with no means of existence and under the constant threat of repression. We carried on as long as we could, but we were evicted from our home and our donations platform that allowed us to receive financial support from our readers has been shut down. We have been stripped of all our resources."

The newspaper was founded in April 1993, partly funded using money provided by Mikhail Gorbachev from his Nobel Peace Prize, and it built a reputation for courageous and ambitious reporting, rooting out corruption and abuses of power and extensively covering the war in Chechnya in the late 1990s and early 2000s. It became the go-to source of information for Russians who wanted more than the official narrative, and it symbolised the power of the press to hold officials accountable. Its long-time former editor-in-chief, Dmitry Muratov, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2021 for his defence of freedom of expression.

The final months were a sequence of escalating blows. On April 9, 2026, Russian police raided the newspaper's headquarters in a 13-hour search and seizure of evidence. Columnist Oleg Roldugin was subsequently charged with illegally accessing computer information and placed in pre-trial detention, and he faces up to six years in prison if convicted. In September, Novaya Gazeta and people associated with the outlet were labelled foreign agents, including Muratov, and more than 100 people were listed as affiliated individuals. On October 15, investigators searched the apartment of editor-in-chief Sergei Sokolov and questioned him before releasing him. On October 28, the authorities announced the closure of the newspaper's headquarters, where editorial staff had operated for the past 29 years, at Potapovsky Lane in central Moscow. The landlord gave staff only a month to vacate the office, and around the same time their bank and payment provider stopped processing reader donations without warning.

The pressure had been building for years. In March 2022, during the invasion of Ukraine, the newspaper suspended print publication inside Russia because of increased government censorship. In September 2022, its Russian media licence was revoked, and its website was blocked, forcing readers to use virtual private networks to reach it. Its printing presses were seized. Reduced to publishing online, with only occasional print runs, the newspaper survived for nearly five years of war through increasingly inventive means, reporting factually but cautiously, strictly adhering to ever more repressive laws, and avoiding reporting on frontline developments, since any article about the military could lead to prosecution.

There was no immediate comment from the authorities. But the Kremlin, which accuses foreign intelligence services of trying to stir up discontent inside Russian society, has said in the past that some censorship is necessary at a time when Russia is locked in what it describes as an existential struggle with the West over Ukraine. The closure was described by the Committee to Protect Journalists as a dark day for independent media everywhere, and by PEN International as a crushing blow for freedom of expression. Reporters Without Borders said Novaya Gazeta would remain a symbol of free journalism and a model for media around the world, even after its closure. Russia ranks 172nd out of 180 countries in the Reporters Without Borders World Press Freedom Index, and as of April 2026 it held 48 journalists in prison.

On Monday, November 9, the newspaper closed its doors for good, after 33 years in existence. In its final days, staff packed up more than three decades of newsroom history into moving boxes: back issues, books from the staff library, posters and photographs taken down from the walls. Editor-in-chief Sergey Sokolov held the final editorial meeting at the Moscow office on Monday. Muratov, for whom the newspaper was his entire life, told colleagues that they had not died, they had been killed, along with the Constitution. Novaya Gazeta Europe, the edition published outside Russia, is expected to continue publishing, but inside Russia, the newspaper that had survived the murder of its journalists, the loss of its licence and the blocking of its website is gone.
