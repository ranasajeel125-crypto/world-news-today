Trump Kicks Off Asia Trip With Quad Talks in Manila Ahead of China Visit

world · 17 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Quad meeting — the United States, Japan, Australia and India — will be Trump's first and takes place two years after the last gathering of the group.

US President Donald Trump flies into the Philippines on Tuesday at the tail end of a Southeast Asian summit, where he could face protests ahead of the first meeting in two years of the leaders of the Quad group of Indo-Pacific countries, Reuters reported.

Trump's Asia trip, which also includes the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum in Shenzhen and a much-anticipated trilateral meeting with his Chinese and Russian counterparts, comes after his Republican Party's defeat in the midterm elections and spy allegations that could complicate a fragile trade-war truce between the United States and China.

There could be protests in Manila against Trump's visit, after days of demonstrations that have included the burning of effigies of the president and calls for an end to the Middle East war and the scrapping of the US-led Pax Silica initiative.

The meeting of the Quad — the United States, Japan, Australia and India — will be Trump's first and takes place two years after the last gathering of the group, which his predecessor Joe Biden regarded as a signature foreign policy initiative. It comes just ahead of the APEC gathering hosted by Chinese President Xi Jinping, who has previously objected to the Quad as an effort to encircle Beijing. Quad members have said the grouping is not directed against any one country.

At the summit of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations on Monday, Chinese Premier Li Qiang hailed what he called “truly extraordinary” economic achievements of China and ASEAN in the face of global trade restrictions, expressing Beijing's commitment to free trade and the need to “stand united, ward off interference, and work together”. China is ASEAN's biggest trading partner and a key source of investment, but the biggest economies of the 11-member bloc — which has a combined GDP of more than $3.9 trillion — want to deepen ties with the United States despite protracted trade and diplomatic uncertainty under the Trump administration.

Former US Deputy Secretary of State Kurt Campbell credited Washington's top diplomat, Marco Rubio, with the timing and reconvening of the Quad, which he said had lost ambition under Trump. “Part of what he (Rubio) is thinking is that this is probably the best possible signal if you're going to go into a trilateral meeting,” said Campbell, co-founder of The Asia Group, referring to Thursday's US-China-Russia talks. “The linchpin of everything that we do in Asia is the US-Japan relationship, and a perception that this is not at the top of the president's mind would concern me.”

White House spokesperson Olivia Wales said on Friday that under Trump's leadership, the Quad had demonstrated it is “a partnership of action, tackling the region's toughest challenges and driving opportunities to ensure a free and open Indo-Pacific”.

A Japanese foreign ministry official said last week it was expected that Quad leaders would try to show unity, with maritime security, export restrictions, critical-minerals supply-chain diversification and AI among potential discussion points.

Before Trump's late afternoon arrival, ASEAN will hold meetings on Tuesday with leaders of Japan, South Korea, China and India, ahead of the annual East Asia Summit. Trump will skip the summit but will join a US-ASEAN “Special Summit” and will meet Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr on Wednesday. The Philippines is a US defence ally.

Trump is also expected to hold talks with Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul on Tuesday, Thai media quoted the commerce minister as saying, with a joint statement to be issued on negotiations on a reciprocal trade agreement. The US is Thailand's largest export market, accounting for 24% of its total shipments this year. The US designated Thailand as a major non-NATO ally in 2003.

The Manila stop is the opening act of a week in which Trump will sit down with the two leaders Washington has spent the year circling. The trilateral meeting on Thursday in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, brings together Trump, Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin — and the Kremlin moved on Monday to play down the historical weight being attached to it. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov dismissed as “games of political scientists” any suggestion that the meeting was comparable to the 1945 Yalta conference, which tried to set out the future of post-war Europe. Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning told reporters in Beijing that it would be the first face-to-face meeting among the heads of state of China, Russia and the United States, and Peskov said part of the meeting would be open to the media.

The domestic backdrop to the trip is unusually difficult for a president travelling abroad. The Democrats won control of both the US House of Representatives and the Senate in the midterm elections, a result this site reported last week, leaving Trump to conduct foreign policy with a Congress now in opposition hands. At the same time, the sudden resignation of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang last week — amid reports of an affair with a woman who worked at a Singapore-based cloud company that partners with Nvidia — has kept spy allegations in the headlines, and the administration has just moved to further restrict China's access to advanced AI chips, a step that has already rattled Asian markets.

That is why the order of the week matters. Campbell's point, echoed by the Japanese official's account of the agenda, is that the Quad meeting is meant to set the signal before Trump sits down with Xi and Putin: a public display of unity with Japan, Australia and India on maritime security, export controls and critical minerals, the very issues at the centre of the trade and technology contest with China. The White House, for its part, is presenting the Quad as a working partnership rather than a talking shop, and the choice of Manila — a treaty ally on the front line of the South China Sea disputes — underlines the point.

For ASEAN, the week is a balancing act it has performed for years. The bloc's biggest economies rely on China as their largest trading partner while also wanting deeper ties with the United States, and Li Qiang's speech on Monday was a reminder that Beijing intends to compete for that loyalty with promises of free trade and resistance to what he called interference. Trump's decision to skip the East Asia Summit while joining a separate US-ASEAN meeting, and to hold a bilateral with Marcos on Wednesday, shows Washington making its own case — one built on defence ties and trade deals, including the reciprocal agreement under negotiation with Thailand, the United States' largest export market in the region.

Why it matters: this is the first time in two years that the leaders of the Quad have met, and it comes just two days before Trump sits down with Xi and Putin in Shenzhen. The Manila meeting is the warm-up and the message at the same time — a show that the United States still has its Indo-Pacific partners beside it before it negotiates with its two biggest rivals. Whether the week ends with a calmer trade truce, a harder line on chips and minerals, or simply a photograph of three leaders in one room will shape the rest of the year in Asia, and the Quad's first meeting in two years is where that story begins.
