Trump and NATO Chief Urge Russia Not to Widen the Ukraine War After Mar-a-Lago Talks

world · 25 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Trump and Rutte met at Mar-a-Lago in Florida, days after Trump's meetings with Putin and Xi in Asia. The warning comes as Russian weapons have already landed on NATO soil.

Russia should avoid actions that expand its war in Ukraine, US President Donald Trump and NATO chief Mark Rutte said in a joint statement on Wednesday, after a meeting in Florida where they also discussed the war on Iran.

The two leaders met at Mar-a-Lago, Trump's estate in Palm Beach, and described the meeting in the joint statement released by the White House as “outstanding” and “very impactful”. The war in Ukraine and the US-Israeli war on Iran were the primary subjects of the talks, according to the statement, which did not say what was discussed on Iran.

“We also discussed the importance of Russia avoiding any actions that intentionally, or unintentionally, expand the war, and the importance of obtaining a peaceful resolution to the war as rapidly as possible,” the leaders said in the statement.

The wording was deliberately broad. By warning against actions that expand the war “intentionally, or unintentionally”, Trump and Rutte covered both a deliberate Russian escalation and the kind of accident that has already happened during the war — Russian weapons landing on the territory of a NATO member state.

The two leaders also talked about boosting defence spending and investment, according to the statement. They discussed “the importance of continuing to expand defense spending and investment in our industrial base, both in the United States and across NATO”, a theme Trump has returned to throughout his second term as he presses allies to spend more on their own defence.

The meeting came days after Trump met Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping last week in Asia, referring to each as his “friend”. On Thursday 19 November, the three leaders held the first-ever trilateral meeting of the leaders of the world's three biggest nuclear powers, spending 90 minutes together at Kylin Villa, a secluded government retreat in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit.

That meeting ended with a four-point joint declaration, described by the leaders as a “peace declaration”, covering reform of the United Nations, increased production and supply of refined petroleum products, national consent and respect for the immunity of state officials, and the principle that a nuclear war “cannot be won and must never be fought”. It named no cap on nuclear arsenals and set no timetable — and it did not mention Ukraine at all, announcing no agreement on Ukraine or Iran despite the wars dominating the day's separate bilateral meetings.

Trump gave his first public assessment of the Putin talks the next day, Friday 20 November, at a White House news conference after returning from Asia. He said he and Putin had held “very good talks” on Ukraine. “We're having very good talks. I think there's a real good chance that we can get it done,” he said, adding that Putin had “clearly expressed his desire to see the war between Russia and Ukraine end by diplomatic means”.

Trump was blunt about the obstacle he sees between the two sides. “The problem is President (Volodymyr) Zelenskiy and President Putin have tremendous hatred, and that's never easy,” he said, referring to the Ukrainian president and his Russian counterpart.

The diplomacy has drawn a backlash at home and abroad. Trump's meeting with Putin triggered criticism from domestic Democratic opponents and European allies, who have pushed to be included in the US-backed talks to end the war in Ukraine. Zelenskiy, who has himself been criticised by Trump, has said Europe had to be included in the peace talks — a demand that goes to the heart of the argument over who gets to shape any settlement.

The warning about widening the war also lands against a recent, concrete example of spillover. On Friday 20 November, three Russian objects — a Kh-101 cruise missile, a second missile and what was most likely a jet-powered drone — landed in south-eastern Poland during one of the largest Russian attacks on Ukraine of the war. Nobody was hurt and no damage to civilian property was reported, but the objects came down on the soil of a NATO member state at the very moment European allies were gathering in Poland for military exercises with the “Coalition of the Willing”, the group of Ukraine's allies planning a possible postwar security force.

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said there was no doubt the objects were Russian. “It seems we are dealing with an intentional provocation,” he said, adding that Rutte would visit Warsaw the following day. The timing of the Mar-a-Lago warning, five days later, suggests the episode is fresh in the minds of both men — and that “unintentionally” in the joint statement is doing real work, covering the miscalculation that could turn a strike on Ukraine into a direct clash with NATO.

The gap between summit language and battlefield reality was on display the same week. Hours after the three leaders issued their peace declaration in Shenzhen, Russia launched one of the largest missile and drone attacks of the war on Ukraine, pummelling electricity facilities and killing eight people. Russian ballistic missiles hit three bridges over the Dnipro River in Kyiv itself, killing three people and injuring at least 15, in the biggest attack on the crossings linking the capital's two banks since the start of the war. France called for a meeting of the UN Security Council, and the Dutch government summoned the Russian ambassador after its embassy in Kyiv was slightly damaged.

Rutte's role in the Mar-a-Lago meeting reflects the balancing act he has performed since taking over as NATO secretary general. He has repeatedly tried to soothe tension within the alliance and keep Trump onside, and has said it was normal for major nuclear powers to meet — a defence of Trump's direct engagement with Putin that many European governments have found hard to accept.

Trump, for his part, has often criticised Washington's Western and democratic allies over issues such as NATO spending, trade tariffs and what he calls their inadequate support for the war on Iran. That friction is the backdrop to the Mar-a-Lago talks: the joint statement projects unity on Ukraine, but the allies' demand for a seat at the negotiating table remains unresolved, and the statement said nothing about what the two men discussed on Iran.

Why it matters: the warning is the clearest joint signal yet from Washington and NATO headquarters that the war's borders — geographic and political — are the red line. The test is not the statement itself but what follows it: whether Russian strikes keep spilling into NATO airspace, whether the US-backed talks open up to the European allies who have demanded a place in them, and whether a peace process that began with a declaration in Shenzhen that did not even mention Ukraine can produce a settlement that Kyiv and Europe will accept. Until then, the words from Mar-a-Lago are a marker of intent, not a result.
