United Fidelity Bank Shut in Biggest US Bank Failure in Two Years as First Citizens Buys Its Assets

business · 27 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

United Fidelity Bank, of Evansville, Indiana, was closed by US regulators on Friday in the biggest American bank failure in more than two years. First Citizens Bank will take over its deposits and most of its assets.

US regulators closed Indiana-based United Fidelity Bank on Friday, in the biggest American bank failure in more than two years, handing control of the lender to the Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. and selling most of its business to First Citizens Bank.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency said it had closed United Fidelity Bank, fsb, of Evansville, Indiana, after finding the bank in an “unsafe or unsound condition to transact business” and likely to be unable to pay its obligations or meet its depositors’ demands in the normal course of business. The OCC appointed the FDIC as receiver.

First-Citizens Bank & Trust Company of Raleigh, North Carolina, agreed to assume substantially all of United Fidelity’s deposits and to buy substantially all of its assets, the FDIC said. The deal covers $5.9 billion in assets, $4.5 billion in deposits and $3.1 billion in loans, based on the failed bank’s balances as of 30 September.

Depositors will become customers of First Citizens and will keep all of their funds and their deposit insurance cover, the FDIC said. They can still write cheques, use ATMs and use their debit cards over the weekend, and the bank’s 22 branches — in Indiana, Illinois, Florida, Colorado and the US Virgin Islands — will reopen as First Citizens branches, on Saturday where they normally open on Saturdays and on Monday at the rest.

The failure is the biggest in the United States since Republic Bank of Philadelphia closed in April 2024 with $6 billion in assets, according to FDIC data, and the second-biggest since the 2023 regional banking crisis that brought down Silicon Valley Bank and First Republic.

What alarmed regulators and analysts was the scale of the loss. The FDIC estimated the failure would cost the industry’s deposit insurance fund $1.83 billion — an unusually high 31% of United Fidelity’s $5.9 billion in total assets. First Citizens agreed to take on the bank’s loans only at a steep discount, and it assumed $4.5 billion of deposits against the $5.4 billion the FDIC said the bank held at the end of September.

Neither the OCC nor United Fidelity has said what brought about the failure. The regulator’s statement gave no details of the bank’s loan book or of the losses that pushed it into an unsafe condition, and the FDIC said only that the bank had been closed after the OCC acted.

First Citizens is a familiar buyer of failed banks. It bought the bridge bank set up after Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023, in the largest bank failure since the 2008 financial crisis, and it has now stepped in again to take over a distressed franchise at a discount.

United Fidelity is the seventh US bank failure of 2026, the FDIC having counted six before it, up from two in each of 2024 and 2025. The step-up in number and scale is notable, though analysts caution that a single failure, even a large one, is not on its own evidence of a broad banking crisis.

The Evansville lender was the first bank in its home city to fail since Integra Bank in 2011, when federal agents seized the downtown office on a Friday afternoon. This time the handover was arranged in advance: the FDIC ran a competitive bidding process, and First Citizens’ winning bid included an asset discount of roughly $2.6 billion and no premium for the deposits.

For customers, the practical effect is limited. The FDIC said depositors do not need to change their banking relationship, and the branches will operate under the First Citizens name from Saturday. The FDIC has opened a call centre for customers with questions about the acquisition, open until 8 p.m. Central Time on Friday evening and from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. on Saturday.

Why it matters: a $5.9 billion bank does not fail without a deep hole in its balance sheet, and the $1.83 billion hit to the deposit insurance fund — nearly a third of the bank’s assets — is a measure of how distressed United Fidelity was when regulators stepped in. The failure is contained for now: depositors are protected, a buyer is in place, and the branches reopen within a day. But it is the largest American bank failure in more than two years, and the seventh of 2026, and it will sharpen the questions regulators face about what went wrong in Evansville — and whether other lenders are carrying the same kind of losses.
