UK to Require Economic Benefits from Major Foreign Defence Contracts

Britain will require overseas defence suppliers winning selected military contracts worth more than £50 million ($67 million) to deliver jobs, investment, skills or other economic benefits in the UK, under a new policy announced on Friday, 30 October, Reuters reported. The policy was announced by Chancellor John Healey and Defence Secretary Wes Streeting on a visit to BAE Systems' Scotstoun shipyard in Glasgow, and it forms part of the government's "Back British" approach to procurement set out in the Budget two days earlier.

The government said the policy would apply on a case-by-case basis from April 2027, with a new Offset Authority overseeing agreements and delivery. Contractors could be required to generate economic benefits in Britain worth up to the value of a contract — meaning a £100 million contract could result in up to £100 million of investment, jobs or skills programmes. The standard offset commitment will be the same as the contract's full value, although this would not necessarily mean spending the same sum in cash: benefits can take the form of new British jobs and apprenticeships, opening UK factories, investing in domestic suppliers, transferring technology and innovation know-how, supply chain participation and support for small and medium-sized enterprises. Suppliers will normally have up to ten years to fulfil their commitments, and the Offset Authority will agree, value and monitor delivery through dedicated offset agreements, ensuring consistency across the lifetime of each arrangement.

An offset, sometimes called an industrial participation arrangement, is a reciprocal commitment that a purchasing country requires of a foreign contractor when it buys defence equipment from abroad. In practice, the foreign contractor commits to channel investment, technology, jobs or production into the purchasing country, usually to a specified value. The UK's policy follows similar approaches used by allies including Poland, Norway and the Netherlands, and it marks a clear break from the existing position, under which the UK encouraged foreign defence contractors to invest in Britain on a voluntary basis.

The policy's stated objective is that offsets related to defence and security acquisitions from abroad should contribute to maintaining and developing an innovative, resilient and competitive UK defence industrial base that supports national security and economic growth. The Ministry of Defence wants selected imports of defence capability to deliver additional enduring benefits for the UK, including greater sovereign capability, supply chain resilience, skills, innovation, exports and growth among small and medium-sized enterprises. The £50 million threshold reflects the ministry's aim to cover only large, strategically important programmes, where the benefits are likely to justify the additional administrative burden and potential cost premium, and the ministry can raise or lower the threshold depending on market conditions or its implementation capacity. UK-registered suppliers are not automatically outside the scope: the policy may apply where a significant part of the contract value, production or delivery sits overseas.

The scale of the spending involved is large. More than £35 billion has been spent on defence contracts with overseas suppliers since 2016, representing about 11 per cent of direct Ministry of Defence spending with industry, the government said. Britain sees higher defence spending as a way to strengthen both its military and its industrial base, and ministers argue the new rules will make sure more of that spending supports British jobs and skills rather than flowing entirely overseas. "When a big contract goes overseas, these changes will ensure 100 per cent of the value creates new opportunities for workers and businesses here at home, strengthening our industrial base and supporting growth across Britain," Healey said.

The announcement comes days after the government unveiled an extra £10 billion of economic and military support for Ukraine in the Budget, delivered by Healey in the House of Commons on 28 October. The package, described by the Treasury as the largest UK package ever, is based on loans, export finance guarantees and half a billion pounds in urgent military funding for this winter, including £500 million of additional support for Ukraine's air defences. It sits on top of the existing commitment of £3 billion a year in military support until 2030-31, and takes total UK support for Ukraine to £35 billion, including £16 billion of military support.

The Budget also confirmed that the government's Defence Investment Plan is now fully funded. In June, the government announced £15 billion for the plan between 2026-27 and 2029-30, and the Budget confirmed that the final £4.7 billion will be paid for by reprioritising capital budgets. Defence capital spending is set to rise from £23.0 billion in 2025-26 to £34.5 billion in 2029-30, and the government will set out at next year's spending review how it will meet its NATO commitment to spend 3.5 per cent of GDP on defence by 2035, with a target date of 2030 for 3 per cent. The Budget also launched an Innovative Defence Financing Taskforce to bring private and international finance into defence, and confirmed treaty negotiations on a multilateral defence mechanism with Finland, Poland and the Netherlands to boost defence investment and joint procurement across NATO allies.

The offsets policy was published after a consultation launched by the Ministry of Defence in October 2025, and much of the practical detail is yet to be published in guidance at a later date. Industry lawyers note that questions remain about how benefits will be valued, how the requirements will interact with trade rules, and whether the added obligations will raise the cost of foreign equipment. For the government, though, the message is that defence spending should do double duty: buying the equipment the armed forces need, and building the factories, skills and supply chains that let Britain make more of it at home. With defence spending climbing the political agenda across Europe amid the wars in Ukraine, Iran and Yemen, the policy is likely to shape how the UK buys some of its most expensive military equipment for years to come.
