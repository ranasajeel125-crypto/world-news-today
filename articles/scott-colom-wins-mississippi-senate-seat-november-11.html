Democrat Scott Colom Wins Mississippi Senate Seat in Historic Upset

Democrat Scott Colom has won the race for a United States Senate seat in Mississippi, defeating the sitting Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith in a result few people had predicted and one that took more than a week to settle. The Associated Press called the contest for Colom on Wednesday evening, 11 November, eight days after Election Day, after local officials finished counting the ballots that had kept the outcome uncertain since 3 November. Hyde-Smith, 67, called Colom late on Wednesday afternoon to concede and released a concession speech online shortly afterwards.

With 99% of the vote counted, Colom had 49.2% to Hyde-Smith's 48.7%, a lead of about 4,600 votes, according to the Associated Press, with independent candidate Ty Pinkins taking about 2.1 per cent. The race was the closest Senate contest in the country, and it came down to more than 100,000 mail-in ballots and affidavit, or provisional, ballots that took election officials over a week to process. Every county in the state shifted to the left compared with votes in the 2024 presidential election, according to data from the AP and the New York Times, and Colom flipped at least 18 counties that voted for Trump in 2024, according to the USA TODAY Network, including the traditionally Republican counties of Madison and Forrest. He came within about three points of Hyde-Smith in Harrison County, the largest on the Mississippi Gulf Coast.

Colom, 43, a district attorney for Mississippi's 16th Judicial District and a seventh-generation Mississippian, is the first Democrat to win one of the state's Senate seats since 1982, when John C. Stennis was re-elected, and the first Black politician elected statewide in Mississippi since the Reconstruction era following the American Civil War. He will be the state's first Black senator since Blanche K. Bruce, whose term ended in 1881, and one of seven new Black senators in the next Congress. At his victory party in Jackson on Wednesday, Colom told supporters he had come close to giving up during the long wait for the result.

The result is the eighth Senate seat Democrats have flipped from Republicans in this year's midterm elections, giving them a minimum 55-seat majority going into 2027, with the Alaska race still to be decided. Mississippi was one of two Senate seats that had remained uncalled for more than a week after Election Day. As of 11 November, the only Senate race that remained uncalled was Alaska, where Democrat Mary Peltola was narrowly ahead in the slow-to-count state. Colom will be the Democrats' 55th senator in the party's largest Senate majority since the Obama administration, and in the next Congress no Democratic senator will represent a redder state than Colom.

The Cook Political Report had listed the contest as Likely Republican going into the vote, and President Donald Trump won Mississippi by more than 23 points in 2024. The result was part of a powerful blue wave that propelled Democratic candidates to victory across the country in the midterms, with Democrats retaking the House of Representatives as well as the Senate. Analysts pointed to voter disenchantment with Trump and his party, an unpopular war in Iran and persistent affordability problems as factors that reached even reliably Republican states. Trump travelled to Mississippi eight days before the election, while former President Bill Clinton and former Vice President Kamala Harris campaigned with Colom in the weeks before Election Day.

Colom was born in Columbus, Mississippi, into a family of lawyers. His mother, Dorothy Winston Colom, is a retired Chancery Court judge who was the first Black judge in her district's court, and his father was a Republican who worked for Ronald Reagan in the 1980s. He attended Millsaps College in Mississippi, where he played basketball and majored in English and history, and earned his law degree from the University of Wisconsin in 2009. After law school he worked at the Mississippi Center for Justice, representing people who could not afford a lawyer in civil cases, before joining his father's firm. In 2011 he was named a justice court judge in Lowndes County, at 28 the youngest and first Black judge in the county. His identical twin brother, Andrew, played a major role in his campaign. He is married to Nadia Colom, a real estate agent, and the couple have two daughters.

In 2015, at age 32, Colom defeated a 27-year incumbent, Forrest Allgood, to become district attorney for the 16th Judicial District, which includes the white-majority counties of Clay, Lowndes, Noxubee and Oktibbeha, becoming the first Black prosecutor in a majority-white district in the state. He easily won re-election in 2019 and 2023. His campaign says his office has won 94 per cent of its trials, and as district attorney he established programmes to help first-time offenders avoid prison and provide assistance with substance use issues, as well as counselling services to victims of violent crime. One of his most notable cases was that of Eddie Lee Howard, a Black man who had spent 26 years on death row for murder but was released in 2021 for lack of evidence, after a judge dismissed the murder charge and Colom confirmed prosecutors were dropping it.

Colom's political biography is bound up with his opponent's. In 2022, then-President Joe Biden nominated Colom to serve as a federal judge on the US District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi, a pick backed by Republican Senator Roger Wicker and former Republican governors Haley Barbour and Phil Bryant. The nomination stalled after Hyde-Smith declined to return her blue slip, the Senate Judiciary Committee procedure that allows home-state senators to weigh in on judicial nominees, citing Colom's past support for a political action committee funded by billionaire George Soros and his position on legislation concerning transgender athletes. Colom subsequently launched his Senate campaign in September 2025, challenging the senator who had derailed his judicial nomination, casting himself as an independent-minded Mississippian rather than a national party figure.

He built his campaign around pocketbook issues, making the cost of living the centrepiece of his message and accusing Hyde-Smith of neglecting the state and "selling out" Mississippians. He advocated a higher minimum wage, lower costs and protections for rural hospitals, and also spoke in favour of ending the US-Israel war on Iran. His campaign gained national traction with a series of unconventional advertisements, including a viral spot mocking Hyde-Smith's response to questions about rising beef prices, in which she had suggested shoppers pick another protein. Colom's advertisement called on people not to pick another protein, but to "pick another senator instead". He also pointed to the Supreme Court's Louisiana v. Callais decision on congressional redistricting and voting rights as a factor that helped galvanise Black voters. Final certified totals are not expected until Friday at 5 p.m., and Mississippi has no recount process for a federal race. Colom will be sworn in next January.
