US Proposes New Tariffs on 14 Trading Partners to Fight Excess Factory Capacity

business · 25 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The proposed duties would hit China hardest and spare Mexico and Norway for now, while the US Trade Representative publishes the full rate schedule. The duties are proposed, not yet in effect, and face a public comment period and a hearing first.

The Trump administration on Wednesday proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners to combat what it calls structural excess industrial capacity, with the highest rates to hit China, which Washington blames most for flooding world markets with cheap exports. The US Trade Representative's office announced the duties after completing an eight-month investigation under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 into 16 trading partners, including close allies Japan, the European Union and South Korea.

The announcement turns Tuesday's expectation into a formal proposal. A day earlier, three people with knowledge of the matter had told Reuters that the findings of the probe were about to be released, with new import duties expected as soon as Tuesday or Wednesday. Now the administration has named the countries in the firing line — China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam — and said the specific rates would be published in a US Federal Register notice on Wednesday.

Two countries on the original list were spared, at least for now. For the time being, the US Trade Representative did not propose tariffs on goods from Mexico and Norway, and a senior US official said the office was still reviewing the excess capacity probes into those two countries, declining to give a timeline for their completion. That is a significant carve-out: Mexico is the United States' largest trading partner, and any duties on its goods would have carried immediate consequences for the North American supply chains built under the US-Mexico-Canada trade agreement.

Washington's conclusion, in the words of the senior official, is that each of the 14 trading partners “creates, sustains, or contributes to structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors through various acts, policies, and practices that we find to be unreasonable and burden or restrict US commerce.” The Trade Representative defines structural excess capacity as situations where countries “are promoting production and capacity divorced from the realities of demand and market principles,” including through industrial policies, subsidies and the use of components imported from non-market economies.

China would face the highest tariff rates of the 14 trading partners. The proposed 20% excess-capacity tariffs on Chinese goods would be in addition to the roughly 25% tariffs imposed during President Donald Trump's first term in office, the official said, adding that this would keep China above the 20% proposed rate for Vietnam. In other words, Beijing is not just first on the list — it is singled out for the steepest treatment, exactly as the administration signalled when it launched the probe in March.

There are limits, though, and the administration was careful to spell them out. The proposed duties would not stack on top of the 10% or 12.5% tariffs imposed in July on a wider group of 60 trading partners over allegations of lax enforcement of anti-forced-labor laws. Nor would the new duties exceed the tariff limits in trade deals the administration has already negotiated: a 20% cap for China during Trump's second term under a year-old trade truce with Beijing that is due to expire on 10 January, a 15% cap agreed by the European Union, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, and a 19% cap agreed by Malaysia.

The duties would also not apply to about $30 billion worth of Chinese imports that the United States is considering for tariff reductions under a bilateral process known as the “Board of Trade.” The administration says the two tracks — new duties on the one hand, tariff cuts on non-strategic goods on the other — can run in parallel. And despite the escalation, Washington said it did not expect Beijing to retaliate against the new excess-capacity duties. “We know that they don't like these actions, but we are seeking stability with the Chinese. China is seeking stability with us,” the official said, adding that Trump's meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping last week was “really great” and that there had been progress on supplies of Chinese rare earth minerals in recent days.

The proposal is, in substance, the rebuilding of the tariff regime the US Supreme Court struck down in February. The court invalidated the administration's sweeping global import taxes, which had been imposed under an emergency-powers statute; the new duties and the July forced-labor duties are widely viewed as the administration's attempt to reconstruct that regime on firmer legal ground. Section 301 is the instrument for the job: it allows the Trade Representative to investigate foreign practices it deems unfair and to impose duties in response, and Trump used it in his first term to levy tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods, setting off the US-China trade war of 2018 and 2019.

Not every country on the list has a deal to cap what it will face. Several countries, such as India and Vietnam, do not have finalized trade agreements with the United States, and the official said the new duties were expected to result in more trade agreements being negotiated and finalized, possibly at a G20 leaders' summit in Miami in December. The message is plain: the tariffs are not only a punishment but a lever, and countries that want lower rates will be expected to come to the table.

For now, nothing has actually taken effect. The proposed duties will be subject to a public notice and comment period, with a hearing scheduled before they go into effect, and the official said that process could push implementation into January. That timetable matters: it gives affected governments and American businesses weeks to lobby, to argue for exemptions, and to prepare. US officials briefed congressional committees on the changes late on Tuesday, and the affected countries were also notified.

The politics of the move are complicated by the cost of the tariffs already in place. The administration's tariffs have in part contributed to higher prices on consumer goods in the United States, and a Reuters/Ipsos opinion poll conducted after November's midterm elections showed that 62% of respondents would support efforts to repeal the tariffs. With the midterm results fresh, a new round of duties risks sharpening a debate the White House has struggled to win: whether tariffs are protecting American industry or taxing American shoppers.

Nowhere is that tension sharper than in the relationship with China. The administration has singled out Beijing as the biggest source of excess industrial capacity, arguing that Chinese factories are flooding global markets with steel, aluminum, electric vehicles, appliances, solar products and other goods, driving down prices and undercutting producers elsewhere. China has rejected the accusations, saying its exports reflect competitive advantages — cheaper energy, scale and manufacturing know-how — rather than unfair state support. The probe is also partly directed at Chinese investment in Southeast Asia, where Chinese firms have built factories in countries including Vietnam, Indonesia and Cambodia to manufacture goods for the US market — a route the administration sees as a way of avoiding tariffs imposed directly on China.

Why it matters: the proposal is the administration's most consequential trade decision since the Supreme Court struck down its global tariffs in February. If the duties take effect in January, they would reshape the cost of imports from 14 economies that together account for the overwhelming bulk of what America buys from abroad, test the fragile trade truce with China weeks before it expires on 10 January, and put the administration's central economic argument — that tariffs rebuild American industry — to a fresh test with voters who, according to the latest polling, are increasingly sceptical. What the Federal Register notice says about rates and exemptions, who lobbies for relief in the comment period, and whether Beijing holds its fire will set the terms of that argument for the year ahead.
