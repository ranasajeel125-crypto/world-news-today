Zelensky Warns Russia Is Planning New Strikes on Energy, Bridges and Civilian Targets

world · 7 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Zelenskiy said Ukraine's air force and armed forces were preparing for defence; hours later, ballistic missiles struck Kyiv overnight, damaging a kindergarten building.

world · 7 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said on Friday that intelligence showed that Russian forces were planning new strikes against key targets and the Ukrainian military was braced for a tough mission.

"We have intelligence at this time that Russia is preparing new strikes on energy, on bridges across the Dnipro, on civilian infrastructure," Zelenskiy said in his nightly video address. "The air force and the armed forces of Ukraine as a whole are preparing for defence. It will not be easy."

Zelenskiy repeated his calls for allies to supply Ukraine quickly with missiles for the US-made Patriot system to defend Ukrainian cities. "Every day in the delivery schedule is critically important," he said. "We very much ask our partners not to postpone deliveries." He said Ukraine needed interceptor missiles for its Patriots, and that every agreement mattered and every day counted when it came to deliveries.

The warning followed a barrage of Russian strikes on energy facilities across Ukraine. Kyiv, along with other Ukrainian cities, has been subjected to near-constant attacks for several weeks. Two major bridges in the capital were hit by Russian forces late last month. Since October 1, Russian drones have repeatedly hit Kyiv's South Bridge, a major transport crossing connecting the capital's eastern and western banks of the Dnipro River, and later the North Bridge. On October 27, Russian forces struck the South Bridge again, prompting traffic closures.

The bridges matter because the Dnipro River divides Ukraine, and Kyiv, in two. The river runs through the middle of the capital, and the bridges are the lifelines that carry traffic, supplies and military logistics between the eastern and western banks. If the bridges are closed, even temporarily, the city is cut in two, and repair crews, fuel and military supplies cannot move freely. According to a Ukrainian armed forces officer, Russia is using a "small cuts" approach, with repeated strikes that force bridges to close for inspections and repairs rather than destroy them outright. Each strike, even if it does not bring a bridge down, takes it out of service while engineers check the damage.

The energy campaign is the other half of the strategy. Overnight on November 2-3, Russia launched what the state grid operator Ukrenergo called one of the largest attacks on Ukraine's energy sector, targeting facilities more than 20 times in 24 hours. The strikes knocked three DTEK thermal power plants offline and forced emergency blackouts in Kyiv, the Kyiv region and the Dnipropetrovsk region. Russia's latest assault has battered Kyiv's energy infrastructure, causing mass blackouts and the worst water supply shortages of the full-scale war. The attacks are aimed at heating, water and power as winter approaches, when temperatures fall and the loss of heating becomes a threat to life.

Russia's Chief of General Staff, Valery Gerasimov, reported to President Vladimir Putin that Russian forces began specifically targeting strategic bridges, railway infrastructure and data centres across Ukraine on October 16, publicly acknowledging the campaign for the first time. Around the same time, Zelenskiy said Putin had ordered his military commanders to abandon previous constraints on aerial attacks and intensify strikes on civilian sites. The combination of targets is the point: a strike on the power grid forces blackouts, and a strike on the Dnipro crossings prevents repair crews, fuel and military logistics from moving between the eastern front and the western rear.

Hours after the warning, Russian ballistic missiles struck Kyiv overnight on 6-7 November, damaging a building on the grounds of a kindergarten and injuring three people, including a child, according to Ukrainian officials. Ukraine's air force said at least six ballistic missiles were detected heading towards the capital shortly before 11 p.m. local time on Friday. On November 7, Zelenskiy said Russia had fired 36 ballistic missiles and Ukraine had shot down only four, a figure that underlined how hard ballistic missiles are to intercept and how thin Ukraine's air defences have become.

The Patriot system is central to that defence. Patriot is a US-made surface-to-air missile system designed to shoot down aircraft, cruise missiles and ballistic missiles, and it is the only system in Ukraine's arsenal that can reliably intercept the ballistic missiles Russia is firing at Kyiv. But interceptors are in short supply, and each one is expensive. The US State Department said last week it had approved the sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 missiles to Ukraine for an estimated $2.1 billion. The missiles are, however, not expected to be delivered until the early 2030s, which means the approval does little to help Ukraine this winter. Zelenskiy's plea was for deliveries to be speeded up, not for new approvals.

In a separate statement on Friday, Zelenskiy said Kyiv would keep striking Russian oil refineries in retaliation for Moscow's attacks on Ukraine's power grid, heating and water supply systems. "The Russians are not changing the nature of their aggressive activity," he said, adding that they had refused to take de-escalation steps, as discussed with Ukraine's partners, and that they were counting on further attacks. Ukraine's long-range strikes on Russian energy infrastructure have become a central part of its strategy, aimed at imposing costs on Moscow for the attacks on Ukrainian cities.

The warning came as trilateral talks involving Ukrainian and Russian envoys and US mediators were scheduled for November 10 and 11 in Abu Dhabi, the first such direct meetings in months. The timing of the strikes, and of Zelenskiy's warning, underlined the gap between diplomacy and the battlefield: even as talks were being prepared, the attacks on Ukraine's energy grid and bridges were intensifying, and the winter ahead promised to be the hardest of the war.
