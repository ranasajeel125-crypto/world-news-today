Bank of England Raises Rates for First Time Since 2023, Says More May Come

business · 5 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

The Bank of England had held off raising rates while its main peers tightened in response to inflation pressures from the war with Iran.

The Bank of England raised interest rates for the first time in over three years on Thursday and warned that at least one more increase was likely if the US-Iran conflict continues, Reuters reported.

The central bank's Monetary Policy Committee voted 8-1 to raise rates to 4% from 3.75%. The dissent came not from a policymaker seeking to keep rates on hold but from one seeking a bigger hike: Catherine Mann voted for a half-point increase to 4.25%. The decision was unanimous over the rate hike itself, and divided only on its size. Mann had said last month that the Bank had been slow to reassure markets that it had a grip on inflation.

"Today's increase shouldn't be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes. But unless the conflict in the Middle East is resolved, neither does it seem likely to be the last increase," Governor Andrew Bailey said. The Bank indicated that the sharp decline in energy prices after the US midterm elections, if sustained, could limit the prospect of further interest rate hikes in the coming months, and Bailey said at a press briefing that "we've seen encouraging news in the past 48 hours."

British inflation is likely to reach around 5% in the middle of next year, the Bank said, more than double its 2% target, a blow for new Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who has sought to ease cost of living pressures. The Bank now sees inflation peaking at an average 4.8% in the second quarter of next year and not returning to 2% until the third quarter of 2028. UK inflation stood at 3.5% in September, and the prolonged conflict in the Middle East has continued to drive up energy prices.

Until now, the Bank of England had not followed the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and other central banks in raising rates in response to inflation pressures from the Iran war, which began in late February. The decision ends a run of three rate holds in 2026 and six rate cuts in 2024 and 2025, and it is the first time the Bank has raised the base rate since August 2023, when it lifted rates to 5.25%. Sterling strengthened against the US dollar after the decision and two-year British government bond yields rose by 4 basis points.

The Bank of England is the United Kingdom's central bank, and its main job is to keep inflation at 2%, a target set by the government. Its Monetary Policy Committee, a group of nine people including the governor, meets eight times a year to set Bank Rate, the interest rate the Bank pays on reserves held by commercial banks, which in turn shapes the rates that households and businesses pay on mortgages, loans and savings. Raising Bank Rate makes borrowing more expensive and saving more rewarding, which is intended to cool spending and bring inflation down. Cutting it does the opposite.

The reason the Bank has moved now is energy. The war in the Middle East has pushed up the price of oil and gas, and those higher energy prices feed through into almost everything else: transport, heating, food production and manufacturing. When energy prices rise sharply, inflation rises with them, and the Bank's concern is that higher inflation becomes embedded in the economy, through wage demands and price rises, even after energy prices themselves stabilise. That is the "second-round effect" the Bank has warned about, and it is the reason it acted even though the British economy is not booming.

For households, the immediate effect is on mortgages. Borrowers on tracker mortgages, whose rates move directly with Bank Rate, will see their monthly payments rise at once. Those on variable rates will see a rise if their lender passes it on, while those on fixed-rate deals are protected until their deal ends. An example given by financial educators was an increase of about 29 pounds a month on a 200,000-pound loan. Savers, by contrast, may see slightly better returns, although banks are often slow to pass higher rates on to savings accounts.

For the government, the decision is awkward. Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who is new in office, has sought to ease cost of living pressures, and a rise in interest rates makes that harder, by raising the cost of borrowing for households and businesses and by increasing the government's own debt interest bill. At the same time, the Bank is independent of the government in setting interest rates, a principle established in 1997, and the government cannot overrule its decisions. The Bank's mandate is to control inflation, even when that means short-term pain for borrowers.

The decision also puts the Bank on a path already begun by its main peers. The US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and other central banks have already raised rates in response to inflation pressures from the Iran war, and the Bank of England had been the outlier in holding off. Its move brings it into line, but it also raises the question of how far it will have to go. The Bank's next interest rate decision is scheduled for December 17, and markets will be watching closely for any signal about whether a further rise is coming.

The outlook, the Bank said, largely hinges on what happens with the Iran war. If the conflict is resolved, energy prices should fall and inflation should come down, reducing the need for further rises. If it continues, the Bank has warned that at least one more increase is likely. The sharp decline in energy prices after the US midterm elections offered a glimmer of hope, but the Bank was careful not to overstate it, saying only that if the decline is sustained it could limit the prospect of further hikes.

For businesses, higher rates mean more expensive borrowing for investment and expansion, and they come at a time when many firms are already facing higher energy bills. For the housing market, they mean higher mortgage costs, which tend to cool demand and slow price growth. For the pound, the rise in rates made sterling more attractive to investors, which is why it strengthened against the dollar after the announcement.

The Bank's forecast that inflation will not return to 2% until the third quarter of 2028 is a sobering one. It implies more than two years of above-target inflation, and it means that the cost of living pressures that have dominated British politics for several years are not going away soon. The Bank's job is to bring inflation back to target without causing a recession, a balance that has proved difficult before and that will be tested again if the war in the Middle East drags on. Thursday's rise was a first step, and the Bank has made clear it may not be the last.
