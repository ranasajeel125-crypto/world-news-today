Paraguay President Santiago Pena Resigns as Bank Collapse Sparks Crisis

world · 5 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Vice President Pedro Alliana is set to succeed Pena if Congress accepts his resignation.

world · 5 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Paraguayan President Santiago Pena resigned on Thursday after the collapse of digital lender ueno left millions of customers unable to access their accounts, triggering a political crisis in one of Washington's closest allies in South America, Reuters reported.

Pena, 47, an economist who was elected in 2023, announced his resignation in a video posted on X, saying he was stepping down with the stability of the country, social peace and the need to preserve democratic institutions in mind. "I have made the decision to submit my resignation as president of Paraguay," he said. "I want to apologize to the people if I have failed them at any point."

Minutes after the announcement, Paraguay's Chamber of Deputies voted unanimously to open impeachment proceedings against him, while lawmakers were debating charges brought by 35 opposition lawmakers and ruling-party dissidents accusing him of serious dereliction of duty. The lower house voted 78-0 to impeach him, as thousands of Paraguayans protested for a third day over frozen savings, pensions and wages. The Senate will determine whether the proceedings move forward, and both chambers of Congress were due to meet in a joint session to consider his resignation.

Ueno, which grew rapidly from a niche fintech player into Paraguay's fifth-largest bank, had about 3 million customers in a country of about 6.5 million people and administered close to $850 million in public funds, including pension money. The bank said it had 2.9 million customers, around 60 percent of Paraguay's adult population. The central bank intervened earlier this week after finding the bank had breached minimum solvency requirements, leaving customers temporarily unable to access their accounts. Ueno Bank launched as a fintech in 2021 and began operating as a bank in November 2023, and it went from an obscure finance company to a household name in less than five years.

The collapse began on Monday, when Paraguay's central bank decided to put Ueno into resolution, the strongest action it can take short of placing the bank into liquidation, after finding that its solvency ratio, a measure of its capital against its risk-weighted assets, had dropped to less than half of the legal requirement. Regulators investigating Ueno's loans to companies linked to its owners found poorly documented lending, as well as expenses the bank had wrongly counted as assets. Once the books were corrected, the bank's capital, the cushion banks must keep to absorb losses, fell to less than half the legal minimum. The central bank found a pattern of risky lending to insiders: Ueno had loaned $146 million to companies tied to its owner, Grupo Vazquez. The bank managed about $800 million of public funds, including about $380 million invested by Paraguay's state pension and health fund, according to data from the Central Bank of Paraguay.

The central bank seized the lender on Tuesday, and Ueno's app, cards and transfers went dark, its branches locked their doors, and desperate depositors lined up outside with no way to cash out their savings. The sudden closure sparked street demonstrations in Asuncion after depositors were unable to access their accounts, and protesters clashed with riot police. Demonstrators burned Ueno Bank cards and a flag depicting Pena outside the National Congress, and one protester told reporters that Pena and the ruling Colorado Party had stolen all their savings. The bank's nearly 3 million customers still cannot withdraw cash, make digital payments or use their bank cards.

Pena has faced accusations of conflict of interest, misuse of pension funds and institutional cover-up over his past business ties to Grupo Vazquez, the conglomerate that controls ueno. He has said he was a shareholder until March 2025, when he sold his stake; ueno says he never had a direct relationship with the bank as a shareholder or investor. Prosecutors allege that, while president, he used his position to influence public agencies and state-owned entities to maintain deposits with the bank despite his connections to its parent group. Pena requested a 72-hour extension to present his defence at an impeachment hearing, saying he had been given less than 12 hours to answer an accusatory writ and about 300 pages of attachments, adding that that was not due process.

Prosecutors said on Thursday they had opened a criminal investigation into Pena, and arrest warrants have been issued for eight ueno executives this week. Authorities detained eight senior executives, including founder Miguel Vazquez and bank president Juan Manuel Gustale. Pena has denied any wrongdoing.

What followed the resignation was swift. Pena resigned on Thursday and was removed from office on Friday, when the Senate voted unanimously to dismiss him after carrying out impeachment proceedings, accusing him of alleged diversion of pension funds and illicit intervention. The following day, prosecutors charged him with breach of trust and administration for personal gain, and he was ordered held pending the investigation. He has pleaded not guilty. Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in as president to serve out the rest of Pena's term until August 2028, and he is constitutionally in line to succeed Pena for the remainder of his five-year term, which runs to 2028.

Congress also rushed through a law providing a state-guaranteed fund for repaying depositors within five business days, starting on Thursday with small accounts holding up to $1,600. The crisis has also put the spotlight on Horacio Cartes, a tobacco magnate and former president of Paraguay who wields considerable political and economic influence inside the ruling Colorado Party. For Paraguay, the collapse of a bank that served half the population, and the fall of a president who was a close ally of President Donald Trump, is the deepest political crisis in more than a decade, and the question now is whether the state can repay depositors, restore confidence in the banking system, and hold those responsible to account.
