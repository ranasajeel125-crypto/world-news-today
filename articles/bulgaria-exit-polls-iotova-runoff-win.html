Exit Polls Put Bulgaria's Iotova Ahead in Presidential Runoff

world · 1 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Two exit polls put Iotova on 51% and 52.5% respectively, against 44.7% and 43.4% for Gyurov; turnout was estimated at 26.8%.

Exit polls published after voting ended at 8 p.m. local time on Sunday put Bulgaria's incumbent president, Iliana Iotova, ahead of former caretaker prime minister Andrey Gyurov in the country's presidential runoff, Bulgarian National Radio reported, putting her on course to become the country's first elected female head of state.

The Myara exit poll for BNR, taken as of 8 p.m., gave the Iotova-Kiril Valchev ticket 51 per cent of the vote, against 44.7 per cent for the Gyurov-Georgi Kandev ticket, with 4.3 per cent choosing "I do not support any candidate". A second exit poll, by Alpha Research, put Iotova ahead by 52.5 per cent to 43.4 per cent. A third poll, by Market Links for BTV, gave her 50.7 per cent against 44 per cent for Gyurov. Turnout was estimated at 26.8 per cent, only a few percentage points higher than the record low of about 23 per cent in the first round.

An Associated Press report on the exit polls said Iotova, at around 52 per cent, was the likely winner, with Gyurov on around 44 per cent, and noted that if the result was confirmed she would become Bulgaria's first woman to be elected head of state. Flanked by Prime Minister Rumen Radev, a jubilant Iotova declared victory after the first exit polls showed her in the lead, saying her message had centred on national consensus and bridging the country's political rifts.

The exit polls were not the official result, but the count that followed confirmed them and widened her margin. With 99.95 per cent of polling station protocols processed, the Central Election Commission said Iotova had won 57.58 per cent of the vote, about 1.09 million votes, while Gyurov received 39.47 per cent, about 750,000 votes. Parallel counts by five leading polling agencies had placed her share at between 56.6 and 58.2 per cent, and Gyurov accepted the projected outcome. Iotova won 28 of Bulgaria's 31 regions, while Gyurov led in Sofia city and among voters abroad, where he took 56.4 per cent against her 41.4 per cent. She is expected to be inaugurated on 22 January for a five-year term.

The runoff followed a tight first round on 25 October in which neither candidate reached the threshold for an outright win in a field of 27 candidate tickets, and in which only about 23 per cent of eligible voters cast ballots, a record low for a presidential election in Bulgaria. The low turnout in both rounds reflected widespread disengagement in a country of 6.4 million people that has endured years of political instability, with repeated parliamentary elections and caretaker governments.

The runoff was described in reporting on the vote as a choice between Iotova, who favours dialogue with Moscow and opposes military aid to Ukraine, and Gyurov, a banker and former caretaker prime minister backed by pro-Western opposition parties, at a time of heightened Black Sea security tensions. Iotova is an ally of Prime Minister Rumen Radev, who stepped down from the presidency in January to run in parliamentary elections, when she took over as acting president. She began her career as a television journalist in the 1990s and previously served in both the Bulgarian Parliament and the European Parliament, representing the Socialist Party.

The contest took place against the backdrop of a series of drone attacks in the past month on merchant ships off Bulgaria's Black Sea coast. Bulgaria is a member of both NATO and the European Union, and the security of Black Sea shipping has become a central concern for Sofia since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Iotova's victory was seen as a political boost for Radev, whose camp also holds a parliamentary majority, and as strengthening a ruling camp that opposes military aid to Ukraine and seeks closer ties with Moscow.

Reuters describes Bulgaria's presidency as a largely ceremonial role, with most executive power resting with the prime minister and the government. Even so, the president holds influence over foreign policy, can veto legislation, appoints caretaker governments when parliament cannot form one, and commands the armed forces, which is why the contest between a candidate favouring dialogue with Moscow and one backed by the pro-Western opposition was watched closely in Brussels and other European capitals.

The result was welcomed by Iotova's supporters as a mandate for consensus and dialogue, while her critics warned that a president sympathetic to Moscow could complicate Bulgaria's position within the EU and NATO at a moment of war on the Black Sea. What is not in doubt is the historic character of the outcome: after decades in which the presidency was held by men, Bulgaria has elected its first woman as head of state, and she did so with a decisive margin despite the lowest turnouts in the country's presidential history.
