IMF Warns Developing Economies Face Mounting Financial Pressure

business · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Daniyal Ahmed (Business Correspondent)

Emerging-market investors face higher borrowing costs and a strong dollar, the IMF warns.

The warning came in the International Monetary Fund's October 2026 World Economic Outlook, titled "Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices", released on 13 October during the Fund's annual meetings in Bangkok. The report raised its forecast for global growth to 3.1 per cent in 2026 and 3.4 per cent in 2027, but said that resilience was becoming uneven, with the poorest countries absorbing the heaviest blows from energy shocks, high borrowing costs and shrinking fiscal space.

The Fund described a world economy split in two. Advanced economies were forecast to grow by 2.1 per cent in 2026, lifted by a surge of investment in artificial intelligence, with economies in the AI supply chain such as Taiwan and South Korea posting significantly higher figures. Low-income developing countries, by contrast, were being battered by the energy shock from the war involving the United States and Iran, the strongest El Niño on record and higher global interest rates, while missing out on the technology investment boom almost entirely.

Debt is at the centre of the squeeze. Average debt-service costs for developing countries have risen to about 14 per cent of government revenue, compared with about 6 per cent a decade ago, and low-income governments are now spending roughly 8 to 14 per cent of their revenue on debt repayments. The chair of the Group of 24, Taiwo Oyedele, noted during the meetings that developing countries pay 500 billion dollars more for debt than advanced economies. Global public debt stands at almost 100 per cent of gross domestic product, above the level recorded after the Second World War, and the Fund projects public debt in emerging and developing economies to rise from 74 per cent of GDP in 2025 to 84 per cent by 2031.

For ordinary people, the pressure shows up in the cost of living. The Fund warned that low-income households remain particularly vulnerable to food-price increases driven by transport disruptions, higher fertiliser costs, export restrictions and extreme weather. When borrowing costs rise in major economies, developing countries face higher financing costs and reduced access to international capital, and when loans are denominated in dollars, every rise in the US currency makes repayment heavier in local terms. The United Nations Development Programme has warned that as many as 130 million people could be pushed into poverty if the price shocks are passed through in full.

Many governments now face what the Fund's officials described as an impossible choice. Petya Koeva Brooks, the IMF's deputy director of research, told reporters in Bangkok that heavily indebted oil-importing countries were facing the worst of the energy shock, and that many had "no choice" but to cut spending or face debt restructuring. She said that after years of shock after shock, debt levels had risen sharply while inflation had become a problem, leaving the most vulnerable countries with far more difficult trade-offs than richer ones.

The Fund's advice is to use narrow, targeted subsidies to protect the poorest households rather than broad support that governments can no longer afford, and to rebuild fiscal buffers through credible revenue measures and more efficient spending. Where public debt is no longer sustainable, the Fund argues that an orderly and timely restructuring is always better than waiting, because the cost of restructuring further down the road will be much higher. Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva raised the same concerns after the Group of Twenty finance ministers and central bank governors met in Asheville in the United States on 1 September, calling for faster debt restructuring, including further improvements under the G20 Common Framework.

Georgieva's message to policymakers was blunt about the priorities. Central banks should focus on their price-stability mandate, while fiscal authorities should draw up credible medium-term plans to bring borrowing down. The Fund expects global inflation to reach 4.7 per cent in 2026 before easing to 4.1 per cent in 2027, leaving central banks with difficult choices between containing prices and supporting economic activity. Average ten-year government bond yields across the G7 rose by more than one percentage point between February and September 2026, pushing up the cost of borrowing for governments and businesses competing for the same capital.

The risks hanging over the outlook are unusually large. The Fund's forecasts assume that the Strait of Hormuz reopens by next March and that AI investment remains strong. In a worse-case scenario, a sharp reassessment of AI profitability could trigger a market correction, reduce investment and tighten global financial conditions, cutting US output by 1.8 per cent and global output by 1.1 per cent by 2028. The October Global Financial Stability Report also flagged vulnerabilities linked to private credit, hedge funds, insurers and pension funds, where rising borrower leverage and limited transparency could become dangerous in a downturn.

There are also signs of a response. The World Bank reported mobilising a record 112 billion dollars in private capital during its 2026 financial year, compared with about 35 billion dollars in 2022, and 25 billion dollars in guarantees, while its president, Ajay Banga, outlined an initiative with six multilateral development banks to standardise the assessment and packaging of development investments. But the money remains unevenly spread: private capital mobilisation in low-income countries stood at only about 3 billion dollars, highlighting the financing gap the Fund says must be closed.
