Thousands Protest in Prague Over Czech Government's Public Media Overhaul

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

Protesters filled Prague's historic Old Town Square on Sunday, chanting “Resignation” and “We've had enough”.

Thousands protested in Prague on Sunday after government lawmakers gave initial backing to public media funding changes in a rare overnight vote, despite criticism that the overhaul will damage media independence.

Protesters filled Prague's historic Old Town Square on Sunday, chanting "Resignation" and "We've had enough". The proposal's first reading was unexpectedly brought to the lower house floor outside the planned order of proceedings on Friday. Opponents and media advocacy groups have objected to fast-tracking the legislation, which will abolish household fees that currently fund Czech public television and radio stations.

The populist government of billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babis, backed by the far-right SPD party, has frequently clashed with public and private media. It wants to move funding for Czech Television and Czech Radio to the state budget, which critics say will leave broadcasters vulnerable to political pressure. Babis says it is a matter of funding transparency and not an attack on independence. His coalition, comprising his catch-all ANO party, the far-right SPD and the Eurosceptic Motorists, insists the plan will not affect editorial independence or programme content — but that broadcasters need to cut costs. Babis has been in office since December 2025, and abolishing the fee was in the coalition's manifesto, which he says eases the burden on households.

Czech households currently pay 205 crowns, about 8 euros, a month to fund public broadcasting: 150 crowns for television and 55 for radio, with larger firms paying multiples of that. The government's bill abolishes the fee and has the state budget pay Czech Television and Czech Radio instead. For 2027 it sets about 5.74 billion crowns for Czech Television and 2.07 billion for Czech Radio. Media-freedom groups say that amounts to a cut of about 15 per cent, or 1.4 billion crowns a year. Critics say the change would reduce television's budget to 2008 levels and radio's to 2005 levels. The proposal was introduced by Culture Minister Oto Klempir in April, and it has already led to several large street protests since then.

Smaller protests first broke out on Friday at the bill's unexpected introduction. Speaker Tomio Okamura of the SPD limited debate and scheduled a vote for 4:36 a.m. (0336 GMT) on Saturday. In the early hours of Saturday, 24 October, the Chamber of Deputies rejected opposition motions to throw the bill out, despite 65 deputies waiting to speak. The bill is now at the committee stage before a later vote. Under the Chamber's guide to the legislative process, the bill must pass four stages. It now faces a long road before it can become law. The coalition holds 108 of 200 seats, enough to override both the Senate and a veto, but a senior ANO figure, deputy speaker Patrik Nacher, has said he expects the Senate to send the bill back and does not rule out a presidential veto. Babis has himself admitted the vote did not go well, and has hinted that changes may come at second reading, though the coalition has not agreed on a model yet. The time of the vote — 4:36 — has become another symbol of the dispute, and a later rally was dubbed "Never Again 4:36".

"Mr Babis ... Mr Okamura, nobody in this country believes that your intention was pure," Mikulas Minar, a protest leader, said on Sunday, criticising the overnight vote. "(This protest) is a clear message to this government: you have really gone too far this time," he told protesters who had filled Prague's historic Old Town Square, amid chants of "Resignation" and "We've had enough". Organisers plan further protests. Minar is the head of the Million Moments for Democracy movement, which organised the demonstrations with backing from unions, students, universities, churches, artists, athletes and international media bodies. He later presented a petition calling on the government to withdraw the media financing bill, but also to dismiss Okamura, accusing him of endangering national security.

Czech Television is the country's most-watched broadcaster across major age groups. Public media are among the most trusted news sources in the country, according to a 2025 Reuters Institute survey. The proposal would cut funding for Czech Television and Czech Radio by about 15%, possibly as early as next year.

Opposition parties have compared the funding changes with reforms in Hungary under former Prime Minister Viktor Orban, who oversaw a major expansion of pro-government media. On Friday, the European Broadcasting Union, along with 15 other groups including other public broadcasters as well as Reporters Without Borders, called for more debate and the removal of any measures threatening the broadcasters' financial sustainability. President Petr Pavel also called for more expert scrutiny. "The Czech Republic is among the few countries in central and eastern Europe where the system of public service and private media works very well," he said on X on Saturday.

The dispute did not end with the October protest. On 3 November, Czech Television and Czech Radio staff went on a one-day strike, while thousands including university students rallied across the country. Then, on 15 November, an estimated 350,000 to 450,000 people gathered on Prague's Letná plain — organisers put the crowd at between 350,000 and 450,000, and Czech Radio described it as the largest protest since the Czech Republic became independent in 1993. The rally came days before the 17 November holiday commemorating the 1989 Velvet Revolution, and opposition leaders attended, turning it into a wider protest against Babis's government. Demonstrators demanded the government withdraw the bill, hold an open debate and apologise for the night vote, and called for the removal of Okamura as Chamber of Deputies chairman. The opposition also intends to challenge the law in the constitutional court. What began as a dispute over a household fee has become one of the largest civic mobilisations in the country's modern history — and a test of whether a government with a parliamentary majority can reshape the funding of the broadcasters that most Czechs trust, over the objections of the broadcasters themselves, the president and hundreds of thousands of people in the streets.
