Two Oil Tankers Hit Mines, Explode and Catch Fire in Strait of Hormuz, Iran Says

world · 25 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly a fifth of the world's petroleum liquids and is the only sea route out for Gulf liquefied natural gas. Iran says the two tankers were using a route it had not authorised.

Two oil tankers struck naval mines, exploded and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday, Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said in a statement carried by state media, in the latest attack on shipping in the waterway through which about a fifth of the world's oil and gas flows.

The statement said the vessels had been attempting to transit what Iran described as an unauthorised route through the strait. No details of the tankers — their names, flags, owners, cargoes or crews — were immediately available, and no casualty figures were reported.

In a separate statement on Wednesday, Iranian Army spokesperson Mohammad Akraminia said the military had deployed new systems and upgraded existing ones to strengthen surveillance and control of the strait. He warned that vessels passing through without coordinating with Iranian forces had faced greater risks and had struck mines in recent weeks — a message that frames passage through Hormuz as something ships must now negotiate with Tehran, not simply navigate.

The incident is the latest in a sustained campaign against shipping in the strait that has not eased even as traffic through the waterway has recovered. On Friday 13 November, Iran's Revolutionary Guards navy said a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas had struck a naval mine and suffered an explosion in the strait, also while using what they described as an unauthorised and dangerous route, and Iranian state media broadcast footage of a ship burning in the waterway. A day earlier, Iranian media had reported that four oil tankers were hit by explosions there on Thursday.

At least 10 ships were hit in the first 10 days of November, after 37 attacks in October, which was the highest monthly tally since the conflict began in late February, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy. The pace of attacks in November suggests the danger to shipping has not eased, even as traffic through the strait has recovered and markets have begun to price in a lower risk of a wider war.

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the most important waterways in the world for energy. It is the narrow channel between Iran and Oman through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas must pass, connecting the oil-producing states of the Gulf with the open ocean. Roughly a fifth of the world's petroleum liquids pass through the strait, and for the gas-exporting states of the Gulf, above all Qatar, it is the only sea route out. Any sustained threat to shipping there raises insurance costs, pushes up freight rates and feeds directly into global energy prices.

The oil market reacted to the latest reports before Wednesday's incident was even confirmed in detail. Brent crude opened 0.5% lower at $90.97 a barrel in Asia on Wednesday after a choppy session on Tuesday, which saw crude prices settle more than 2% higher as new attacks were reported on vessels in and around the Strait of Hormuz. The swing captures the market's dilemma: supply is rising and a surplus is expected, but every new attack on a tanker in the strait is a reminder that the physical flow of oil through the world's most important chokepoint is not guaranteed.

The attacks also come with a political price tag attached. Tehran has told Washington it will not restore normal shipping through the Strait of Hormuz until its demands are met, including the lifting of a US naval blockade of Iranian ports and an end to the war on all fronts. Under a proposal conveyed through Qatari mediators, Tehran offered to reopen the waterway within 48 hours of Washington meeting its conditions. The offer makes explicit what the pattern of attacks has implied for months: that the mines and the warnings are, in Tehran's hands, leverage in a negotiation as much as weapons of war.

The pattern of the recent attacks is striking. The vessels hit have, according to Iranian media, been accused of ignoring warnings and entering areas Iran considers restricted, or of using routes Iran had not authorised. That framing suggests the attacks are not random but are aimed at enforcing Iranian control over which ships may use the waterway and on what terms. For shipowners and charterers, the message is that passage through Hormuz now depends on more than navigation: it depends on political calculations about which routes are safe and which flags, cargoes and destinations might draw attention.

Naval mines are among the oldest and cheapest weapons of sea warfare, and also among the hardest to defend against. A mine anchored to the seabed or floating just below the surface can wait silently for weeks or months before a passing ship triggers it, and clearing a mined waterway is slow, dangerous work that requires specialised minesweepers. The use of mines in a narrow, congested channel like Hormuz is particularly disruptive, because ships have little room to manoeuvre around a suspected minefield and because even an unconfirmed mine sighting can force a channel to close while it is investigated.

The incident followed a separate Iranian claim on Tuesday night, when the Iranian Army said its air defence forces had shot down two US drones over the strait — a Lucas suicide drone in the west and an MQ-9 in the east, according to the semi-official Tasnim news agency. The claims have not been independently verified and the United States has not confirmed them. They are the latest in a series of Iranian drone shoot-down claims in the war, and they underline how the confrontation now spans the sea, the air and the diplomatic channel at the same time.

Iran has long threatened to close the Strait of Hormuz in response to attacks on its territory, and the waterway has been the scene of repeated confrontations. During the so-called tanker war of the 1980s, in the Iran-Iraq war, dozens of ships were attacked in the Gulf. In more recent years, vessels have been seized, sabotaged and struck by drones and missiles, and the United States and its allies have maintained a naval presence in the region to protect commercial shipping. The current round of attacks began in late February, when the US-Israeli war on Iran started, and it has made the strait the most dangerous stretch of water for merchant shipping anywhere in the world.

For the shipping industry, the consequences are practical and immediate. War-risk insurance premiums for transits through the strait have risen sharply, and some owners have rerouted cargoes or delayed sailings. LNG carriers are particularly sensitive, because their cargo is carried at very low temperatures in specialised tanks, and a fire on board an LNG carrier is among the most feared events in shipping. The fact that two oil tankers have now exploded and caught fire after striking mines, less than two weeks after an LNG carrier was hit the same way, is likely to sharpen concern among oil and gas buyers in Asia and Europe, who depend on Gulf supplies.

What happens next will depend on whether the attacks continue, on whether any ship is lost or any crew is killed, and on whether Washington answers Tehran's offer. The proposal conveyed through Qatar — reopening within 48 hours in exchange for lifting the blockade and ending the war on all fronts — sets a price on normal shipping that the United States has so far shown no sign of paying. Until it does, or until the attacks stop, the strait will remain the place where a regional war can most quickly become a global economic shock.

Why it matters: two tankers exploding and catching fire in the Strait of Hormuz is not a routine incident in an ongoing war — it is a direct hit on the channel that carries a fifth of the world's oil and the only sea route for Gulf gas, and it comes with Tehran openly conditioning the waterway's reopening on its demands. With at least 10 ships hit in the first 10 days of November and 37 in October, the campaign against shipping is intensifying, not fading, and every new mine strike raises the cost of moving energy through the Gulf.
