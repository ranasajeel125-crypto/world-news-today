Iran Fires Drones at US Radar in Bahrain; US Jets Strike Iranian Missile Site in Reply

world · 17 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The exchange ended a roughly two-month lull in fighting, with Bahrain saying its air defences intercepted the drones and a US official saying American jets struck a Revolutionary Guard missile site on Qeshm Island in self-defence.

Iran fired drones at a US military radar system in Bahrain on Tuesday, its third attack on American targets in the Gulf kingdom in five days, and the United States answered hours later with a fighter-jet strike on an Iranian missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, Reuters and the Associated Press reported.

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it had targeted the US military radar system at Bahrain’s Sheikh Isa Air Base, and warned Bahrain over its alliance with the United States. Bahrain’s Defence Force said its air defences had intercepted and destroyed the Iranian drones before they reached their targets. US Central Command said multiple Iranian one-way attack drones launched towards Bahrain “were intercepted without any damage to US equipment or injury to American personnel”.

Hours later, a US military official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said US fighter jets had fired on a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Qeshm Island, using precision-guided munitions. “The strike was in response to IRGC aggression targeting US personnel and assets,” the official said, describing the operation as self-defence. CBS News first reported the American strike, and Air & Space Forces Magazine confirmed it with a US military official. Qeshm Island sits in the Persian Gulf just outside the Strait of Hormuz, the world’s most important oil chokepoint.

Iran’s account of the American strike was narrower. Ahmad Nafisi, deputy governor of Hormozgan province, said the strike caused no damage to residential or commercial areas or to infrastructure, and Iranian media, citing an Iranian official, said the US attack did not damage infrastructure. Iran also claimed it had downed a US MQ-1 drone in the latest exchange, an assertion that has not been independently verified. The damage to the Qeshm missile site is unknown, and the number of drones launched towards Bahrain on Tuesday, and where they were intercepted, has not been established.

Tuesday’s attack followed a pattern that has built up over five days. On Friday, the Revolutionary Guard said it had targeted and destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain, in retaliation for what it described as an American attack on a communications tower in Sirik, on Iran’s southern coast near the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command rejected that account, saying American forces shot down two Iranian one-way attack drones launched toward Bahrain, with no injuries or damage. On Sunday, the Guard said six of its drones had targeted the US air-defence radar at the al-Riffa helicopter base; Bahrain said its air defences intercepted the attack, and sirens sounded across the capital, Manama.

The exchange interrupted a roughly two-month lull in fighting. The war began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, and the two sides have since been locked in a stand-off over the Strait of Hormuz. Bahrain hosts the headquarters of the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet, and the kingdom has now used its air defences in anger three times in five days. The US military has carried out what appear to be limited strikes on Qeshm Island and other Iranian targets at intervals this month, but Tuesday’s strike was the first direct American answer to an attack on Bahrain.

The fighting came despite diplomacy under way in the background. Talks between Iran and mediators Oman and Qatar were continuing, Reuters reported, and a Qatari-mediated meeting with Iran was set for Tuesday. But the political space for a deal has narrowed: President Donald Trump said last week that the United States was no longer interested in a deal and ordered Vice-President JD Vance, who had been leading efforts to restart negotiations, to turn his attention to domestic politics instead. Iranian negotiators had been discussing a proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if the US met seven conditions, reportedly including the lifting of the naval blockade and the release of frozen Iranian assets.

The strait itself has been a second front in the confrontation. Earlier this week, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard navy said two oil tankers struck mines and caught fire on a route south of the strait, and Iran’s regular navy said further tankers were hit in a restricted area east of it, while Tehran warned shipping against what it called “fake American routes”. The United States reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports after a preliminary June agreement to end the war collapsed within weeks, and October recorded 37 vessel attacks, the highest monthly total of the conflict, according to Lloyd’s List.

What happens next will turn on whether either side treats Tuesday’s exchange as the end of this round or the start of a wider one. Bahrain has now publicly accused Tehran of using Bahraini soil to settle its score with Washington, and its government has said it holds Iran fully responsible for the attacks and their consequences. Tehran, for its part, has warned that any further American action will be met with a harsher response, and has described its strikes as retaliation rather than escalation. With no talks scheduled and no deal on the table, the space for a miscalculation — a drone that gets through, a radar that is actually hit, a ship struck in the strait — is growing with each exchange.

Why it matters: for two months the confrontation was fought mostly at sea, through mines, seizures and a blockade. Tuesday put American military sites in Bahrain and Iranian missile positions beside the Strait of Hormuz into the same developing confrontation, and it did so in a single day. The competing accounts — Tehran claiming a radar targeted and a drone downed, Manama and Washington claiming every drone intercepted, and both sides giving different accounts of the damage on Qeshm — show how hard it has become to establish what is actually happening in a war fought increasingly through claims on Telegram and denials from military commands. Until independent evidence emerges, the one fact both sides agree on is the most important one: the drones are flying again, the jets are answering, and the lull is over.
