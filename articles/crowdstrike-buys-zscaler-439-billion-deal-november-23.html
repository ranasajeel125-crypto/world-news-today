CrowdStrike to Buy Zscaler in $43.9 Billion Deal, the Biggest Cybersecurity Takeover on Record

technology · 23 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Technology Desk)

CrowdStrike's $43.9 billion all-stock deal for Zscaler would create one of the world's largest pure-play cybersecurity providers, with more than $10 billion in combined annual recurring revenue.

CrowdStrike said on Monday it would buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal that values the company at about $43.9 billion, the biggest cybersecurity acquisition on record, uniting two of the industry's biggest names as advances in artificial intelligence fuel increasingly sophisticated cyber threats.

Under the terms announced on 23 November, Zscaler shareholders will receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each Zscaler share they own. The exchange ratio implies a value of about $260.41 per Zscaler share, representing a premium of about 34% to Zscaler's closing price on Friday. Existing CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84% of the combined company once the transaction closes, which is expected in the second half of 2027.

The market's first reaction was split. Zscaler shares jumped more than 20% in premarket trading to $235.50, but remained well below the deal's implied value of $260.41 per share. CrowdStrike shares fell nearly 7%. The gap between Zscaler's trading price and the value of the offer comes as investors weigh the prospects for regulatory scrutiny of a transaction that would combine two of the cybersecurity industry's biggest players.

That scrutiny is possible but far from certain to block the deal, according to analysts. "Antitrust scrutiny is certainly possible given the size of the transaction and the importance of both companies, but it would be premature to assume that regulators will necessarily try to block it," said Axel Rudolph, chief technical analyst at IG Group. "The key issue is likely to be how much overlap regulators see between the two businesses and whether customers would genuinely have fewer competitive alternatives as a result."

The deal brings together two complementary halves of corporate security. CrowdStrike specialises in protecting endpoints — computers, servers and other devices — and cloud workloads, while Zscaler secures employees' access to applications and data through its "zero-trust" architecture, which treats no user or device as trusted by default. The combination will create one of the world's largest pure-play cybersecurity providers, as enterprise customers seek to reduce the number of security vendors they use and favour providers that can deliver multiple products through a single relationship.

The business logic is as much about customers as it is about technology. The combined company is expected to start with more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue, including roughly $3.9 billion from Zscaler, and CrowdStrike expects the deal to generate about $1 billion in annualised revenue synergies within 30 months of closing. CrowdStrike said the deal would expand its addressable market by more than $185 billion. Just as important, it gives CrowdStrike access to roughly 8,000 Zscaler customers that do not currently use its products — a ready-made cross-selling audience that includes large companies such as Unilever and United Airlines.

George Kurtz, CrowdStrike's chief executive, will lead the combined company, while Zscaler chief executive Jay Chaudhry will stay on to run the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike's board. The companies will continue to operate separately until closing. The agreement carries a $1.4 billion breakup fee payable by Zscaler if it walks away under certain circumstances, including to accept a rival offer. Qatalyst Partners, JPMorgan and BofA Securities are advising CrowdStrike on the transaction, while BDT & MSD Partners, Citigroup and Goldman Sachs are advising Zscaler.

The deal was announced alongside strong quarterly results from both companies. CrowdStrike forecast fourth-quarter revenue of between $1.638 billion and $1.644 billion, above analysts' average estimate of $1.62 billion, according to data compiled by LSEG, and expects adjusted profit of 36 cents per share, in line with estimates. Third-quarter revenue rose 28% to $1.58 billion, helped by a 29% increase in subscription revenue, against analysts' expectations of $1.53 billion, and adjusted profit of 34 cents per share beat estimates of 31 cents. Zscaler, meanwhile, reported a 20% rise in first-quarter revenue to $949.3 million, beating estimates of $938 million, and adjusted profit of $1.23 per share topped estimates of $1.16.

The scale of the deal sets it apart even in a sector used to large transactions. At about $43.9 billion it eclipses Alphabet's $32 billion acquisition of Wiz in 2025, Cisco's $28 billion purchase of Splunk in 2023 and Palo Alto Networks' $25 billion deal for CyberArk in 2025 — the previous landmarks in Reuters' list of the largest cybersecurity deals. It is also the latest and by far the biggest step in a consolidation wave: companies are racing to build broader platforms offering multiple services, because customers increasingly want one supplier rather than a patchwork of specialist vendors.

CrowdStrike has been one of the most active buyers in that wave. The Zscaler deal is the eighth acquisition the company has announced since August of last year, and the third this month, according to Barron's — a pace that shows how aggressively the biggest vendors are moving to cover every part of a customer's security stack before a rival does. Buying Zscaler, rather than building a competing network-security business from scratch, gives CrowdStrike the zero-trust capability immediately, along with the customers and the revenue that come with it.

The timing reflects what is driving demand across the industry. Demand for cybersecurity tools has grown as enterprises worldwide confront increasingly sophisticated attacks, with advances in AI making it easier to identify and exploit software vulnerabilities while also improving the tools designed to detect and stop threats. Recent outages affecting airlines, banks and government agencies, along with high-profile vulnerabilities in widely used Java and PHP software, have further heightened concerns over the resilience of critical digital infrastructure — and the budgets to protect it.

Remarkably, the biggest cybersecurity deal in history was not even the biggest story on the day it was announced. It landed on the same Monday that Jensen Huang returned as chief executive of Nvidia after an 11-day absence, a drama that dominated Wall Street's attention and, as Barron's put it, left the CrowdStrike deal "lost in the noise". That the market could absorb a $44 billion takeover almost as a sideshow says something about the scale of the Nvidia story — and about how routine mega-deals have become in the technology sector.

Why it matters: if the deal clears regulatory review and closes in the second half of 2027 as planned, it will create a cybersecurity provider spanning endpoints, cloud workloads and network access, with more than $10 billion in recurring revenue and a customer list few rivals can match. The gap between Zscaler's share price and the offer value is the market's own verdict on the odds: investors are pricing in real doubt about approval, even if analysts think a block is premature to assume. For the industry, the message is that consolidation is accelerating and that the winners will be the vendors who can offer everything — and that the losers will be the specialists who cannot. For everyone else, it is a reminder that the companies guarding the world's digital infrastructure are themselves becoming very large, very fast, and very few.
