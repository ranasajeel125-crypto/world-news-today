Jordan Sentences Three Men to Death Over Deadly Karak Church Attack

world · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The attack on a Greek Orthodox church funeral in the village of Hamoud, Karak governorate, on 23 October killed nine mourners and wounded dozens.

Jordan’s State Security Court sentenced three men to death on Sunday for carrying out an attack on a church in the southern governorate of Karak last month that killed nine people, the state news agency Petra reported.

The court convicted the men of offences including carrying out terrorist acts resulting in death, possessing weapons and ammunition for terrorist purposes and promoting the ideology of a terrorist group. The ruling is subject to appeal and automatic review by the Court of Cassation, Petra said, so the sentences are not yet final.

Gunmen opened fire during a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in the village of Hamoud on 23 October, in a rare attack on Jordan’s Christian minority. Authorities said 34 people were also wounded. The first official toll on the day of the attack was seven dead; it rose to nine the following day as two of the wounded died, and Jordan’s health minister later put the number of injured at 40.

The victims were mourners gathered for a funeral at St George’s Greek Orthodox Church, about 120 kilometres south of Amman. Eight of the nine dead were buried together on 25 October, in a joint funeral that drew mourners from across the Christian community and beyond. The names of the dead published in Jordanian media were Alaa Masarweh, Ihab Halaseh, Khaled Halaseh, Marwan Al-Khetan, Maher Zuriqat, Assem Al-Burqan, Fadi Halaseh, Mazen Qussous and Mohammed Qussous.

Security forces arrested the three men on 26 October after a shootout at an abandoned house in the Karak area of al-Jadeeda. All three were wounded in the raid, which lasted about three hours, while no security personnel were injured, according to the Public Security Directorate. The director, Major General Abdullah al-Maaytah, named the suspects as Hazza Zaidan Mohammed, described as the planner, his brother Meshal Zaidan Mohammed, and Ahmad Sami Hussein, a relative by marriage.

No group had claimed responsibility for the attack in the days after it happened, and Jordanian officials did not publicly link the three men to a named militant organisation at the time of the arrests. The court’s conviction for promoting the ideology of a terrorist group is the first formal judicial finding tying the attack to extremist ideology. Two further defendants in the case each received six-year prison terms after one charge against them was reclassified as possession of a firearm, according to Amer Helsa, counsel for the victims’ families.

The attack sent shockwaves through Jordan, where Christians are a small minority and attacks on churches are rare. The United Nations Resident Coordinator in Jordan, Sheri Ritsema-Anderson, condemned the attack and said places of prayer must remain safe havens. The Council of Heads of Churches in Jordan announced the cancellation of Christmas celebrations this year in solidarity with the bereaved families, according to church news reports.

The case did not end at the border. Later on 26 October, the day of the arrests, Jordan launched airstrikes on southern Syria against groups it linked to the church attack. Jordan’s armed forces called the operation “Jordanian Deterrence – Hmoud” and said it was conducted in coordination with the Syrian government, targeting positions it said were associated with Islamic State militants. Syrian authorities said seven people, including three children and a woman, were killed in the strikes in Daraa province, and Damascus denied that the operation had been coordinated with it in advance.

Jordan later acknowledged that its airstrikes had killed civilians in Syria, and the two countries agreed to form a joint security committee to review the incident and its consequences. The dispute briefly strained relations between two governments that had been working to rebuild ties since the overthrow of former Syrian president Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, and to deepen security cooperation along their shared border.

On 9 November the two sides moved to mend the rift. Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shibani and Jordanian Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ayman Safadi met in Damascus, signed 11 cooperation agreements covering trade, transport, energy, investment and food security, and confirmed a joint security and technical fact-finding committee to investigate the circumstances of the airstrikes. Al-Shibani said Jordan had shown understanding and openness towards resolving the incident, and efforts were under way to address the consequences and provide compensation to the families of those killed.

What happens next now plays out on two tracks. In Amman, the death sentences go to the Court of Cassation, which must review them automatically, and the men can also appeal; until that process ends, no execution can take place. In Damascus and Amman, the joint committee’s findings on the 26 October strikes will test whether the two governments can agree on what happened in Daraa — and whether the compensation and cooperation deals signed on 9 November are enough to close a diplomatic wound that opened in a single violent week.

Why it matters: the Karak attack was a rare strike at Jordan’s Christian minority and a reminder that the extremist violence that has scarred the region for a decade has not gone away, even as Jordan has been one of the region’s most stable states and a close partner of the United States in the fight against extremism. The speed of the trial — arrests on 26 October, death sentences less than a month later — shows how seriously Amman took the attack, and the automatic review by the Court of Cassation is the safeguard built into Jordanian law for exactly these cases. But the airstrikes that followed the attack show the harder problem: a domestic atrocity with suspected cross-border links pulled Jordan into a military operation that killed civilians in a neighbouring country, strained a fragile new relationship with post-Assad Syria, and is still being investigated three weeks on. How the court review and the joint inquiry end will shape both Jordan’s standing at home and its border security with Syria.
