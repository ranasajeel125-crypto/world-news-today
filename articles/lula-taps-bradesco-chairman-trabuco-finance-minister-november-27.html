Lula Taps Bradesco Chairman Trabuco as Finance Minister and Brazil's Markets Cheer

business · 27 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Bradesco chairman Luiz Carlos Trabuco, whom President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has named as Brazil's next finance minister. Markets rallied on the news, with the real and the Bovespa both jumping on Friday.

Brazilian markets have rallied after President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva tapped a market-friendly bank executive to be his next finance chief, ending weeks of investor anxiety over the fiscal direction of the leftist leader's upcoming fourth term.

Bradesco chairman Luiz Carlos Trabuco, 75, accepted Lula's invitation on Thursday afternoon after travelling to Brasilia to meet the president, with whom he has shared a close relationship for years. The presidency confirmed the appointment later on Thursday, after a meeting of nearly two hours at the Alvorada presidential residence. Trabuco becomes the first cabinet member officially confirmed for Lula's next administration, and he will succeed Finance Minister Dario Durigan in January, following the presidential inauguration.

The market reaction was swift and emphatic. On Friday, the real strengthened as much as 4.7% to 5.47 per US dollar in spot trading before paring some gains to trade at 5.55, while the benchmark Bovespa stock index jumped as much as 3.9% before trimming gains. Interest rate futures fell sharply, and longer-dated international bonds rallied by around two cents on the dollar, broadly clawing back all the losses they had suffered since Lula's re-election. Latin American equities rose with Brazil: the MSCI Latin America index gained 3.2% on Friday, extending the real's rally to a fifth straight session.

In truth, the rally began before the announcement was official. On Thursday, even before the government confirmed Trabuco's nomination, the Bovespa closed 6% higher and the real strengthened 1.2% on reports that the two men were meeting. Markets had already responded positively earlier in the week when rumours of Lula's interest in Trabuco started circulating, and Trabuco had emerged as the front-runner as early as Monday. Investors, in other words, had already priced in the appointment — and then cheered it again once it was confirmed.

The scale of the reaction says as much about the weeks before it as about the appointment itself. Brazil's financial markets have deteriorated since Lula beat right-wing rival Flavio Bolsonaro in October's presidential election, pushing the leftist leader to court a more market-friendly figure to head the finance ministry and ease investor fears. Since his surprise election victory on 25 October, Lula has pledged fiscal responsibility but repeatedly dismissed market criticism, saying financiers would not dictate how he governs Brazil. That tension — between a president elected on promises of more social spending and investors worried about the public finances — has been the defining economic story of the weeks since the vote.

Trabuco is, by any measure, a heavyweight choice. Bradesco is Brazil's second-largest private lender and its fourth-largest bank by assets. Trabuco joined the bank as a clerk at 17 years old and spent more than four decades rising through its ranks before being appointed chief executive in 2009, a position he held until 2018. His tenure at the helm was marked by the acquisition of HSBC's Brazilian operations in 2015, one of the biggest deals in the country's banking history. He is a philosophy graduate of the University of São Paulo with a postgraduate degree in socio-psychology, and he has long maintained an open channel of communication with Lula — he is widely regarded as one of the president's most trusted interlocutors in the financial sector.

That combination — a career banker who is also personally close to the president — is precisely what made him attractive to both sides of the bargain. For Lula, Trabuco is a figure he trusts and can work with. For investors, he is a figure whose reputation in the financial community is beyond doubt. “Given his strong reputation within the financial community and longstanding experience in the banking sector, the appointment could be interpreted as a signal of a somewhat more market-friendly approach,” analysts at Citi said in a note after the announcement.

Other names had been considered for the post. Vice President Geraldo Alckmin reportedly told allies he would like the job, and Rafael Fonteles, who was re-elected governor of Piaui state, was also in the running. That Lula passed over his own vice president in favour of a private-sector banker underlines how seriously he took the market's message — and how much he wanted the appointment itself to do the talking.

The job Trabuco is walking into is a difficult one. His most urgent task as minister will be to convince investors he can slow the rise in public debt, which now stands near 83% of gross domestic product and has risen about 11 percentage points since the start of Lula's third term. Fiscal worries since the re-election, after Lula campaigned on boosting social spending, have weakened the real. High official interest rates, at 13.75%, add to the pressure on the government to turn around the fiscal situation, because every point of borrowing cost makes the debt burden heavier to carry.

The government has already announced fiscal measures targeting savings of 0.5% of GDP in 2027, but analysts argue that further spending cuts are needed to stabilise debt and borrowing costs. Treasury Secretary Daniel Leal said on Friday that Trabuco is expected to have a say in discussions over additional fiscal measures currently under consideration — a sign that the new minister will be involved in shaping the fiscal package before he formally takes office in January, rather than simply inheriting it.

Economist Roberto Troster, a partner at consultancy Troster & Associados, expects Trabuco to take on the hardest questions. “I believe Trabuco will focus on long-term fiscal sustainability, which means tackling some bitter issues, including further pension reform and revisiting indexation mechanisms,” he said. Pension reform and the automatic indexation of government spending to inflation are among the most politically sensitive subjects in Brazilian politics, and neither has been tackled in a serious way during Lula's third term. Whether Trabuco can move them — and whether Lula will let him — is the question on which the market's early enthusiasm will ultimately be judged.

That judgement will not come quickly. A finance minister's credibility is built over months of budgets, fiscal targets and delivery, not in a single day of market moves. The rally of Thursday and Friday is a vote of confidence in the signal the appointment sends, not a verdict on the policies that will follow. Trabuco has not yet set out a programme, and the additional fiscal measures under discussion have not yet been announced. What investors have bought, for now, is the idea that a banker they respect will be in the room when those decisions are made.

The appointment also matters beyond Brazil. Latin America's largest economy is closely watched by investors across the region, and the real's slide since the election had weighed on neighbouring currencies and on regional equity markets. Friday's rebound, which lifted the MSCI Latin America index more than 3%, showed how quickly sentiment can turn when the region's biggest economy offers reassurance — and how quickly it can turn back if that reassurance proves hollow.

Why it matters: a finance minister is the single most consequential appointment of any Brazilian presidency, and Lula has used his first confirmed pick of the fourth term to tell markets he has heard them. The choice of a veteran banker he personally trusts is a deliberate bridge between a president who campaigned on social spending and investors who feared the bill. The markets have answered with one of the sharpest rallies of the year. The harder test starts in January, when Trabuco must turn a signal into a fiscal plan — with public debt near 83% of GDP, interest rates at 13.75%, and a president who has said financiers will not dictate how he governs.
