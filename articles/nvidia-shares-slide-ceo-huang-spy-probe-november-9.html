Nvidia Shares Slide as Board Probes CEO Huang Over Spy Claims

technology · 9 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Huang told staff he had been unfaithful to his wife; the board says it has no reason to believe trade secrets were compromised.

Nvidia shares tumbled on Monday after the chipmaker said its board had opened an independent investigation into whether chief executive Jensen Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort, putting the future of the world's most valuable chipmaker under scrutiny.

The shares fell around 8 per cent in early trading, extending last week's sell-off and pushing the stock to fresh multi-month lows, before paring losses to about 6 per cent. At the early lows, the company stood to lose around 350 billion dollars in market value from Friday's closing price of 186.64 dollars, Reuters reported. The stock closed the session at 178.67 dollars, down 4.27 per cent on the day, after falling as far as 170.24 dollars intraday. Shares in the AI chip leader were roughly flat for the year so far, but off about 22 per cent from their peak in October, when the stock hit an intraday record high above 243 dollars.

The probe, announced on Sunday, followed a New York Times report in late October that Huang had a close relationship with Alice Huang, an unrelated former executive of Singapore-based company Megaspeed, who US authorities investigated and concluded was a Chinese intelligence asset. The Times reported, citing people briefed on American intelligence assessments, that officials believed Alice Huang had been recruited by China's intelligence services and had cultivated an intimate relationship with the Nvidia chief. The two were photographed together in Taipei in June 2024. Huang told Nvidia employees he had been unfaithful to his wife, without disclosing details of the relationship, and in a memorandum made public on Sunday he apologised to his family and employees.

Nvidia said a committee of independent directors would oversee the investigation with the assistance of independent outside legal counsel. The board said it had no reason to believe any trade secrets had been compromised, and in its statement it said it had no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, while expressing confidence in the chief executive's leadership. "I support the investigation and will cooperate fully," Huang said in his statement, and the company said he intended to remain chief executive throughout the investigation. The board later retained former acting US Attorney for the Southern District of New York Joon H. Kim and his firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen and Katz to conduct the inquiry.

The affair sits inside a much larger story about the control of advanced computer chips. Megaspeed, the Singapore data-centre company where Alice Huang was an executive, bought more than 4 billion dollars in Nvidia hardware, and reports put the figure at between 4.6 and 4.8 billion dollars in Nvidia technology imported between 2023 and 2025. According to the Times investigation, Megaspeed rented Nvidia chips at Southeast Asian facilities to Chinese firms such as Alibaba, and was suspected of physically moving restricted chips into China, which would breach US export controls. The Justice Department has taken over a Commerce Department probe into whether Megaspeed helped Chinese companies evade US restrictions, and is leading multiple investigations involving Alice Huang. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, and no charges have been filed.

China's foreign ministry rejected the espionage allegations, calling them a groundless accusation and a smear, Reuters reported. Beijing has denied that Alice Huang is a spy. The Times reported that US intelligence briefed President Donald Trump roughly a year ago on Alice Huang's ties to Jensen Huang and that the president took no action, and that Jensen Huang himself was not briefed. Analysts have noted that the central judgement rests on anonymous sources describing classified assessments, which is an intelligence view rather than a judicial finding, and that it is unknown whether she obtained or passed on any critical information.

Congress moved quickly. On 5 November, three Republican chairs of US House of Representatives committees requested that Huang attend a closed-door briefing on 18 November. The scandal has heightened concerns in Washington that third-country companies could route AI chips to China, and lawmakers have pressed for stronger guardrails on export restrictions. Documents later released by the House Foreign Affairs Committee showed Nvidia had sought licences to sell tens of thousands of advanced AI semiconductors to companies linked to Alice Huang, including an application in April to supply 40,800 H200 chips to a Megaspeed affiliate.

Huang, 63, has become the most prominent salesman of the artificial intelligence boom, with Nvidia's value rising from about 420 billion dollars in late 2022 to 4.5 trillion dollars as of 6 November, making it one of only three US companies valued at over 4 trillion dollars. Its next milestone is its fiscal third-quarter results call on 17 November, while shares in key Asian suppliers including TSMC rebounded on Monday after last week's sell-off. Prediction markets moved sharply on the question of whether the affair might cost Huang his job, with bettors on one platform putting roughly 32 per cent odds on him being out by the end of June.

The story did not stop on Monday. On 11 November, the New York Times reported that Huang had an extramarital relationship with Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reports directly to him, and the following day the company announced that Huang had resigned. The board was reported to be preparing to meet to determine his future after the Wall Street Journal reported it was considering ousting him, and shares plunged almost 10 per cent in extended trading on Wednesday. Colette Kress was named interim chief executive, and Trump, who has supported Huang throughout the fallout, later called for his reinstatement. What began as a board investigation into a possible foreign intelligence operation had become a test of how far US export controls on advanced AI chips depend on the companies that sell them.
