Russian Warship Capsizes and Sinks After Suspected Drone Attack at Benghazi Port in Libya

world · 22 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

A Russian Navy corvette docked at the Benghazi naval base in eastern Libya capsized and sank after a suspected drone attack on Sunday, satellite imagery showed, in the second suspected drone strike on a Russian vessel in two days.

An explosion aboard a Russian ship docked at the Benghazi naval base in eastern Libya was caused by a suspected drone attack, Libya’s eastern-based government said on Sunday after a preliminary investigation. Satellite imagery taken later in the day showed that most of the vessel had capsized and sunk, with only its bow still visible above the water.

The vessel was the Merkury, a Russian Navy corvette — a small warship — according to satellite imagery analysed by the provider Vantor, which said smoke was still rising from part of the ship’s superstructure and significant debris was scattered along the pier. Ten people, including Russians and Libyans, were slightly injured in the incident and were receiving treatment in Benghazi, medical sources said. No deaths had been reported.

Libya’s eastern-based government said the ship had stopped at Benghazi port to refuel and resupply on its way back to Russia, under maritime cooperation agreements between the two countries. It said port and naval base facilities, including warehouses, were safe, and that normal operations at the port had resumed after the fire was brought under control and the site secured.

The government condemned the suspected attack as a violation of Libya’s sovereignty and a threat to maritime safety, saying it would take legal and diplomatic measures against anyone found responsible. “The Libyan government will not allow Libyan ports, territory or territorial waters to be turned into an arena for settling scores or exporting international conflicts,” it said in a statement.

There was no immediate reaction from the Russian authorities, and no one had claimed responsibility for the suspected attack. Who launched the drones, and from where, remains unknown — the eastern government’s statement described only the outcome of its preliminary investigation, not the identity of the attackers.

The attack did not end with the warship. Later on Sunday, a commercial vessel flying the Maltese flag was hit by a drone boat off the coast of Benghazi, two Libyan military sources told Reuters. Libyan naval forces rescued the vessel’s crew, and the health ministry said some crew members were in stable condition after sustaining minor injuries. Libyan naval and air forces also intercepted more than 20 drone boats off the coast of Benghazi on Sunday evening, the sources said.

The Benghazi attack was the second suspected drone strike on a Russian vessel in two days. On Saturday, the Russian cargo ship Sparta II was hit by drones and caught fire in the Gulf of Finland while bound for the Russian exclave of Kaliningrad, drifting into Finnish waters, in what Finland’s military said was likely an attack by Ukraine. The 16-member crew evacuated onto lifeboats and then onto a nearby Russian ship. The Sparta II, a 121-metre Russian-flagged cargo ship owned by Oboronlogistika, a company that provides logistics services for the Russian Defence Ministry, is subject to United States sanctions.

The two attacks, in two different seas and barely a day apart, point to a widening campaign against Russian shipping. The Gulf of Finland strike hit a civilian cargo vessel on a routine Baltic run; the Benghazi strike hit a naval warship at anchor in a foreign military port, and was followed within hours by a drone-boat attack on a commercial ship and the interception of more than 20 further drone boats. The pattern suggests an attacker able to operate uncrewed craft across the Mediterranean as well as the Baltic — though no evidence has yet been published linking the two incidents, and no one has claimed either of them.

The attack took place in territory held by Libya’s eastern-based government, which sits in Benghazi, while the country’s other government sits in Tripoli — a division that has defined Libyan politics since the civil war. Russia has built close ties with the eastern Libyan authorities in recent years, including military cooperation, and the maritime agreements under which the Merkury stopped at Benghazi are part of that relationship. A Russian warship using a Libyan naval base as a refuelling stop, and then being sunk there, puts the eastern authorities in an uncomfortable position: their port has become a target, and their statement made clear they do not want Libya drawn into other countries’ conflicts.

What happens next will be decided by the investigation the eastern government says it has begun, and by whether Moscow responds. Russia has said nothing so far about the loss of one of its warships, and it is not clear how many of the Merkury’s crew were among the ten injured, or what the vessel was carrying. The questions that matter most — who launched the drones, whether they were flown from the air or sailed in as drone boats, and whether more attacks are planned — have no public answers yet. Shipping off the Libyan coast, meanwhile, is now on notice: a Maltese-flagged commercial vessel was attacked on the same day, and the Libyan navy spent Sunday evening intercepting drone boats.

Why it matters: a Russian Navy warship sunk in a foreign port is a rare and serious event — warships are not meant to be lost at anchor in peacetime, and the sinking of the Merkury, a day after the Sparta II was set ablaze in the Baltic, suggests Russian vessels are now being hunted in more than one sea. For Libya, the attack is a warning that its ports can be turned into a battlefield by conflicts that are not its own, which is exactly what the eastern government’s statement said it would not allow. For the wider Mediterranean, the drone-boat attack on a commercial ship and the swarm of more than 20 intercepted craft show that the threat is not limited to military targets. Until someone claims the attack or evidence emerges about who carried it out, the sinking will hang over both the war in Ukraine and the fragile politics of Libya — two conflicts that, on Sunday, collided in a single harbour.
