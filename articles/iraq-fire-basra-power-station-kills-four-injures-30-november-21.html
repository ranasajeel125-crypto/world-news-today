Fire at Basra Power Station Kills at Least Four and Injures 30, With Toll Expected to Rise

world · 21 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

A fire at the Hartha power station north of Basra killed at least four people and injured 30, with the toll expected to rise.

At least four people were killed and 30 injured in a fire at a power station in southern Iraq on Saturday, security officials said, with the toll expected to rise. Civil defence teams brought the fire at the Hartha power station, north of the city of Basra, under control after firefighters responded to the incident, Iraq's state news agency, INA, reported.

The fire spread to neighbouring houses and forced operations at the power station to stop, the security officials said. The Hartha station has an installed generating capacity of 800 megawatts but had been operating at around 400 megawatts before the fire, they said.

There was no immediate official information on the cause of the fire or the extent of the damage, INA reported. The absence of an official account in the first hours left the trigger unclear, and officials did not say when the station might return to service or how long repairs could take.

The spread of the fire to neighbouring houses underlines that the danger was not confined to the plant itself. Homes close to large industrial sites are exposed when a blaze breaks out, and the damage to those houses will form part of the assessment now under way. The casualty figures are expected to change as rescue teams complete their work and hospitals report on the injured.

Losing around 400 megawatts of generation at Hartha is a significant blow to electricity supply in the south. The station was supplying about half of its installed capacity before the fire, so its shutdown removes hundreds of megawatts from a system that already struggles to meet demand. Even a partial loss of that size can mean longer power cuts for households and businesses in Basra and the surrounding areas while engineers assess the damage.

Iraq's electricity infrastructure has long struggled with decades of underinvestment, inadequate maintenance and an inability to meet peak demand, particularly during the extremely hot summer months. Fires and technical failures at substations and power facilities have previously disrupted electricity supplies and caused widespread outages.

The strain on the grid is the product of a long history. Iraq's power sector was damaged by war and sanctions, and the years of conflict that followed the 2003 invasion left plants and transmission networks in poor repair, with investment lagging far behind the growth in demand. Rebuilding has been slow and uneven, and the gap between what Iraqis need and what the grid can deliver has become one of the country's most persistent problems.

Basra, Iraq's second-largest city and its main port, sits at the heart of the country's oil-producing south. The city and the surrounding province host much of Iraq's oil production and export infrastructure, and the Hartha station is one of the plants feeding power into a grid that serves homes, hospitals and industry across the region.

In summer, temperatures in Basra regularly climb above 50 degrees Celsius, and demand for electricity surges as residents run air conditioning around the clock. The grid has repeatedly failed to keep up, and power cuts are a familiar part of daily life in the south — a grievance that has fuelled street protests in Basra in past years, including the unrest of 2018, when demonstrators demanded reliable electricity and clean water.

To plug the gap, Iraq still imports electricity and gas from neighbouring Iran, a dependence that has itself become a recurring political issue. The imports help keep the lights on, but they do not fix the underlying weakness of Iraq's own plants and networks — and every fire or breakdown at a domestic station, like the one at Hartha, exposes how thin the margin has become.

Attention will now turn to what started the fire and how much of the plant was damaged. Officials have not said whether the blaze began in the generating units, in fuel or electrical systems, or elsewhere on the site — and until engineers can inspect the plant, the scale of the damage and the time needed for repairs will remain unknown.

The incident is the latest in a series of fires and breakdowns at Iraqi power facilities. Such failures have previously disrupted supplies and caused widespread outages, a reminder that the country's energy problems are not only about how much power it can generate, but about keeping ageing plants and networks running safely.

Why it matters: a fire that kills four people, injures 30 and knocks a major power station out of service in Iraq's oil heartland is a human tragedy and an energy shock at once. The toll is expected to rise, the cause is unknown, and the station's 400 megawatts are now missing from a grid that was already stretched. For the people of Basra, the immediate question is how long the power stays off — and the longer question, familiar across Iraq, is when the country's electricity system will finally be rebuilt to match the demand placed on it.
