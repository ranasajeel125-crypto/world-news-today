Trump Backs Iraq's Prime Minister in His High-Stakes Crackdown on Oil Smuggling, Militias and Kurdish Autonomy

politics · 27 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, a 41-year-old businessman and political novice who came to power in a compromise six months ago, has ordered federal forces to take control of border crossings in the Kurdistan region.

United States President Donald Trump on Friday backed the Iraqi leader's drive to assert state control across the country, including stopping the smuggling of Iraqi oil, in a public endorsement that puts Washington squarely behind a crackdown that has set Baghdad against two of the most powerful forces in Iraq at once: the Iran-aligned militias and the Kurdish regional government.

“We support Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi’s efforts to assert state authority throughout Iraq, stop the smuggling of Iraqi oil, end corruption, and bring all weapons under the exclusive control of the state,” Trump said in a Truth Social post. The message landed shortly after the Iraqi government made its own case in public: government spokesman Haider al-Aboudi told the Iraqi state news agency that Baghdad considered crude oil exports that bypass the state oil marketer, SOMO, to be smuggling, because the proceeds do not reach the state treasury.

Aboudi put a number on the loss. Speaking to the state news agency, he said Iraq had lost an estimated $100 billion to oil smuggling through its Kurdistan region between 2008 and 2025. Reuters noted that it was unclear how the government had calculated the figure — a caveat worth keeping in mind, because the number is now the centrepiece of Baghdad's argument that the crackdown is not a power grab but a rescue of the public purse. Either way, the stakes are enormous: Iraq is one of the world's leading oil producers, and it is hoping to attract wide-scale foreign investment into an economy that has struggled for years.

The confrontation moved fast this week. On Thursday, Iraq closed two border crossings in the Kurdistan region, one with Syria and one with Iran, after Zaidi ordered federal authorities to take control of crossings run by the semi-autonomous regional government. Thousands of Kurds protested on Thursday against Zaidi's moves to put oilfields under federal control and to deploy federal troops along the region's international borders.

The legal groundwork had been laid a day earlier. Iraq's highest court ruled on Wednesday that Baghdad can deploy federal forces along all international borders, including in the Kurdistan region. The decision appears to be a victory for Zaidi, a 41-year-old multimillionaire businessman and political novice who came to power in a compromise just six months ago. But political analysts quoted by Reuters cautioned that it may yet prove difficult to implement measures that challenge entrenched interests — a warning that applies to almost everything Zaidi is attempting.

The Kurdish front is only half the story. The moves against the Kurds came just a week after Zaidi ordered a showdown against a heavily armed Shi'ite militia aligned with Iran, Kataib Hezbollah, putting him in confrontation with two major factions in a country where armed groups have amassed power over decades of ethno-sectarian civil war. Both crackdowns have been linked to alleged oil-smuggling rings, which is how Baghdad frames them: not as sectarian or ethnic score-settling, but as a single campaign against the theft of the country's oil.

In the militia standoff, the prime minister ordered the army last week to prepare to enter Jurf al-Sakhar, a town south of Baghdad where the government accuses Kataib Hezbollah of running a smuggling operation. The group was initially defiant but agreed to let soldiers enter the city. That climb-down, if it holds, is a notable moment: a militia that has long operated as a state within a state allowed the state's army through its gates. Whether the soldiers find what Baghdad says is there, and whether they stay, will be the real test.

The Kurdish response has been sharper in tone. The Kurdistan Regional Government on Wednesday rejected what it called the “military language and threats” used in a statement from the spokesman for the Iraqi military announcing the new steps. But it also said it was working with federal authorities to combat corruption and called for an end to oil smuggling across Iraq — language that leaves room for a deal even while protesting the method. Ties between Baghdad and the Kurds have often been strained over control of oil, money, territory and trade. The central government responded with force when Kurdistan held an independence referendum in 2017, though relations have lately grown calmer.

That history is why this week's moves carry such risk. The referendum crisis of 2017 ended with Baghdad retaking disputed territory by force, and the memory of it still shapes Kurdish politics. Zaidi is betting that the court ruling, the anti-corruption framing and now Trump's public backing give him cover that his predecessors did not have. The protests on Thursday, and the regional government's complaint about “military language”, are the first signs of how far that cover will stretch.

Trump's endorsement matters because Iraq sits in an almost impossible position between the United States and Iran. It is an ally of both, and both have armed friends inside the country. A prime minister who takes on an Iran-aligned militia and the Kurds in the same fortnight is taking on the two groups with the most to lose from a stronger central state — and the most capacity to fight back. Washington's public support raises the political cost for anyone who defies him, but it also ties Zaidi's fortunes to an American president whose own regional policy is in flux.

The timing adds another layer. The crackdown comes as the wider Middle East is in the middle of a war involving Iran: at their Mar-a-Lago meeting on Wednesday, Trump and NATO chief Mark Rutte discussed the US-Israeli war on Iran as one of their primary subjects, and the Houthis struck Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery on Thursday, killing a worker, in the latest attack on Gulf energy infrastructure. Iraq's oil, its borders and its militias are not a sideshow to that war — they are part of the same map, and a Baghdad that controls its own oil and its own borders is a different player in it.

What happens next will be decided on the ground, not in court rulings or social media posts. The first question is whether federal forces actually take control of the two closed crossings and hold them, or whether the closure becomes a bargaining chip in a negotiation with Erbil. The second is whether the army's entry into Jurf al-Sakhar produces arrests, seizures or evidence of the smuggling operation Baghdad alleges — or whether it fades into a face-saving compromise. The third is whether the Kurdish protests grow, and whether the regional government escalates from words about “military language” to action.

There is also the question of what Trump's backing is worth in practice. The post commits the United States to supporting Zaidi's efforts, but it announces no new American action, no aid and no deployment. Its value is political: a signal to Baghdad's rivals, to investors watching Iraq's struggling economy, and to Zaidi himself that he is not alone. For a 41-year-old political novice who reached the premiership through a compromise six months ago, that signal may be the most valuable thing he has received so far — and the most dangerous, if it encourages him to push further than the forces arrayed against him will tolerate.

Why it matters: Iraq has lived for two decades with armed groups, regional governments and smuggling networks that operate beyond the state's reach, and every prime minister has promised to bring them to heel. Zaidi is now trying to do it on two fronts at once, with a court ruling in his pocket and the American president's public backing behind him. If he succeeds, Iraq keeps more of its oil wealth, attracts the foreign investment it desperately wants, and becomes a state in the full sense of the word. If he fails, he will have united the country's two most powerful factions against a government that is only six months old. The next few days — at the border crossings, in Jurf al-Sakhar, and on the streets of Kurdistan — will show which way it goes.
