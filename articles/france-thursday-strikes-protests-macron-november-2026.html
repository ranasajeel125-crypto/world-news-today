Hundreds of Thousands March Across France as Thursday Strikes Test Macron

world · 5 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The mobilisation, called by trade unions including the left-wing CGT and backed by student groups, follows demonstrations that triggered the worst civil unrest France has seen in years.

Hundreds of thousands of protesters marched through French cities and public workers went on strike on Thursday, in a fresh test for President Emmanuel Macron after weeks of unrest that have rattled his government and dominated the agenda five months ahead of a presidential election, Reuters reported.

The mobilisation was called by trade unions including the left-wing CGT and backed by student groups, after demonstrations over living costs, public services and education triggered the worst civil unrest France has seen in years. Five unions called the strike, uniting workers across the public and private sectors to demand higher wages and better public services. Authorities deployed around 70,000 police nationwide, BFM TV reported, and the domestic intelligence service expected between 500,000 and 700,000 people to join demonstrations outside Paris. A total of 220 demonstrations were planned across the country on Thursday, and some counts put the number higher.

"We are extremely vigilant because it is out of the question to relive October 17," government spokeswoman Maud Bregeon told a press conference after Wednesday's cabinet meeting, referring to the day last month when riots and looting caught security forces off guard, leaving damaged shops and public buildings across the country.

The Education Ministry said 17.5% of public primary school teachers were expected to strike, while unions warned of likely disruption to rail and public transport services. Four main rail unions at the state operator SNCF were on strike, causing major disruption on regional and Paris-area lines, though high-speed TGV services were less affected. In Paris, the RATP network expected normal metro, bus and tram service, except for the RER B line, which ran at reduced frequency. French power unions also joined Thursday's strike, state-owned utility EDF said; a previous power sector strike in mid-September reduced electricity supply in the country and cut delivery capacity at France's biggest liquefied natural gas terminal, Dunkirk LNG. Dockworkers affiliated with the CGT planned a strike at all French ports, air traffic controllers had filed strike notices, hospital staff in the Paris region walked out over budget cuts, and postal workers joined, with the CGT announcing an open-ended postal strike from 5 November.

The unrest began in the classroom. On 17 September, teachers at the Antoine de Saint-Exupery High School in Creteil, a southeastern suburb of Paris, went on strike to draw attention to staff shortages. High schoolers in Lyon, Marseille, Paris and other major French cities followed, barricading their schools and protesting in the streets against the lack of teachers, overcrowded classrooms, overpacked schedules and their schools' crumbling infrastructure. They also called for an overhaul of the university admissions platform, Parcoursup, saying it is too complex and opaque. At the beginning of the 2026-2027 academic year, more than half of France's high schools and middle schools lacked at least one teacher. The protests eventually affected more than 1,000 of France's approximately 3,700 high schools, and about 90% of universities were blockaded at one point, according to CGTN.

As the demonstrations grew, their focus expanded beyond the school system to include concerns about affordability, diesel prices and general dissatisfaction with the government. The movement brought together students with trade unions such as the General Confederation of Labor, and the Yellow Vests, the grassroots movement that led anti-government protests in 2018. The government's proposed 2027 budget, which includes a 54 billion euro package of cost-cutting and tax measures, became a further target. On 17 October, about 550,000 people demonstrated nationwide, according to the Interior Ministry. Most marched peacefully, while a small minority attacked buildings, looted shops and clashed with police, who fired tear gas. The historic town hall in Limoges was largely destroyed by fire. Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said 1,200 people were arrested and 150 police officers were injured. Macron addressed the nation that Sunday, saying: "Those who have destroyed property must pay. Those who have looted must reimburse their victims. Those who sought to bring down the republic will be held accountable."

Macron convened a defence and security meeting on Wednesday ahead of the protests, underlining how a movement that began with student anger over deteriorating school conditions has evolved into a broader challenge to his government. "The goal in the coming weeks is to get the river that overflowed back into its bed and to launch the presidential election in the calmest possible conditions," an official close to Macron said. Lecornu has been meeting with unions and student groups, but passing the budget through a deeply divided parliament leaves very little room to address protesters' demands.

Among those marching on Thursday were students who say conditions in schools and growing uncertainty over their future remain major concerns. "We're trying to keep mobilising as much as possible. Some people aren't doing so because they're afraid. A lot of people are afraid of police violence," said Irma de Banville, a 19-year-old student in Paris. The French government has defended police tactics while saying any abuses would be investigated. In Lille, a day that began peacefully descended into clashes, with police dispersing demonstrators with tear gas, and there were confrontations in Paris and Lyon.

Nationwide protests last Saturday were generally peaceful, with some clashes on the fringes. A grassroots protest movement backed by some unions is set to hold a new round of demonstrations on Saturday, as it has done weekly since the October 17 protest, and some unions and housing rights associations have called for pan-European protests from November 20 to November 22.

The political stakes are high. France's presidential election is scheduled for April 2027, and Macron, who has served two terms, is barred by the constitution from running again. The unrest has sharpened political divisions, with parties seeking to present themselves as the answer to the frustration visible on the streets. The populist-left leader Jean-Luc Melenchon has embraced the student movement, and according to an OpinionWay poll for Les Echos, he leads among voters aged 18 to 24 with 49 percent support, a 10-percentage-point increase compared with 2022. Far-right leader Marine Le Pen has focused on the disorder, framing it as a crisis of security and authority, and remains the leading contender in the race overall. Lecornu has accused the extreme left of instigating violence as a "warm-up round" for the presidential election. Analysts warn that the scenes of violent riots risk helping the far right's law-and-order message, while the far left, popular among young voters, could also benefit — setting up a contest between the two extremes, with the centre that Macron represents under pressure from both sides. Whoever wins the election will inherit a stack of problems: underfunded schools, strained public services, a contested budget and a country where the street has once again become a central political force.
