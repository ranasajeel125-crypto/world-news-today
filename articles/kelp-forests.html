Over 60% of World's Kelp Forests Are Degraded, UN Biodiversity Report Warns

climate · 24 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

Kelp forests shelter a quarter of marine species — and they are disappearing faster than rainforests.

Over 60 per cent of the world's assessed kelp forests are degraded, according to the second edition of the State of the World's Kelp Forests report, published by the Kelp Forest Alliance and launched at the UN biodiversity summit, COP17, in Yerevan, Armenia. The report draws on global datasets, expert assessments and case studies, and finds that despite growing efforts to protect and restore these underwater forests, most of them still show some degree of degradation.

Kelp forests fringe nearly a third of the world's coastlines, and about 950 million people live within 50 kilometres of one. They are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, sheltering fisheries, protecting coastlines from storms and locking away carbon, yet they receive a fraction of the attention given to coral reefs or rainforests, partly because their collapse happens out of sight, underwater. The report puts the potential value of their ecosystem services at more than 500 billion US dollars a year, and estimates they draw down about 32 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, although the authors stress there is wide uncertainty around that figure.

The decline has been long and widespread. Roughly 40 to 60 per cent of kelp forests have experienced reductions in abundance over the past half century, and 61 per cent of long-term datasets spanning more than twenty years show significant declines, with only 5 per cent showing increases. The best available estimate puts the global rate of decline at about 1.8 per cent a year, which across a worldwide extent of about 641,000 square kilometres works out at roughly 11,500 square kilometres of forest lost in a single year.

Experts surveyed for the report rank ocean warming, including marine heatwaves, as the biggest threat, followed by overgrazing and then physical disturbances such as harvesting, coastal development and sediment. The mechanism is often a cascade: warming seas and the loss of predators such as sea otters and large fish allow sea-urchin populations to explode, and the urchins graze kelp down to bare rock "barrens" that can persist for decades. Marine heatwaves then finish off weakened forests faster than they can regrow.

The pattern is visible in well-studied places. In Tasmania, warming waters contributed to the collapse of roughly 95 per cent of giant kelp forests over recent decades, as long-spined sea urchins arriving from warmer northern waters stripped reefs bare. In northern California, bull kelp canopy area fell by more than 95 per cent during the unprecedented 2014-2016 marine heatwave, when unusually warm water, sea star wasting disease and a rapid expansion in sea-urchin populations combined, and it has yet to recover. Research along the coast of Baja California in Mexico found one area shifting to an urchin-barren state after herbivore densities rose by more than 2,400 per cent between 2016 and 2023, with giant kelp canopy down 95 per cent.

When a forest dies, the carbon stored in its tissue goes into the water instead. Kelp grows in shallow, well-mixed water where gas moves quickly between sea and air, and about half of that carbon can reach the atmosphere. What replaces the forest, turf algae or a rocky barren grazed bare by urchins, either stores less carbon or releases carbon dioxide of its own. Warmth also does damage well short of killing anything: where kelp is near its temperature limit, growth slows and the plants take up less carbon, while warmth speeds grazing and decay, so less of it reaches deep water.

Protection remains thin. Although 23 per cent of the global kelp biome overlaps protected areas, only 1.2 per cent is confidently classified as highly protected, and less than 2 per cent of kelp forests sit inside fully protected areas. Restoring a hectare of kelp costs between 10,000 and 707,000 dollars, while protecting the same area inside a managed and enforced marine reserve costs between 650 and 1,300 dollars, which is one reason scientists argue that protection is far cheaper than repair.

Restoration does work where it is tried, but it is dwarfed by the losses. The report documents 19,071 hectares of successful kelp restoration, and the largest verified projects took years of sustained effort: a team in northern Norway killed off urchins on 70 hectares with quicklime over four years, while volunteers in Santa Monica Bay in California spent twelve years pulling about six million urchins off 32 hectares. One recent comparison of the carbon won back by restoration against what warming seas are taking away found the losses running 1,000 to 10,000 times ahead of the gains, and projected a loss of 25 million hectares by 2050, close to the size of Oregon.

Scientists at the talks argued that kelp deserves a place in national climate and biodiversity plans, with protection, urchin management and restoration planting all shown to work where they are tried. The Kelp Forest Alliance's global Kelp Forest Challenge calls for the protection of three million hectares and the restoration of one million hectares of kelp forest by 2040, and researchers have put the price of achieving it at 14 billion US dollars, a figure published in the journal Biological Conservation. The question, as so often at biodiversity summits, is whether governments will fund the unglamorous work at scale, and whether they will do it fast enough to keep pace with a warming ocean.
