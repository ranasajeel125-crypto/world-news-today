Over 60% of World's Kelp Forests Are Degraded, UN Biodiversity Report Warns

climate · 24 Oct 2026 · 3 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

Kelp forests shelter a quarter of marine species — and they are disappearing faster than rainforests.

Over 60% of the world's assessed kelp forests are degraded, according to a report published by an Australian non-profit at the UN's biodiversity talks, with ocean warming and overgrazing identified as the most severe drivers of decline.

Kelp forests are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. They shelter fisheries, protect coastlines from storms, and lock away carbon — yet they receive a fraction of the attention given to coral reefs or rainforests, partly because their collapse happens out of sight, underwater.

The mechanism is a cascade: warming seas and the loss of predators such as sea otters and large fish allow sea-urchin populations to explode, and the urchins graze kelp down to bare rock 'barrens' that can persist for decades. Marine heatwaves then finish off weakened forests faster than they can regrow.

Scientists at the talks argued that kelp deserves a place in national climate and biodiversity plans, with protection, urchin management and restoration planting all shown to work where they are tried. The question, as so often at biodiversity summits, is whether governments will fund the unglamorous work at scale.
