Nobel Winner Maria Corina Machado Returns to Venezuela After Nearly a Year Away

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 7 min read · By Rafael Costa (Latin America Correspondent)

Machado announced her return on social media on Sunday, with her press team sharing images of her in Lara state.

Venezuelan opposition leader Maria Corina Machado has returned to Venezuela after nearly a year outside the country, she said on social media on Sunday.

“I’m here. With you all,” the Nobel Peace Prize-winning Machado wrote on social media early on Sunday. Her press team shared images of her in central-western Lara state, in the Plaza del Pueblo de Santa Rosa, where she appeared at a church.

Her return had been long awaited. Machado, 59, had announced it and attempted it several times, and when she finally arrived on Sunday, 25 October, it sent shockwaves through the country's political landscape. She made her first public appearance among worshippers at the Church of Santa Rosa in Lara state, one of the country's most important pilgrimage sites, west of the capital, where she was quickly surrounded by an ecstatic congregation. She then travelled on to Caracas, where hundreds of cheering supporters swarmed her car. Starting from the state of Lara, her caravan crossed Yaracuy, Carabobo and Aragua as it inched toward Caracas, a journey of more than 300 kilometres that took her nearly 11 hours to complete. At about 10:25 that evening, her caravan passed through Santa Mónica, along the main route that leads to the capital, and thousands of people took to the Valle-Coche highway in Caracas to welcome her, waving from the back of a pickup truck as repressive bodies passed in patrols through the crowd.

Machado was barred by Nicolas Maduro’s government from exiting Venezuela, but she secretly left in late 2025 to receive the Nobel Peace Prize, after spending more than a year living mostly in hiding. In January 2025, she went into hiding in Venezuela after being briefly detained following a protest, and after her kidnapping, allegedly by government paramilitaries, at her last public protest in 2025, she quietly slipped out of the country. She reappeared in December in Norway, where she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for her peaceful challenge to Maduro's authoritarian government and her social organisation campaign during Venezuela's 2024 presidential elections, which were ultimately stolen by Nicolás Maduro. Exiled since December 2025 and living in Panama, Machado had tried at least seven times to return to her country, according to her team and that of her liberal-leaning party, Vente Venezuela, and she finally succeeded, by road, on 25 October, after ten months abroad and 16 months in hiding and exile.

The issue of her return has strained her relationship with the US Trump administration, which she has sought to cultivate as an ally since before it removed President Nicolas Maduro from power in January. Hours after US special forces seized Maduro on the morning of 3 January and whisked him to New York to face narcoterrorism charges, Trump said Machado lacked the support to lead the country, and he has since built his Venezuela policy around Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro's former vice president, who took over after his capture and was sworn in as interim president on 6 January. Trump has repeatedly expressed support for interim President Delcy Rodriguez, Maduro’s former deputy, and hailed deals on oil production and other business ventures by US companies in Venezuela. Trump said in September it is too soon to consider democratic elections in Venezuela.

Speaking at the White House on Sunday, Trump said he did not know anything about Machado’s return and suggested that the US would continue to back Rodriguez. “She’s a very nice woman,” he said, referring to Machado. “But we’re doing really well in Venezuela.” During an interview on Fox News on Sunday, Trump also said that the US had “total control of Venezuela” and that it was “taking out millions of barrels of oil a week.” Neither the White House nor the Venezuelan communications ministry immediately responded to requests for comment on Machado’s return. The US role in facilitating the opposition leader's return, after she had handed her Nobel Peace Prize medal to Trump on January 15, framed and inscribed, remained unclear.

The oil deal Trump hailed was announced on 28 August, when he called it the biggest oil deal in world history. Under the agreement nodded through by Rodríguez's government, a small, hitherto unknown oil company, North American Blue Energy Partners, was granted 100-year concessions over 17 Venezuelan oil fields containing an estimated 65 billion barrels, about a fifth of Venezuela's proven reserves. The company, in turn, granted the US State Department a 35 per cent equity stake in its parent company, 20 per cent of its oil at production cost and right of first refusal for the other 80 per cent, with a majority of its board of directors required to be US citizens and the US holding a veto over board appointments. Reporting by ProPublica revealed discrepancies between the White House account and the text of the agreement, whose 50-year contract, not the 100 years boasted by the US government, apparently violates Venezuelan law, which limits oil concessions to 25 years, and since the company currently produces 200,000 barrels of oil a day and will only reach 500,000 in 2028, it is unclear if and when the arrangement will benefit US consumers as Trump has promised. Rodríguez has proved receptive to US demands, opening the oil and mining sectors to foreign investors and releasing hundreds of political prisoners, and she held her first meeting with Trump in New York on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, an informal pull-aside at a reception Trump hosted, for which the White House published no readout.

Machado plans to meet with victims of the deadly earthquakes that struck Venezuela in June, as well as the families of political prisoners, the international community and various leaders from the democratic movement in Venezuela, David Smolansky, her top foreign affairs adviser, told Reuters. “She will work proactively for a date for free and fair elections,” he added. Twin devastating earthquakes struck northern Venezuela on 24 June, eventually killing more than 6,500 people, injuring more than 16,800 and leaving about 17,000 homeless, with nearly 61,000 people attended to at hospitals and the United Nations saying 6.76 million people may be affected. The government's response was criticised, with military personnel and other officials arriving late and doing little to help victims, including people trapped in the rubble, and the US Southern Command mobilised for earthquake relief. Machado has said only a “serious, democratic government” can provide the stability needed by investors. In June, she said she would do whatever it took to come home and help the survivors of the devastating twin earthquakes that month, and her most determined push to go home came in their aftermath. Her desire to return had caused friction in Washington, with US officials asking her to delay her trip, Reuters reported in June, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, long her most prominent champion in Washington, was reported in January to have argued against backing her after Maduro's ouster and in June urged her to be patient.

A faction of the opposition and the interim government held their latest session of talks this month, which aim to remake the top court and electoral council, among other goals, ahead of eventual elections. They do not include Machado’s political movement. US-brokered talks between the Rodríguez government and parts of the opposition, under way since August, exclude Machado and Edmundo González. Edmundo Gonzalez, a former presidential candidate for Machado’s movement who has been living in Spain, confirmed her return in his own post and said he would soon join her. Machado was barred from the July 2024 presidential election by the regime-controlled Supreme Court and backed González to stand in her place, and Washington itself later recognised him as the winner of a vote Maduro stole. Once the richest country in South America, Venezuela now suffers from the world's highest inflation rate, driven by a quarter century of political fracturing, corruption, economic mismanagement and US sanctions, and though the country possesses the world's largest proven oil reserves, more than 300 billion barrels, electricity shortages are a fact of life and many Venezuelans live in poverty amid crumbling infrastructure. Machado's return energises an opposition that has been sidelined by Washington's deals with Rodríguez, and her standing at home, where she commands the most popular support in the country, is an asset Washington has so far chosen not to use.
