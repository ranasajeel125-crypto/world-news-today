World Court Rules Myanmar Committed Genocide Against the Rohingya in 2017 Crackdown

world · 19 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The court's 9-6 ruling, read out over nearly three hours in The Hague, found Myanmar's forces killed about 10,000 Rohingya and acted with genocidal intent — a landmark judgment survivors had waited almost a decade to hear.

The United Nations' top court ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya minority in a brutal 2017 military operation, delivering a landmark judgment that survivors of the atrocities had waited almost a decade to hear.

International Court of Justice President Yuji Iwasawa announced the decision, reached by nine votes to six, at the end of a reading of the judgment that lasted nearly three hours in the court's Great Hall of Justice in The Hague. He said the court had found that Myanmar “has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine state, in violation of its obligations under the Genocide Convention.”

The 15-judge panel rejected Myanmar's defence that the 2017 campaign was a legitimate operation against insurgents. The court found that Myanmar's security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence and acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to escape in large numbers to neighbouring Bangladesh.

The court said the evidence showed that about 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed during the clearance operations. In its reasoning, the court said the scale and nature of the atrocities went well beyond what would have been required to punish the population, and that the “only reasonable inference” was that Myanmar's forces acted with the intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine state.

The ruling is the first time the International Court of Justice has held a state responsible for committing genocide in its 80-year history, and only the third time the court has handed down a judgment in a genocide case. In a previous judgment in 2007, the court found that Serbia had violated its obligation to prevent genocide in the 1995 Srebrenica massacre, but stopped short of finding that Serbia itself had committed genocide.

The case was brought by The Gambia, a small West African nation, in November 2019 under the 1948 Genocide Convention. Gambia argued that the 2017 offensive by Myanmar government forces, which forced at least 730,000 Rohingya into neighbouring Bangladesh, constituted genocide. Hearings on the merits of the case were held in January 2026.

In August 2017, Myanmar's military launched what it called a clearance campaign in Rakhine state in response to an attack by a Rohingya insurgent group. The campaign led to accusations that security forces committed mass rapes and killings and burned thousands of homes. A UN fact-finding commission concluded the offensive included “genocidal acts”, and Rohingya refugees recounted killings, mass rape and arson. Myanmar denies accusations of genocide and has said the offensive was a legitimate counter-terrorism campaign.

About 1.2 million Rohingya refugees now live in Bangladesh, primarily in crowded camps in the Cox's Bazar district, where they endure harsh conditions marked by fire hazards, infectious diseases, monsoon landslides, inadequate sanitation and strict work restrictions. Gambia has asked the court for reparations for the Rohingya, including ordering Myanmar to grant them full citizenship and the right to return home.

For survivors, the judgment was a moment of recognition after years of waiting. “This is a great day for the Rohingya, and it is a great day for all oppressed people, everywhere, who have been told that their suffering does not count and that no one is listening,” said Yasmin Ullah, a Rohingya rights activist who sat in the court's Great Hall of Justice for the three-hour reading of the ruling. “Because so far, we've just been pushed around into this limbo state,” she said. “It's been almost 10 years now that Rohingya have been living in very, very precarious conditions across the Asia-Pacific.”

The court's rulings are final and without appeal, and legally binding — but the court has no police power or other way to enforce them beyond reporting non-compliance to the UN Security Council. Whether Myanmar, which is ruled by the same military that carried out the 2017 campaign, complies with the judgment remains an open question.

The judgment comes as the World Court is also handling genocide allegations linked to the Gaza conflict and the war in Ukraine. South Africa has accused Israel of breaching the Genocide Convention during its offensive in Gaza, a claim Israel denies, and Ukraine has a case pending against Russia. “Genocides have similarities in how they are committed so this decision will have an impact on other cases,” said Melanie O'Brien, a professor of international law at the University of Western Australia.

The decision also comes at a time when the broader idea of international justice has come under fire from the Trump administration in Washington, CNN noted. The ruling's authority, supporters say, rests on the court's careful, years-long examination of the evidence — and on the fact that its judgment, once delivered, cannot be appealed.

What happens next: the court will now consider Gambia's request for reparations, including citizenship and the right of return for the Rohingya. Attention will turn to whether Myanmar's military government acknowledges the ruling at all, and to what the UN Security Council does if it does not. For the roughly 1.2 million Rohingya in Bangladesh, the judgment changes nothing overnight — but it gives their demand for recognition, and for a route home, the weight of the world's highest court.

Why it matters: for almost a decade, the Rohingya have lived in limbo — denied citizenship in Myanmar, confined to camps in Bangladesh, and told by the government that expelled them that no crime took place. Thursday's ruling is the first time any court has said, as a matter of law, that a state committed genocide against them. It sets a precedent that will be cited in every genocide case that follows, and it puts the world's highest court on record against a crime that Myanmar's generals have spent nine years denying.
