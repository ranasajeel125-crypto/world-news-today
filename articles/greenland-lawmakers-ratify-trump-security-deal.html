Greenland Lawmakers Set to Ratify Trump Security Deal

Greenland's parliament, the Inatsisartut, ratified on Tuesday, 3 November, the Arctic island's security agreement struck with US President Donald Trump, after a majority of lawmakers signalled support for the deal in a first debate last month. The vote was 16 in favour and none against, with seven abstentions, although some opposition party members questioned the deal's economic value. The island's 31-seat parliament had been expected to approve the agreement, which Greenland's government presented as the best available way to end months of tension with Washington while protecting the island's sovereignty.

The United States, Denmark and Greenland signed the agreement on 22 September at the United Nations headquarters in New York, aimed at ending tensions after Trump's repeated threats to take control of the strategically located and mineral-rich island, a self-governing Danish territory. It was signed by Trump, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly, after more than eight months of negotiations. The agreement amends and supplements the 1951 Greenland Defense Agreement, the Cold War-era treaty that has governed the American military presence on the island, and expands and clarifies the framework for that presence.

The deal expands the US military's role in Greenland, paving the way for two new military bases and Trump's "Golden Dome" missile defence system, while reaffirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlanders' right to self-determination. It authorises the United States to modernise and expand its activities at Pituffik Space Base, the remote American installation in northwest Greenland, and provides for additional American defence areas at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the northeast, with a procedure for further defence areas. Only NATO members will be able to establish manned or unmanned military installations on Greenland, a provision aimed at Russia and China, and the agreement contains provisions intended to preserve the defence arrangements if Greenland becomes independent. The full text of the agreement has not been made public, and key details, including the exact size of the future American presence, remain unclear.

Trump announced the agreement on 18 September on his Truth Social platform, saying the United States had "entered into an Agreement with The Kingdom of Denmark, and Greenland, that gives the United States permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland," at no cost to the United States. Officials in Washington, Copenhagen and Nuuk described a deal under which non-NATO states, Russia and China above all, would be barred from establishing bases or stationing forces on the island, non-NATO entities could not invest in sensitive infrastructure or strategic sectors without American approval, and the United States would hold permanent access, basing and overflight rights, including the right to build further facilities for projects such as Golden Dome. At the same time, a joint statement would uphold Danish sovereignty and Greenlandic self-government and rule out purchase or annexation.

The agreement followed a major diplomatic crisis. Since returning to the White House in January 2025, Trump had repeatedly said the United States should control Greenland, calling the island vital for American security, and he refused to rule out a military invasion, causing fear among Greenlanders, the majority of whom rejected US control, according to opinion polls. He also announced a 10 per cent tariff on eight European countries that objected to his plans. The crisis eased after talks with NATO chief Mark Rutte, and a high-level working group of civil servants from all three countries, established after a meeting in Washington in January 2026 between Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen, Greenlandic Foreign Minister Vivian Motzfeldt, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio and US Vice President JD Vance, drafted and negotiated the final agreement.

The two new base sites both carry history. Narsarsuaq, in southern Greenland, was home to the American base known as Bluie West One during the Cold War, which the United States closed in 1958; only a few dozen people live in the settlement now. Mestersvig, in the northeast, is a military outpost used today by Denmark's Sirius Dog Sled Patrol, a Danish special forces unit. The United States has been in Greenland for decades, keeping a heavy presence there during the Cold War, when nuclear bombers and early warning systems were based on the island. About 150 American troops now stay at Pituffik year round, monitoring for missile launches, and the base is a key node in the United States' missile warning and space surveillance networks.

Greenlandic Foreign Minister Mute Egede said the ratification was another important step for the island. "The road to this point has been a difficult one for us as a people. But we have stood firm on our fundamental principles, chosen dialogue and taken responsibility for our country's interests," he wrote in a post on Facebook. "This is about all of us. The Greenland we love. About our security. And about our right to shape our own future," he added.

The Danish parliament is due to give the bill a first reading on November 11, followed by a second reading and a vote on November 19. The executive agreement will enter into force once Denmark and Greenland have ratified it and does not require approval from the US Senate. Greenland, home to around 56,000 people, has been a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark since home rule was introduced in 1979, and the 2009 Self-Government Act recognised Greenlanders as a people with the right to self-determination, including the option of independence. The island's location between North America and Europe, its position on Arctic shipping routes that are opening as sea ice retreats, and its deposits of rare earth minerals and other resources have made it a focus of growing competition between the United States, Russia and China, and the new agreement is likely to shape the Arctic's security landscape for years to come.
