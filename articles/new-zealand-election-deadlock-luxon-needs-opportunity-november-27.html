New Zealand Election Ends in Deadlock as Luxon's Coalition Falls One Seat Short — and a Centrist Newcomer Holds the Balance of Power

politics · 27 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Opportunity Party leader Qiulae Wong, 38, became leader less than a year ago. Her party, which had failed three times to enter Parliament, won seven seats and now holds the balance of power.

New Zealand's ruling centre-right coalition has narrowly missed winning a parliamentary majority, the final results of the 7 November election showed on Friday, leaving Prime Minister Christopher Luxon needing the support of a centrist party that had never before won a seat in Parliament to remain in power.

Data from the Electoral Commission showed the coalition with 65 seats, one short of a majority in the 130-seat parliament. Luxon's centre-right National Party secured 40 seats, and its junior coalition partners ACT and New Zealand First won 13 seats and 12 seats respectively. Labour, the largest opposition party, won 36 seats. The new political party Opportunity won seven seats — and its leader, Qiulae Wong, has said she would negotiate first with the party that won the most seats.

The result is a reversal of election night, when the conservative bloc finished with a wafer-thin majority that left the outcome too close to call. The preliminary count had shown the ruling coalition with a majority of two seats. But the final tally, which includes around 485,000 special votes — ballots cast by New Zealanders voting overseas or outside their home electorate, which typically skew left — cost National two seats. National lost two electorate MPs in the recount, including Wairarapa, where Labour's Kieran McAnulty beat National's Mike Butterick by just 81 votes.

The Electoral Commission said 2,931,604 votes were cast in total, of which 484,865 were special votes. Final turnout was 77.2 percent of enrolled voters, compared with 78.2 percent in 2023, and about 26,000 votes were disallowed. The parliament will have 130 seats because of a 10-seat overhang — six from National and four from Te Pati Maori — created when parties win more electorate seats than their share of the party vote entitles them to.

The party vote tells the story of an electorate that punished both of the country's dominant parties. National won 28.35 percent of the party vote, down from 38.1 percent in 2023, and Labour won 29.88 percent — both below 30 percent for the first time. National still secured more seats than Labour because more of its candidates won local electorate races. The campaign was dominated by a struggling economy and voter disillusionment with the two biggest parties, and the final count showed more support for minor parties on the ideological flanks than before.

The arithmetic leaves no easy path to government for either side. The centre-left bloc — Labour with 36 seats, the Greens with 17, up one from election night, and Te Pati Maori with five — holds 58 seats. The results show that even with Opportunity's seven seats, that bloc would reach only 65, still one short of the 66 needed for a majority. That makes the right-wing coalition the only grouping that can reach a majority with Opportunity's support — but only if all four parties can agree.

That puts an extraordinary amount of power in the hands of a woman who was a political unknown a year ago. Opportunity is led by 38-year-old business consultant and political newcomer Qiulae Wong, who became leader less than a year ago after the party had made three failed attempts to enter Parliament. Her pledges of evidence-based centrism and an end to partisan bickering appear to have appealed to voters who delivered what the Associated Press called a stinging rebuke to the major parties, National and Labour, which have long dominated New Zealand politics.

Wong has said she could work with either side to form a government, and that the options her party would consider included a formal coalition deal and a so-called confidence-and-supply agreement, in which Opportunity's lawmakers would support government bills on a case-by-case basis rather than joining the cabinet. She has also said she would negotiate first with the party that won the most seats — which, in this parliament, is National.

Luxon now needs her, which is an awkward position for a prime minister who vehemently ruled out working with Opportunity before the election. On Friday, he reversed that position, telling reporters at Parliament that he expected to form a coalition between the four parties. He also warned the country to prepare for a long wait. “These negotiations may be challenging and they could take some time,” he said. “But getting it right matters far more than getting it done quickly.”

The negotiations will not be easy, because Opportunity's bottom lines cut directly against the positions of Luxon's existing partners. The new party's five so-called bottom-line policies include stronger measures on climate change and an end to bottom-trawling fisheries — both in direct opposition to the stances of New Zealand First and ACT. Luxon must now hold together a four-party coalition in which the newest member disagrees with two of the oldest ones on issues they care about most.

What happens next: the party leaders had not started negotiating before Friday's final result, because the close finish on election night prompted them to wait for the confirmed totals. Now the talks begin in earnest, and they could take weeks. Recount applications can be lodged within three working days of the official declaration, and a recount could still scramble the numbers. If no government can be formed, New Zealand could face a fresh vote — an outcome nobody in Wellington wants, but one that is now a real possibility.

The result was felt beyond politics. The New Zealand dollar, which hit a 17-year low against the US dollar earlier this week, strengthened slightly after the announcement of the election results, a small sign that markets took some comfort from the prospect of a government being formed rather than prolonged uncertainty. But the currency's weakness through the week was itself a reminder of why this election turned out the way it did: a struggling economy, and voters who no longer trust either of the two parties that have run the country for generations to fix it.

The deadlock also fits a wider pattern seen across democracies in recent years, in which voters drift away from the established parties towards outsiders and newcomers. Opportunity was formed in 2016 and had never won a seat; in this election it became the first party founded outside the modern political establishment to enter New Zealand's Parliament. Its success, like the rise of smaller parties on both flanks, suggests that the two-party dominance that defined New Zealand politics for decades may be coming to an end — whatever government emerges from the negotiations now under way.

Why it matters: New Zealand went to the polls on 7 November and, twenty days later, still does not have a government. The coalition that governed the country finished one seat short, the opposition cannot reach a majority even with the newcomers' help, and the future of the government now rests with a 38-year-old political newcomer whose party had never won a seat before. The negotiations ahead will test whether four parties that disagree on climate, fishing and much else can govern together — and whether a country that has just delivered a stinging rebuke to its two biggest parties can be governed at all.
