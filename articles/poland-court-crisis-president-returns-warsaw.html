Poland's Top Court in Crisis as President Cuts Short Trip and Returns to Warsaw

world · 31 Oct 2026 · 8 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Police detained three people after some protesters breached the tribunal's fence and others threw objects at officers.

Police secured Poland's Constitutional Tribunal on Friday as protesters gathered to demonstrate against what they perceive as the wrongful suspension of the court's president, Bogdan Święczkowski, by the current pro-European government. Police said they detained three people after some protesters breached the tribunal's fence and others threw objects at officers.

The information about Święczkowski's suspension was published on the court's website at around 6 p.m. local time on Friday. The website went down soon after, and The Associated Press could not independently verify the official reasons given for the suspension. Reports said a judge, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, announced one-month suspensions for Święczkowski and his deputy, Bartłomiej Sochański, and claimed temporary charge of the court — a move the tribunal's existing leadership rejected, insisting Święczkowski remains president and describing the events on its X account as an attempted coup. That “coup” characterisation is a political claim by the tribunal's leadership, not an established legal finding.

The crisis erupted on the night of Friday, 30 October, and drew starkly different interpretations from rival political camps in a country more divided than ever. The sudden escalation began in the late afternoon, when Korwin-Piotrowska, one of the judges elected by the Sejm, the lower house of parliament, but whose oath President Karol Nawrocki refuses to administer, announced the suspension of the tribunal's president and its vice president. Hundreds of protesters gathered outside the Warsaw building on Friday night, and at dusk some cut the fence with an angle grinder. Święczkowski managed to enter, ran into the police cordon and withdrew, while riot police cordoned off the headquarters and seven newly elected magistrates entered the building.

President Karol Nawrocki, who was flying to Senegal for a state visit, turned his flight back to Warsaw. “At a moment like this, the President must be in Poland. I am cutting short my flight to Senegal. I am returning,” he wrote on X. He ordered his plane to turn back over Spain and landed in Warsaw shortly before 10 p.m., heading to the presidential palace, where he received Święczkowski and Zbigniew Kamiński, the first president of the Supreme Court, who had just condemned the suspension decision. Korwin-Piotrowska had suggested a meeting at the presidential palace with Nawrocki but opposed his visit to the Constitutional Tribunal for security reasons. The president refused to follow this advice, and shortly before 1 a.m. he arrived at the tribunal's headquarters and forced his way to the building's entrance, saying he had helped the suspended court president enter the building. By Saturday morning he had called urgent consultations with parliamentary representatives, describing events as a forcible takeover of a constitutional institution.

Prime Minister Donald Tusk defended the move. “Restoring the rule of law is our obligation,” he wrote on X on Friday evening, adding: “After years of lawlessness under PiS this is a difficult process, sometimes painful and it requires decisive action. There is no other way.” On Saturday, Tusk said his government would press ahead with efforts to restore the independence of the tribunal, as hundreds of protesters gathered for a second night against the suspension. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek said he had sought the lifting of immunity for Święczkowski and his deputy so criminal proceedings could be brought. The government says Święczkowski repeatedly broke the law and jeopardised the tribunal's work, and Tusk had earlier pledged that his government would ensure the tribunal was again staffed with lawfully appointed judges while acknowledging that his methods may not be to everyone's liking.

Politicians from the national-conservative Law and Justice party (PiS), currently the largest opposition force, condemned the suspension. “This is a blatant, criminal attack on the constitutional order,” former Justice Minister Zbigniew Ziobro wrote on X on Friday evening, calling for resistance that would be “loud, firm, solidary and fearless.” Nawrocki and PiS politicians accused the government of carrying out a de facto coup and undermining the independence of the judiciary, arguing that police intervention on court grounds and the suspension of the tribunal's president were unlawful acts that threatened Poland's democracy and the rule of law. “Since last night, we're dealing with a massive constitutional crisis in Poland, the result of an illegal, forceful attempt to take over an important institution,” Marcin Przydacz, a top aide to the Polish president, told The Associated Press.

Poland's top judicial bodies, including the Constitutional Tribunal, have been a major bone of contention between the current pro-European government and PiS. Critics in Poland and the European Union accuse PiS, which led the country between 2015 and 2023, of imposing political control over key judicial institutions, including the tribunal, and the PiS reforms led the EU to impose fines on Poland. Tusk's government came to power in 2023 with a promise to restore the independence of the judiciary, but its efforts have since been blocked by veto by two successive PiS-aligned presidents, including Nawrocki.

The roots of the crisis go back to October 2015, when the outgoing Civic Platform-led parliament prematurely appointed two Constitutional Tribunal judges, a move PiS later used as a pretext to seize the tribunal. In December 2015 it repeated the election of five judges, including three for already lawfully filled seats, and President Andrzej Rzepiński refused to recognise these stand-in appointments, arguing that the move was unconstitutional. In early 2016 the PiS government stopped publishing the tribunal's rulings, and once Rzepiński's term expired his successor, Julia Przyłębska, turned the tribunal into a partisan instrument. The capture materialised in judicial appointments of former active PiS politicians, Krystyna Pawłowicz and Stanisław Piotrowicz, who moved directly from the Sejm to the tribunal in December 2019, and in high-profile rulings on abortion in 2020 and on the primacy of national law over EU law in 2021.

These events drew domestic protests and international criticism, including opinions of the Venice Commission in 2016 and judgments of the European Court of Human Rights in 2021 and the Court of Justice of the European Union in 2025, holding that the tribunal, including its substitute judges, no longer met the requirement of a tribunal established by law. The European Commission opened its rule-of-law probe in early 2016 and on 20 December 2017 triggered Article 7, the procedure that can end in the loss of EU voting rights, which ran for more than six years and through six Council hearings. The financial penalties followed: in October 2021 the EU's top court ordered Poland to pay a record daily fine of 1 million euros for keeping the Supreme Court's Disciplinary Chamber running, a fine later halved, and Brussels held back pandemic recovery money, then 36 billion euros in grants and loans, until judges could work independently, and withheld about 76 billion euros of cohesion funding.

After the 2023 elections, a centrist government under Donald Tusk took power, and Justice Minister Adam Bodnar, a law professor and former Ombudsman, pursued a broad remedial strategy, including a Sejm resolution declaring three appointments of tribunal judges from 2015 flawed. However, these plans failed when, in 2025, the PiS-backed candidate Karol Nawrocki won the presidency, closing any standard legislative path for reform. Nawrocki, a supporter of Donald Trump elected in June 2025 with PiS support, administered the oath to three judges but withheld it from five, who instead took their oaths without his presence. The tribunal's president refused to allow those five to begin adjudicating, and over the past six months Święczkowski used his power as head of the court to block judges elected by the current parliamentary majority from assuming their court duties. In mid-October, parliament elected another judge, meaning judges not friendly to PiS would have a majority, but Nawrocki refused to swear in the new appointment, and the judges elected this year took an oath anyway during a ceremony in parliament, a move recognised by the government camp but rejected by PiS.

The standoff is expected to continue, with legal challenges to the suspensions and further protests in Warsaw anticipated in the coming days. Protesters have gathered daily outside the court in Warsaw since the suspension, and judges at the tribunal were due to meet on Tuesday to decide its future, with Korwin-Piotrowska calling a general assembly of the court. The European Commission said on 1 November that it is for the Polish authorities to ensure the Constitutional Tribunal can function, adding that the appointment of eight new members lies within the constitutional power of the Sejm, while Justice Commissioner Michael McGrath said the court needs to be able to function in accordance with the Polish constitution and the case law of the Court of Justice of the EU. The dispute is likely to deepen political polarisation in Poland a year before a parliamentary election, further intensifying the standoff between the pro-European government and PiS, which seeks to return to power. Both sides accuse each other of breaking the law, including the constitution, and many legal experts, as well as the European Commission and the EU's top court, agree that the reforms to the justice system introduced by PiS while in power were highly problematic, while the government's chosen path has been described even by jurists most critical of the previous administration as a legal shortcut.
