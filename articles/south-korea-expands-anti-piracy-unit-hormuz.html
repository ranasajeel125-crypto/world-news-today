South Korea to Expand Anti-Piracy Unit Operations Closer to Strait of Hormuz

world · 5 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The unit has been stationed in the Gulf of Aden since 2009; the expanded area would reach waters near the Omani port of Duqm, about 650 km from the strait.

South Korea plans to expand the operations of its anti-piracy Cheonghae unit to waters closer to the Strait of Hormuz, the Defence Ministry said, following pressure from US President Donald Trump to provide support for the war in Iran.

The government will submit a motion to parliament to extend the unit's deployment to the Middle East, which is set to expire at the end of 2026, a ministry spokesperson said, confirming a report by Yonhap News Agency. A revised motion to expand the operational area and add military capabilities passed a vice-ministerial meeting on 5 November. The revised mandate would remain effective through 31 December 2027, if approved by the National Assembly. The motion needs to be approved by President Lee Jae Myung and the cabinet, and then by parliament.

The Cheonghae unit has been stationed in the Gulf of Aden since 2009 to ensure safe navigation of commercial vessels. It currently includes a 4,400-ton-class destroyer, a maritime operations helicopter, up to three rigid-hull inflatable boats and around 300 service members, according to broadcaster YTN. Its latest contingent, the 49th rotation, sailed from South Korea's Busan on 2 October on board the Chungmugong Yi Sun-sin, a 4,400-tonne destroyer carrying a maritime helicopter and about 300 service members, for a 193-day deployment ending on 13 April 2027.

The motion to be submitted to parliament would expand the unit's area of operation to waters near the port of Duqm in Oman, which is about 650 km (403 miles) away from the strait. Under the new mandate, the unit would push northeast into the Arabian Sea south of Oman, roughly doubling its operating radius to about 1,930 kilometres, officials told Yonhap. The unit has thus far operated primarily in the Gulf of Aden, using the port of Djibouti to the west and the port of Salalah in Oman to the east as operational hubs. The revised motion extends the operational range to the southern tip of the port of Duqm in southern Oman, nearly doubling the mission area compared to the previous 1,100 kilometres. The Strait of Hormuz itself, located more than 600 kilometres in a straight line from Duqm, as well as the Gulf of Oman, are not included in the newly expanded mission zone, and a new parliamentary deployment consent motion would still be required to deploy the unit to missions such as mine-clearing operations in the Strait of Hormuz.

Seoul will also deploy a P-8A maritime patrol aircraft and increase the unit's personnel by 75, the Korea Herald reported. One P-8A Poseidon, an advanced maritime patrol aircraft known as a "submarine killer", will be deployed for reconnaissance missions. Unmanned underwater vehicles, unmanned surface vessels and unmanned exploration devices designed to detect and clear naval mines have also been added, along with an explosive ordnance disposal team. The total number of personnel will increase from the current 320 to 395, including 45 aviation personnel and 30 mine warfare specialists, and the Chairman of the South Korean Joint Chiefs of Staff will hold command authority. Seoul has said the mission remains strictly non-combat and under Korean command, independent of allied forces.

According to the ministry, the expansion is primarily intended to address deteriorating maritime security off Somalia, where reported piracy incidents against merchant vessels have risen from 11 last year to 36 so far this year. The government also cited a gap in international maritime capabilities resulting from clashes between Saudi Arabia and the pro-Iranian Houthi rebels. The mine-clearing equipment is interpreted as a move with the Strait of Hormuz in mind, given that the operational area is primarily a zone for pirate activity where finding mines is rare. South Korea's military also considers it highly likely that, in the event the war with Iran ends, troops could be deployed to mine-clearing operations in the Strait of Hormuz.

The Cheonghae unit is South Korea's longest-running overseas military deployment, and it has a storied history. It was first dispatched to the Gulf of Aden off Somalia in March 2009, after its first destroyer, ROKS Munmu the Great, departed Jinhae on 13 March, and has operated continuously since 16 April 2009. It became widely known in January 2011 for "Operation Dawn of the Gulf of Aden", in which South Korean special forces rescued all 21 crew members held hostage by pirates aboard the chemical carrier Samho Jewelry, killing eight pirates and capturing five. The captain of the vessel, Seok Hae-gyun, was wounded in the rescue and survived. In April 2010, the unit pursued the 300,000-ton supertanker Samho Dream, which was carrying $170 million in crude oil, after it was seized by pirates. Since 2009, the unit has directly escorted more than 2,400 merchant vessels while providing navigation assistance to over 39,000 others. Its deployment has been extended annually with the consent of the National Assembly.

Trump has criticised Seoul for what he called insufficient support for the Iran war and scaled back major joint military drills by the two countries' armed forces this summer, a move that unsettled the US ally. South Korea relies heavily on oil imports and sourced 70% of its supplies from the Middle East in 2025, most of it delivered through the Strait of Hormuz, according to the Industry Ministry. That dependence is the strategic core of the decision: the strait is the world's most important oil transit chokepoint, and a closure or mining of the waterway would hit South Korea's energy supply directly.

Lee said on September 18 he would not deploy military assets to the Middle East in a way that could draw the country into conflict, but said Seoul was reviewing playing a greater role in safeguarding navigation in the region. The Cheonghae expansion is the product of that review: a deployment that moves South Korean forces closer to the war without entering it, adds capabilities — a patrol aircraft, mine-clearing equipment and bomb disposal specialists — that would matter in a Hormuz crisis, and stops short of the strait itself. Whether parliament approves the motion, and whether the unit's new reach reassures Washington without alarming Tehran, will decide whether the expansion is remembered as a careful balancing act or as the first step into someone else's war.
