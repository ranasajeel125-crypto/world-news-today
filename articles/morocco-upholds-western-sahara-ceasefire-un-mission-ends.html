Morocco Says It Will Uphold Western Sahara Ceasefire After UN Mission Ends

world · 4 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The 35-year-old UN mission ended after the Security Council did not renew its mandate last week, removing the international presence that monitored the ceasefire in the decades-old dispute.

world · 4 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Morocco will stay committed to the ceasefire in Western Sahara despite the end of the United Nations peacekeeping mission in the territory, the Royal Palace said on Wednesday.

The 35-year-old UN peacekeeping mission came to an end after the Security Council did not renew its mandate last week, the UN said on Sunday, removing the international presence that has monitored the ceasefire in the decades-old dispute. The mission, known as MINURSO, the UN Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara, was established in 1991 after a UN-brokered ceasefire, and it shut down at midnight on Saturday, October 31, a day after the Security Council failed to agree on renewing its mandate. November 1 marked the start of a liquidation phase, with a smaller team organising the withdrawal of equipment and returning it to troop-contributing countries.

The Western Sahara conflict pits Morocco, which considers the territory its own, against the Algeria-backed Polisario Front, a political and military organisation that has been seeking a separate state there since the departure of former colonial power Spain in 1976. The dispute is one of the oldest unresolved conflicts in Africa, and it has shaped the politics of the Maghreb for half a century, poisoning relations between Morocco and Algeria and drawing in the United Nations, the African Union and the great powers.

Morocco's "voluntary commitment" to the ceasefire will not change with the end of the UN mission, the palace said in a statement, following a meeting chaired by King Mohammed VI with the prime minister, royal adviser, and the army chief. The king urged vigilance in ensuring "strict respect for the Kingdom's territorial integrity, national security and the tranquility of its citizens," the palace said. The king also placed the Interior Ministry's MINURSO coordination office in charge of assisting with the mission's closure and withdrawal.

The Security Council failed to renew the mandate because its members could not agree on what should happen to the mission. On Friday, October 30, Russia vetoed a United States-backed resolution which had received 11 votes in the 15-member Security Council. The US proposal would have extended MINURSO for one year, removed "referendum" from its name and made Morocco's autonomy plan the sole basis for future negotiations. A Russian alternative, which sought a straightforward renewal until October 31, 2027, secured only three votes. The failure of both drafts meant the mandate simply expired at midnight on October 31.

The American proposal reflected a shift in how the conflict is seen in Washington and in much of the world. The referendum that MINURSO was created to organise, a vote on whether Western Sahara should become independent or join Morocco, has never taken place, and after 35 years the mission's original purpose had become, in Morocco's words, outdated. The palace said the UN mission had proven unable to ensure compliance with the ceasefire and that its initial functions had become outdated. Morocco's autonomy plan, which would give Western Sahara self-government under Moroccan sovereignty, has gained growing international support, and the US draft would have made it the sole basis for negotiations.

Morocco expressed satisfaction at the non-renewal of the mission, the Polisario said it was "concerned" and Algeria expressed "deep regret." The three reactions captured the three positions in the dispute: Morocco sees the end of MINURSO as a vindication of its view that the referendum is dead and the territory is Moroccan; the Polisario, which has sought independence since 1976, fears that the loss of the UN presence leaves it with less protection and less leverage; and Algeria, which hosts the Polisario and its refugee camps, sees the outcome as a defeat for the principle of self-determination.

Morocco controls all of Western Sahara's urban centers and its coastline. It built a security wall in the 1980s stretching 2,700 kilometers (about 1,700 miles), leaving most of the territory under its control, while the Polisario operates east of the wall with rear bases in the area of Tindouf in southwestern Algeria, where it administers refugee camps. In 2020, the Polisario said it resumed its "armed struggle" when Morocco took a border crossing with Mauritania. But the UN describes the conflict as "of low intensity." The ceasefire that MINURSO monitored has held, in broad terms, since 1991, even after the Polisario declared it over in 2020, and the question now is whether it will hold without UN monitors on the ground.

The palace said last week that the end of the UN mission means the withdrawal of the Western Sahara issue from consideration by the Security Council, and Foreign Minister Nasser Bourita said in a state media interview on Saturday that the next step for Rabat would be to remove Western Sahara from the UN's decolonisation committee, saying Rabat had the support of two-thirds of UN member states. Morocco says it intends to step up its diplomatic efforts and seek the territory's removal from the UN list of non-self-governing territories. The next milestones will be the continuation of the UN envoy's efforts, the winding-down of the mission and a General Assembly meeting announced for November 10. The end of MINURSO marks an institutional turning point, not an agreement between the parties, and the conflict it monitored for 35 years remains unresolved, now without the international presence that watched over it.
