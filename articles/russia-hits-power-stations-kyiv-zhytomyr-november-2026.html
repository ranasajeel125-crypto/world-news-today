Russia Hits Power Stations in Ukraine's Kyiv and Zhytomyr Regions

world · 4 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The strikes follow Tuesday's bombardments that knocked out power to swathes of Ukraine, a week before US-brokered peace talks are set to resume.

Russia has hit two power stations in Ukraine's regions of Kyiv and Zhytomyr, the Russian defence ministry said on Telegram on Wednesday, keeping up nearly daily strikes on its neighbour's energy facilities amid falling temperatures.

The strikes, which also targeted a cargo vessel at sea near Odesa, follow Tuesday's bombardments with missiles and drones that knocked out power to swathes of Ukraine, a week before US-brokered peace talks were set to resume. Elsewhere in Ukraine, Russia hit the regions of Poltava, Dnipro, Kirovohrad and Rivne, in addition to the capital of Kyiv, Ukraine's emergency service and local officials said in statements on the messaging app.

The attacks are part of a sustained campaign against Ukraine's energy system that has intensified as winter approaches. From the evening of 3 November until the morning of 10 November, attacks took place daily, often targeting the same facilities repeatedly. In Kyiv Oblast, Russian drones and missiles struck the Trypilska Thermal Power Station on 5, 7 and 9 November, causing significant damage to the facility, as well as the Kyiv 750 kV substation on 4, 5 and 7 November and the 330 kV Bilotserkivska, Brovarska and Novokyivska substations. The attacks led to a deterioration of the situation in Kyiv, where emergency power cuts had already been introduced on 1 November due to continued Russian attacks on the energy system.

In Zhytomyr Oblast, targets included substations in the capital region and in the Korosten and Zviahel districts on 4, 6 and 7 November. The 330 kV Ovruch substation in the Korosten district is reported to have been almost completely destroyed. Russian strikes on energy infrastructure in Zhytomyr Oblast left more than 650 settlements without electricity, the regional military administration said. In Zhytomyr itself, in addition to power shortages, there were problems with heating, and on the evening of 3 November the city faced a blackout.

The damage in Zhytomyr has had knock-on effects in the capital. On the evening of 6 November, Russian forces attacked an energy infrastructure facility in the Zhytomyr community, and the damage worsened the situation with Kyiv's power supply. Water pressure in Kyiv's water supply system also dropped, according to the city's mayor, Vitali Klitschko, and the water utility was switching power supply to generators at stations where it was possible. A total power cut resulting from a Russian attack also occurred on 7 November in Chernihiv.

Strikes on energy infrastructure nearly doubled in the second half of 2026, with the damage arriving earlier in the season than in 2025, according to data from ACLED, the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project. Russia is moving the focus from energy infrastructure in frontline regions and Kyiv towards energy targets in central and western Ukraine. Russian forces struck a series of 110 kV electricity substations across the country between 7 and 9 November, destroying one in Ovruch in the Zhytomyr region and damaging others in Yahotyn, Pereiaslav-Khmelnytskyi and Bila Tserkva in the Kyiv region.

The strategy is a familiar one. Since the full-scale invasion began in 2022, Russia has repeatedly targeted Ukraine's power plants, substations and transmission lines, seeking to deprive civilians of heat, light and water during the cold months and to wear down the country's will to resist. Thermal power plants, which burn gas or coal to generate electricity and often supply district heating to whole cities, are particularly valuable targets, because they are large, hard to protect and slow to repair. Substations, which step voltage up and down so that electricity can be transmitted over long distances and then delivered to homes, are also critical: destroying a substation can cut power to a whole region even if the power plants themselves are intact.

The human consequences are immediate. When the power goes out, heating stops, water pumps stop, lifts stop, and hospitals, schools and businesses must run on generators or close. In a Ukrainian winter, when temperatures fall well below freezing, a prolonged blackout is not an inconvenience but a threat to life, especially for the elderly and for people living in high-rise apartment blocks. Repair crews work around the clock to restore supply, often under the threat of further attacks, and Ukraine has built up stocks of spare transformers and other equipment, but some of the largest components are made to order and can take months to replace.

The attacks also endanger the safety of Ukraine's nine operating nuclear reactors, because nuclear plants depend on external power to keep their cooling systems running. Strikes on power plants and substations in Khmelnytskyi, Vinnytsia and Zhytomyr and other regions have put more of the country at risk of prolonged and unpredictable blackouts, which in turn raises the risk of an incident at a nuclear plant if its backup systems are called on repeatedly.

Russia-held Crimea ordered power cuts after an accident outside the region, a Moscow-installed official said, and a drone attack near the southern Russian city of Krasnodar, next to Crimea, set fire to a logistics hub of Russian retailer Ozon. The cause of the incident in the peninsula Russia annexed from Ukraine in 2014 was not immediately clear. The neighbours deny deliberately targeting civilians in the war that started with Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, although the pattern of strikes on civilian energy infrastructure has been condemned by Ukraine and its allies as a war crime.

The timing matters. The strikes came a week before US-brokered peace talks were set to resume, and last month a Kremlin aide said Moscow's attitude to three-way peace talks with Ukraine and the United States would depend in part on whether Ukraine launched a large-scale attack on Russia's National Unity Day, a public holiday observed on 4 November. The intensification of the energy campaign in the days before the talks suggests Russia is seeking to strengthen its negotiating position by demonstrating that it can impose costs on Ukraine through the winter.

For Ukraine, the response is a mix of air defence, repair and endurance. Ukrainian air defences shoot down many of the drones and missiles aimed at energy targets, but some get through, and the sheer number of attacks means that even a high interception rate leaves damage. The country has also decentralised parts of its energy system, built protective structures around key facilities and imported generators, but the scale of the Russian campaign, with strikes nearly doubling in the second half of 2026, is testing those defences.

As temperatures fall, the question is whether Ukraine's grid can hold through the winter, and whether the peace talks can produce a pause that gives repair crews time to work. For the millions of Ukrainians now living with scheduled blackouts, the answer cannot come soon enough.
