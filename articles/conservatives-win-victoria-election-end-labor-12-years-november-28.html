Conservatives Win Victoria Election, Ending 12 Years of Labor Rule in Australia’s Second-Most Populous State

politics · 28 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Liberal leader Jess Wilson, 36, is set to become Victoria’s youngest and first female premier after the Liberal-National coalition swept Labor from power in Saturday’s state election.

Australia’s centre-right Liberal-National coalition has won Saturday’s election in the state of Victoria, ending 12 years of Labor rule in the country’s second-most populous state, according to projections by the national broadcaster, the Australian Broadcasting Corp.

The ABC called the contest just before 8 p.m. local time, less than two hours after polls closed, and its election analyst Casey Briggs declared the outcome bluntly about an hour and a half after voting ended. “We have a Coalition government in Victoria,” Briggs said. “We’re confident at this point that that number is not going to climb anywhere near enough to see Labor win enough seats to form government.”

Based on early vote counting, the coalition was on track to win a majority of the state’s 88 lower-house seats, with 45 needed to form government. Labor, which held 54 seats going into the election, was projected to win far fewer than that — a collapse in a state long regarded as one of the centre-left party’s strongest bastions.

Premier Ben Carroll conceded defeat on Saturday night. “Victorians have spoken, and their message is clear: they want change,” he said, telling supporters he had congratulated the coalition’s leader on her victory. Carroll was among the Labor members to lose their own seats, in a state the party has governed for 23 of the last 27 years.

Liberal leader Jess Wilson, 36, will become the state’s youngest and first female premier, and was expected to speak at a campaign event later on Saturday. She will lead a Liberal-National coalition administration in Victoria, home to Melbourne, Australia’s second-largest city.

For the state Labor party, the wipeout was widely expected after 12 years in power, including presiding over the world’s longest lockdown of a city, Melbourne, during the COVID-19 pandemic, which left residual distrust among some voters, and the country’s biggest state debt.

The result also denied a breakthrough to the One Nation anti-immigration party, which had surged in polls over the past year amid rising frustration over housing costs, immigration and living standards. One Nation was projected to win as few as no lower-house seats, the ABC said.

Founded by Pauline Hanson in 1997, One Nation has spent much of its existence on the political fringes. Following the federal conservative coalition’s crushing defeat in 2025, it positioned itself as Australia’s answer to US President Donald Trump, Germany’s Alternative for Germany and Britain’s Reform UK, and Hanson had suggested the party could play a kingmaker role in the next Victorian government. With the Liberal-National coalition poised to win a large majority, that will be unlikely.

Yet the result suggests that, despite increasingly heated debate about worsening affordability, housing and immigration, Australians remain more comfortable venting dissatisfaction through mainstream parties than entrusting power to anti-establishment movements. It may also reassure Australia’s political establishment generally that the populist forces reshaping politics in Europe and North America have yet to fully take hold in the country.

The result is likely to sound the alarm for Labor governments in the larger New South Wales state and federally, which soon face elections amid the same economic pressures. Labor’s defeat in Victoria, seen as a bellwether state, piles further pressure on Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, who is halfway through his second term.

Counting continues, and the formal declaration of results will follow. Wilson was expected to address supporters on Saturday night, and Hanson was also expected to speak — but the verdict itself is no longer in doubt: after 12 years, Victoria has changed government, and it has done so decisively.

Why it matters: Victoria is Australia’s second-most populous state and its bellwether — and its voters have just thrown out a government that had ruled for 12 years, with the premier losing his own seat. The scale of the defeat, and the failure of One Nation to break through despite a year of surging polls, says two things at once: Australians are punishing incumbents over the cost of living, and they are doing it through the mainstream parties, not the populist fringe. That combination will now shape the politics of New South Wales and of the federal government, both of which face the same voters, and the same pressures, next.
