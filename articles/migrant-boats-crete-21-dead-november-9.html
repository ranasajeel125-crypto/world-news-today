At Least 21 Migrants Dead in Boat Emergencies Off Crete

world · 9 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Rescue crews brought survivors ashore in southern Crete after two boats got into difficulty in rough seas; the cause of the deaths has not yet been established.

At least 21 people have died in two separate emergencies involving boats carrying migrants off the southern coast of Crete, Greek authorities said on Monday, as coast guard and EU border agency crews rescued more than 100 others in gale-force winds.

A Morocco-flagged container ship spotted a migrant boat about 15 nautical miles (28 km) south of the port of Kaloi Limenes early on Monday. A coast guard vessel rescued 18 people and recovered 12 bodies; one of the survivors taken to hospital later died, bringing the toll from that boat to 13. Survivors were described as suffering from exposure after what officials believed had been several days at sea, and the boat may have been at sea for days, struggling to reach the shore due to strong winds.

In a separate operation that began late on Saturday, a boat sent an alarm signal south of the island. The tug Pyrros rescued 28 people, and the coast guard later found seven more bodies on board the drifting vessel, bringing the toll from that boat to eight. Survivors said two passengers had fallen into the sea on Friday and were still missing. The 28 survivors picked up by the tug were all Sudanese nationals, and one body was recovered with them before they were transferred to a police vessel and taken to Crete.

Survivors told the coast guard they had left Libya on 3 or 4 November, each paying 12,000 to 16,000 Libyan dinars for the crossing. Two Sudanese nationals, aged 28 and 16, were arrested on suspicion of facilitating the crossings after other survivors identified them as smugglers. The Heraklion Port Authority said the case concerned, among other alleged offences, facilitating unlawful entry and exposure resulting in death.

Two more boats carrying around 40 and 100 people were located by a Frontex drone south of Crete and brought to safety. A Frontex aircraft spotted one boat carrying about 47 people, while a Frontex drone located another with about 40 aboard, and a third boat carrying around 100 people was spotted by a Frontex drone roughly 47 nautical miles south of Crete later on Monday morning. In all, the Greek coast guard and the EU's Frontex border agency rescued a total of 105 people on Monday, according to the port authorities.

The coast guard said severe weather, with gale-force northerly winds, made the rescue operations extremely dangerous, and the cause of the deaths had not been established. Autopsies were expected to be carried out. It was not immediately clear what had caused the deaths, and the identities of the dead and the exact circumstances of their deaths were not immediately known.

Crete has become one of the main entry points into the European Union for people departing from North Africa. The UN refugee agency said more than 37,000 migrants had arrived in Greece during 2026 as of 9 November, with more than 22,000 reaching Crete. Many of the attempted crossings turn deadly when the vessels sink or run into trouble, and the deaths off Crete are the latest in a series of tragedies on the route from Libya.

The route from Libya to Crete is one of the longest and most dangerous in the Mediterranean. Migrants pay smugglers thousands of dinars for a place on an overcrowded, often unseaworthy boat, and they may spend days at sea before reaching land, exposed to sun, cold and rough weather, with little food or water. The boats are frequently old fishing vessels or inflatable dinghies that were never designed for a long open-sea crossing, and when they get into difficulty there may be no one nearby to help. The crossing from Libya to Crete is roughly 300 kilometres, and in bad weather it can take several days.

The people on these boats are mostly fleeing war, persecution and poverty. Many of the survivors in the latest incidents were Sudanese, a country that has been torn apart by a civil war that began in 2023 and has displaced millions of people. Others come from countries across Africa and the Middle East, and they travel through Libya, where smuggling networks operate with the connivance of armed groups and where migrants are frequently detained, extorted and abused.

Greece has become a frontline state in the European Union's migration system, and Crete, which was once a relatively minor arrival point, has overtaken the Aegean islands as the main destination for boats from Libya. The shift reflects changes in smuggling routes, as well as the fact that the route from Turkey to the eastern Aegean islands has become harder, while the Libyan route to Crete has grown. The Greek authorities have struggled to cope with the numbers, and the island's reception facilities have been stretched, with rescued migrants held in temporary encampments at ports such as Heraklion before being moved elsewhere.

The European Union's border agency, Frontex, plays a central role in the rescue effort, using aircraft and drones to spot boats in distress and to coordinate the response. But rescue is only part of the picture. The EU has also sought to work with Libya to stop boats from departing, a policy that has been criticised by human rights groups, who say it leaves migrants trapped in a country where they face serious abuse. At the same time, European governments have been tightening their asylum systems, making it harder for those who do arrive to stay.

The latest deaths follow another deadly incident last month, when 12 people, including an infant, died after two boats capsized while travelling from Turkey to the Greek islands of Lesbos and Samos. The toll from the Mediterranean as a whole runs into the thousands each year, and the central Mediterranean route, from Libya and Tunisia to Italy, Malta and Greece, remains the deadliest in the world for migrants.

For the families of the dead, the immediate question is whether their relatives are among the bodies recovered, and whether they will ever be identified. Autopsies are expected, but identification can take weeks or months, and in some cases the dead are never named. For the survivors, the ordeal is not over when they reach land: they face detention, asylum interviews and an uncertain future in a country they did not choose, and in a Europe that is increasingly unwilling to receive them.
