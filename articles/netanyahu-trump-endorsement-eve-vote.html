Netanyahu Touts Trump Endorsement on Eve of Israel Vote, Hints at New War on Iran

world · 26 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

Tuesday's vote is Israel's first since the October 7, 2023, Hamas attacks, and the first chance for voters to judge the worst security failure in generations.

world · 26 Oct 2026 · 3 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu boasted of his endorsement from Donald Trump on the eve of Israel's election, suggesting the two leaders could restart their war against Iran if he wins, after Trump said they should "finish the job".

"US President Donald Trump has come with a friendship that has never existed before between an Israeli prime minister and an American president. And it is doubtful we will ever have such a friendly president again," Netanyahu said in a televised appearance late on Sunday night. "He has another two years, and he says he is calling on you to vote for me."

In his own social media post on Sunday, Trump, who launched the Iran war jointly with Netanyahu in February, said: "Vote for Bibi Netanyahu for Prime Minister, we have to finish the job that we started!" Trump had already endorsed Netanyahu once before, on October 18, offering him his complete and total endorsement and praising him as a great warrior prime minister and an even greater partner for peace in combating Iran. The renewal of the endorsement on the eve of the vote was a deliberate intervention in the election, and Netanyahu made it the centrepiece of his closing campaign.

Netanyahu said Trump was willing to take action "that would finally, finally, remove the Iranian threat", but only as long as Netanyahu himself remains in power. "And I have already instructed the Mossad and the IDF to bring down this regime, to bring down this regime because that is the only way to put an end to this threat," he said, referring to Israel's main intelligence service and military. The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Netanyahu's remarks, but Deputy Press Secretary Anna Kelly later said that Iran was in very bad condition, both economically and militarily, and that the blockade would remain in full force and effect, with oil flowing in record numbers of barrels through the Hormuz Strait. She added that the United States would not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections, referring to the November 3 vote in the US.

Netanyahu's leading opponent, centrist former military Chief of Staff Gadi Eisenkot, praised Trump as "a true friend of Israel" and said he was confident Trump would work in full cooperation with any leader chosen by the people. Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of "relying on a campaign of fear-mongering, lies, and threats", including images of nuclear bombs in the heart of Tel Aviv. On Sunday, Netanyahu posted a computer-generated photo of a mushroom cloud over Tel Aviv, saying Israel was in existential danger from Iran, and on Monday he shared an AI-generated video of Iran's Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei, whose father Ali Khamenei was killed on the first night of US-Israeli strikes on Iran in February.

The endorsement carried its own complications. According to Axios, Netanyahu spent weeks seeking Trump's permission for an Israeli attack on Iran before voting day, but Trump did not agree, and in the final 72 hours before the election Netanyahu sought to direct the Israel Defense Forces to prepare for a potential strike or take steps short of an overt attack. CNN reported that Netanyahu had sought a joint US-Israeli attack on Iran before the elections and failed to secure Trump's approval, a report the Prime Minister's Office called fake news. Either way, the public message and the private requests pointed in different directions: Trump was willing to endorse Netanyahu and to talk of finishing the job, but he was not willing to authorise a new attack before the American midterms.

Tuesday's vote, Israel's first since the October 7, 2023, Hamas attacks, is the first chance for voters to deliver a verdict on the worst security failure in generations and the wars on multiple fronts that followed. Polls suggest Netanyahu's Likud party will lose seats, potentially depriving his coalition of its narrow majority, though Eisenkot and the opposition also have no clear path to unseat him. Netanyahu, 77, is an unrivalled master of political survival: the only time since 2009 that opponents managed to wrest him briefly from the premiership, it took four inconclusive elections within two years to get him out.

Trump's endorsement of Netanyahu also carried political risks a week before a legislative election in the United States in which his own Republicans were seeking to hold onto power. The Iran war is deeply unpopular in the United States, blamed for a surge in prices especially for fuel, and tying himself to a promise to finish the job in Iran was a gamble for a president whose party was about to face the voters.

The result of the Israeli election bore out the polls. Netanyahu's religious-nationalist coalition lost its majority in the October 27 vote but still emerged as the largest bloc, with 53 of the Knesset's 120 seats, and Likud finished first with 27 seats. Eisenkot's four-party alliance won 48 seats, and two Arab parties won a combined 19 seats, leaving no camp with the 61 seats needed to govern. President Isaac Herzog then tasked Eisenkot, not Netanyahu, with the first attempt at forming a government, and Trump later claimed his endorsement had turned the tide in Netanyahu's favour, turning what he called a likely defeat into a stalemate or victory. The election, and the endorsement that dominated its final days, left Israel deadlocked, with the question of war with Iran still open and the question of who would govern still unanswered.
