Russia Pounds Ukraine's Power Grid as Talks Open in Abu Dhabi

world · 10 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The strikes came as the first three-way talks since February opened in Abu Dhabi, with US envoy Jared Kushner saying recent contacts had “narrowed the gaps” — while Russia's foreign minister said Moscow would not agree to an immediate ceasefire.

world · 10 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Russian forces struck 10 Ukrainian regions overnight in air attacks that targeted five major electrical substations, sparked widespread power outages and killed two people, officials said on Tuesday.

Moscow's latest strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure — part of a renewed air campaign as winter approaches — came as Russian, Ukrainian and US negotiators began two days of talks in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday. The contrast between the negotiating table and the battlefield was stark: even as envoys met to discuss an end to the war, Russian missiles and drones were hitting the power grid that keeps Ukrainian cities warm and lit.

Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergiy Koretskyi said on Telegram that Russia had hit substations in the Poltava, Dnipropetrovsk, Vinnytsia and Kirovohrad regions. The two fatalities occurred in the Vinnytsia region. Earlier, state grid operator Ukrenergo said Russia had struck energy infrastructure in eight regions overnight and into the morning, leaving homes without power in six, including Kyiv, Kharkiv and Dnipropetrovsk.

Ukraine has struggled to intercept Russia's mounting attacks amid a shortage of weapons to down fast-moving ballistic missiles and jet-powered drones. The attacks have grown harder to stop as Russia has increased both the number and the speed of the weapons it fires, mixing ballistic missiles, which are very difficult to intercept, with large waves of drones that overwhelm air defences. Civilian infrastructure in the central Kirovohrad region, including a major home improvement store, also came under attack, regional governor Andriy Raikovych said.

Moscow has expanded its attacks in recent months to strike Ukrainian businesses, consumer logistics and other economic-related targets in a bid to weaken Kyiv's war effort. In the southeastern Dnipropetrovsk region, Russia attacked four districts with drones more than 20 times overnight, regional officials said. The aim, Ukrainian officials say, is not only to damage the power grid but to wear down the economy that sustains the war effort: warehouses, shops, logistics hubs and factories are now targets alongside power stations and substations.

The strikes on November 10 were part of a campaign that had been building for weeks. Overnight on November 2-3, Russia launched what Ukrenergo called one of the largest attacks on Ukraine's energy sector, targeting facilities more than 20 times in 24 hours and knocking three DTEK thermal power plants offline. On November 6, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy warned that intelligence showed Russia was preparing new strikes on energy, on bridges across the Dnipro and on civilian infrastructure, and hours later ballistic missiles struck Kyiv, damaging a kindergarten building. On November 7, Zelenskiy said Russia had fired 36 ballistic missiles and Ukraine had shot down only four. By November 8, more than 600,000 people were without power in Dnipropetrovsk region alone, with outages also reported across Kyiv, Kharkiv, Zhytomyr and Donetsk regions.

The scale of the damage is now enormous. Ukraine's transmission system operator has documented that dispatchable electricity generation capacity has fallen from 36 gigawatts before the full-scale invasion to roughly 12.8 gigawatts. That is a loss of almost two-thirds of the country's ability to generate power on demand, and it explains why blackouts have become a daily part of life in Ukrainian cities. With winter approaching, the loss of heating is as dangerous as the loss of light: in many Ukrainian cities, central heating depends on electricity, and a prolonged blackout in freezing weather can leave apartment buildings uninhabitable.

The talks in Abu Dhabi represent the first three-way meeting since February, and US envoy Jared Kushner said recent contacts with both sides had "narrowed the gaps". Separately, however, Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov said Russia would not agree to an immediate ceasefire without knowing what would happen next. The two statements captured the gap between the negotiating room and the positions of the two sides: Washington is pressing for progress, Kyiv wants a ceasefire and security guarantees, and Moscow is insisting on terms that Kyiv regards as unacceptable, while continuing its attacks on the ground.

The talks were scheduled for November 10 and 11, and they were the first direct meetings between Ukrainian and Russian envoys in months, with US mediators present. The fact that they were happening at all was significant, after months in which diplomacy had stalled. But the strikes that opened the talks served as a reminder of how far apart the two sides remain, and of Russia's willingness to use military pressure to shape the negotiations. Ukraine, for its part, has continued its own long-range strikes on Russian oil refineries, in retaliation for the attacks on its power grid, heating and water supply.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the talks in Abu Dhabi will matter less in the short term than the state of the power grid. The winter ahead is expected to be the hardest of the war, with millions of people facing blackouts, cold homes and disrupted water supplies. Repair crews work around the clock to restore substations and power lines, but each new wave of strikes undoes their work. The question facing Kyiv is not only whether the talks will produce a ceasefire, but whether the country can keep the lights on until they do.
