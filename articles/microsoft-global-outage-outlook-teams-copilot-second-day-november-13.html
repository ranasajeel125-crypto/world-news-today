Global Microsoft Outage Stretches Into Second Day as Fixes Fail to Take Hold

technology · 13 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Outlook was the worst hit service, with reports peaking at about 130,000 on Thursday; schools, doctors' surgeries and businesses were among those affected.

A worldwide Microsoft outage that began on Thursday continued into a second day on Friday, leaving many users unable to access email and other services as the company said its attempts to fix the problem were not working as expected.

Services including Outlook, Microsoft 365, Teams, Copilot and OneDrive were affected. Outage tracker Downdetector recorded reports from 74 countries and territories, with reports about Outlook alone peaking at about 130,000 on Thursday afternoon, and in one count reaching 140,000 people reporting Outlook issues. Around 15,000 Outlook problems were still being reported on Friday morning. By 1:25 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, the platform was receiving almost 20,000 reports about five key Microsoft services each minute, compared with the handful it usually receives on a typical Thursday. Users began reporting issues at around 1 p.m. Eastern, and Microsoft first acknowledged the problem at 5:54 p.m. GMT, warning 13 minutes later that users in any region could be affected.

Microsoft said in updates on Friday that it was seeing improvement in some regions, including Europe and North America, but that its capacity had not yet fully returned to normal and some users could still face intermittent problems. The company said changes intended to improve service stability had not delivered the expected results, and that some servers brought back online had become unresponsive again under live traffic. "We've monitored the status of the changes applied to improve serviceability and confirmed they're not taking as expected. We're identifying alternate workstreams that can be applied to increase capacity," Microsoft said in a status update. The company said it was exploring alternative recovery measures and restricting some traffic to stabilise its infrastructure.

The disruption ran far beyond inboxes. Schools, doctors' surgeries and businesses were among those hit, the BBC reported, and the outage disrupted police emergency calls, court operations, hospitals, airlines and government departments around the world, with the US Capitol among those affected. OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, also reported issues after the outage began, and Anthropic reported problems connecting with Microsoft 365 tools, underscoring the ripple effects as more companies use artificial intelligence platforms to interact with other firms' software. Microsoft said in January it had more than 450 million paid commercial subscriptions to its 365 services, and that reach showed in who was affected.

Microsoft said a process on affected servers responsible for establishing secure connections was becoming overwhelmed, causing the servers to stop processing requests, and that it had not confirmed a definitive root cause. It said there was no evidence of malicious activity associated with the incident, and it did not provide a confirmed timeline for full restoration. "We regret that today's outages caused disruptions for our customers, and we are working to address the issue," Microsoft executive Charles Lamanna wrote on X late on Thursday, confirming there was no indication the outage was the result of malicious or cyber activity. Microsoft is tracking the broader Microsoft 365 incident under reference MO1550821 and the Exchange Online mailbox issue under EX1550543.

The full explanation came in Microsoft's preliminary post-incident report, published on 15 November. It said there was no single cause: three separate problems lined up at the same time, and none of them alone would have been enough to cause a global outage. The first was a hidden defect in the networking gateway, the fleet of servers that receives connections before they reach services like Exchange Online. In July, Microsoft deployed a fix written specifically for those gateway servers, and the fix contained a defect that caused lock contention inside an internal Windows process, with many tasks waiting on the same shared resource until the machines ran out of processor and memory and stopped responding. The change had run worldwide for more than three months without problems, and lab testing missed it because the problem gets worse on servers with many processor cores.

The second factor was a shift in traffic. About 15 hours before the outage, Teams traffic failed over from an alternate gateway to the primary gateway, the same one that also supports Exchange Online, adding load and changing the traffic pattern the gateway servers were handling. The third was capacity: roughly 14 per cent of gateway server capacity was already out of service because of an unrelated issue the day before. At peak, more than 80 per cent of the servers hosting the networking gateway service were unresponsive. Automated rollback mechanisms could not fix the first problem, because the change causing the error had been deployed in July.

Recovery was slow because the fix itself was difficult to apply. As engineers reverted the faulty code and brought servers back in batches, traffic overwhelmed the servers coming up, a cascading failure caused by stampeding requests, and automated traffic routing had to be disabled so hosts could recover before receiving requests. Engineers started rolling out the revert at 7:20 a.m. UTC on Friday, bringing servers back in batches starting with Asia Pacific and then Europe. Webmail was back to normal worldwide by 2:17 p.m. UTC, and Microsoft declared the impact over at 4:10 p.m. UTC on Friday, almost exactly 24 hours after it began, closing both incidents early on Saturday after watching the backlog of delayed mail drain. To make sure messages sent during the outage were not lost, Microsoft extended its email timeout window to 48 hours, and the company said it was deeply sorry for the disruption. A final report, with the full list of repairs, was due by 20 November.
