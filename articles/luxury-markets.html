Luxury Giants Shed €250bn in Value as Chinese Shoppers Pull Back

business · 25 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Daniyal Ahmed (Business Correspondent)

Europe's six largest listed luxury companies — LVMH, Hermes, Kering, Richemont, Prada and Moncler — have collectively lost about 251 billion euros in market value this year, according to the Financial Times, as spending by Chinese consumers wilts and wobbles in artificial intelligence-related stock markets depress demand for high-end shoes and handbags. Results from LVMH, Hermes and Gucci owner Kering this month all missed market expectations that had already been cut significantly, confirming fears that the luxury industry remains in the grip of a multiyear downturn.

The declines have been steepest at LVMH and Hermes, the two largest luxury groups, whose market capitalisations have shrunk by 131.6 billion euros and 93.3 billion euros respectively this year. LVMH shares have fallen by about 40 per cent in 2026, and Hermes by a similar amount, even as the broader United States stock market has risen. It is a remarkable reversal for companies that were, until recently, among the most reliable compounders in European markets.

The immediate trigger was a round of third-quarter results that showed the hoped-for recovery in China had not arrived. LVMH said on 12 October that third-quarter revenue rose 1 per cent on an organic basis to 18.5 billion euros, but sales at its bellwether fashion and leather goods division — home to Louis Vuitton, Christian Dior Couture, Celine, Loewe and Fendi — fell 5 per cent, worse than the 2 to 2.5 per cent decline analysts had expected. Chief financial officer Cecile Cabanis said sales to Chinese clients globally fell by a mid-teens percentage in that division, adding that the deceleration was obviously largely driven by the Chinese clientele.

The picture at Kering, which also owns Saint Laurent and Bottega Veneta, is worse still. Sales to Chinese consumers fell by between 15 and 20 per cent across the group in the third quarter, an acceleration from the mid-teens declines Kering reported in the preceding quarter. Gucci, which accounts for nearly half of the group's profit, saw revenue fall 6 per cent to 1.29 billion euros, extending a decline that has now run for more than three years. Kering's third-quarter revenue overall was 3.345 billion euros, down 1 per cent excluding currency effects.

The China chill has even begun to bite at Hermes, one of the most resilient luxury houses. Hermes reported third-quarter sales up 4.3 per cent at constant exchange rates to 4.1 billion euros, below the 5.2 per cent growth the market had forecast. Overall sales at the group's ready-to-wear and accessories division fell 2 per cent globally, with chief financial officer Eric du Halgouet noting that a recent drop in the Chinese stock market, combined with a crackdown on offshore wealth, had made things tougher. Du Halgouet described the Chinese market as extremely challenging.

Demand from shoppers in China was the industry's motor of growth for over a decade, but a prolonged property slump and weak stock market performance have undermined domestic demand over the past few years. Monthly retail sales growth in the world's second-largest economy has averaged less than 0.5 per cent over the past six months, a sharp slowdown on the previous year. The CSI 300 index, which tracks China's 300 largest listed companies, has fallen 19 per cent over the past four months, a loss of more than two trillion dollars in value, amid fears over weak domestic consumption and an AI bubble. China's AI-heavy STAR50 index has fallen 40 per cent from its July high, wiping out about 480 billion dollars in market value.

New pressure has also come from tax policy. Since July, Chinese authorities have begun collecting a 20 per cent tax on dividends and interest earned from offshore trusts, the vehicles wealthy families use to hold assets abroad, and have required unreported past holdings to be declared. The measure targets the very clients who kept spending through the earlier stages of the downturn, and it is not yet clear whether wealthy clients are cutting back or simply buying more discreetly. Bloomberg reported, citing market research data, that Hermes and Cartier's mainland China sales posted double-digit declines in August and September compared with a year earlier — a sign that the pullback among China's wealthy is hitting even the brands most resilient to economic shifts.

The pain is not evenly spread. Brands at the very top — those selling scarcity and heritage — are holding up better than labels that leaned on aspirational buyers, who are the first to cut back. Hermes, whose handbags are famously hard to obtain, has kept growing overall even as its shares have fallen sharply, because investors had priced in far faster growth. Kering's Gucci, by contrast, has been in decline for years as its fashion cycles have faltered. Watchmakers and leather-goods houses have reported the sharpest slowdowns, while jewellery has held up better within LVMH's portfolio.

Executives are responding by courting American and Middle Eastern customers and by rethinking prices after years of aggressive increases. The groups are also reshaping their store networks: Kering is cutting its store count, while LVMH is leaving older, secondary malls in China for fewer, larger flagships in top-tier locations such as its Louis Vuitton and Dior houses at Beijing's Sanlitun Taikoo Li. Hermes is pushing further towards its wealthiest clients, with higher-ticket categories and more VIP-focused service. Most brands are resisting outright discounts in China, preferring to wait out the slump rather than damage the exclusivity that justifies their prices.

But with China accounting for such a large share of the industry's growth, there is no quick substitute. The United States, Europe and Japan continued to grow in the third quarter, but not enough to offset the slump in China. "The Chinese luxury consumer recovery that the industry was hoping for has taken a pause ... causing a setback in results, downward pressure on share prices and a lot of soul searching," said Bernstein analyst Luca Solca. Riots across France this month have added a further worry, threatening the Paris shopping trips that could offset weak sales in China. For now, the industry that spent a decade assuming China's shoppers would keep spending forever is planning for a slump that could last years.
