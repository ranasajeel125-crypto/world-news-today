Exit Polls: Netanyahu's Likud Leads but Loses Majority as Israel Faces Coalition Deadlock

world · 27 Oct 2026 · 7 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

The projections are exit polls from Israel's three main television networks, not an official vote count, and could change as ballots are tallied in the coming days.

Initial exit polls in Israel's election on Tuesday showed Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud emerging as the largest party but his governing coalition falling short of a parliamentary majority, potentially opening the door to a change in leadership after 19 years in office.

The projections released by Israel's three major television networks after polls closed at 10 p.m. local time showed Likud winning 25 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, compared with 23 seats for former military chief Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party in the Channel 12 and Channel 13 exit polls, and 21 seats according to Kan 11.

The projected parliamentary balance showed 50 seats for Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc, 53 for the main opposition parties and 17 for Arab parties, leaving neither major bloc with the 61 seats generally needed to form a government. Although the opposition parties together with the Arab factions would hold a combined 70 seats, that does not mean they would necessarily agree to form a government together.

The vote was Israel's first national election since the October 7, 2023, Hamas attacks, and was widely seen as a referendum on Netanyahu's leadership after three years of war and continuing questions about responsibility for the security failures that allowed Hamas-led militants to kill about 1,200 people and abduct 251 others.

Turnout surged to a historic high, with 55.7% of eligible voters having cast their ballots by 4 p.m. local time, according to Israel's Central Elections Committee — the highest turnout recorded at that hour since measurements began in 1973. Election officials assured voters that anyone arriving at a polling station before the 10 p.m. deadline would be allowed to cast a ballot.

Netanyahu campaigned on his record of confronting Iran and its regional proxies and on his relationship with US President Donald Trump, who endorsed him before the vote. His opponents accused him of failing to accept responsibility for the October 7 security failures and opposed his government's approach to investigating them.

The results set the stage for potentially weeks of coalition negotiations. Once the final results are certified, President Isaac Herzog will consult with the newly elected factions and select the lawmaker he believes has the strongest chance of forming a government. That candidate will have 28 days to negotiate a governing coalition, with the possibility of a 14-day extension. Official results are scheduled to be published by November 4, and until a new government takes office, Netanyahu's outgoing government will remain in power.

Exit polls are preliminary projections, not an official vote count, and the political picture may change as ballots are counted and verified. If neither Netanyahu nor Eisenkot can assemble a governing coalition, Netanyahu could stay on as caretaker until another election is held.

The first exit polls told a consistent story across the three main networks. Channels 11, 12 and 13 all showed similar results: Likud and Yashar in front, followed by nine smaller parties. All three major networks' initial exit polls had Netanyahu's Likud as the largest party with either 24 or 25 seats — down from its current 32 — while Eisenkot's Yashar was projected to win 21 to 23 seats. Channel 13's exit poll put Likud at 25 seats and Yashar at 23; Channel 12 projected 24 for Likud and 22 for Yashar; Kan 11 gave Likud 25 and Yashar 21.

The Kan 11 breakdown showed the shape of the next Knesset in detail: Likud 25, Yashar 21, B'Yachad — the joint list of Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid — 14, Democrats 10, Joint List 10, Yisrael Beytenu 8, Ra'am 7, and Otzma Yehudit, UTJ, Shas and Religious Zionism 6 each. The three broadcasters projected that the Netanyahu-aligned bloc would fall well short of a majority: 50 seats against 52 or 53 for the opposition bloc, with Arab-led parties expected to secure 17 or 18 seats. The pro-Netanyahu bloc had fallen from 68 seats in the last Knesset to 50.

The biggest surprise of the night was the record share of the vote won by Arab parties — projected to be worth 17 or 18 seats. Arab parties, despite representing about a fifth of the electorate, have never won that many seats; they currently hold just 10 seats in the Knesset. The Arab-led parties also oppose giving Netanyahu another term, meaning there was a clear majority of 70 seats in the Knesset opposed to the premier. But that arithmetic does not translate into a government: the opposition bloc, if it eschews any collaboration with Arab parties, would also not be able to form a government, and opposition within the bloc to relying on Arab parties could complicate any deal.

The exit polls were updated overnight, and the picture shifted in Netanyahu's favour. Channel 12's updated projection gave his bloc 52 seats, compared with 49 for the Jewish-led opposition bloc, with the remaining 18 or 19 seats going to the two Arab-led parties. Other polls differed sharply: the staunchly pro-Netanyahu Channel 14 showed his bloc winning 60 seats, just short of a majority, with the opposition at 43 and Arab parties at 17; the pro-Netanyahu i24 News showed Netanyahu's bloc with 55, the opposition with 49 and Arab parties with 16; and Channel 16, a new network, showed Netanyahu's bloc with 53, the opposition with 51 and the Arab parties with 16. Exit polls have differed from final results in past elections, and counting continued overnight.

Eisenkot claimed victory after polling ended. "The initial exit poll results indicate a complete lack of confidence in Netanyahu and the October 7 government," he said, adding that early results gave him the first shot at forming a government to replace Netanyahu. His bloc led 53-50 or 52-50 on Channels 11, 12 and 13, though Likud remained the largest party. The Jerusalem Post reported that the exit polls raised the possibility of a minority government that could win a confidence vote without 61 seats, though opposition within the bloc to relying on Arab parties could complicate that.

Turnout was the highest of any vote in the past quarter-century. Voter turnout reached 77.7 percent by the close of polling at 10 p.m. on October 27, according to Israel's Central Elections Committee, an increase of 7.1 percent from 2022 and the highest since 1999. By 4 p.m., 55.7 percent of eligible voters had cast their ballots — the highest turnout recorded at that hour since measurements began in 1973. Many voters said they saw the election as pivotal for the country's future.

Both Netanyahu and Eisenkot were expected to appeal to President Isaac Herzog for the chance to form a government. Once the final results are certified, Herzog will consult with the newly elected factions and select the lawmaker he believes has the strongest chance of forming a government. That candidate will have 28 days to negotiate a governing coalition, with the possibility of a 14-day extension. The final count was expected by October 30, while official results were scheduled to be published on November 4. If neither is able to form a coalition, a repeat election would be called, with Netanyahu remaining as caretaker prime minister. For now, the immediate outcome of the election for Israel's voters is more uncertainty: a largest party without a majority, an opposition without a route to power, and a parliament in which the balance of power may rest with parties that neither side can easily embrace.
