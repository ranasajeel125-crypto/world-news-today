New Zealand Early Voting Surges Ahead of November 7 Election

Nearly one million ballots have been cast in the first week of early voting for New Zealand's general election, the country's Electoral Commission said on Monday, 2 November. A total of 913,762 votes were cast between 26 October and 1 November, a 28 per cent jump compared with the same period during the 2023 election, when 712,192 people had voted by the same point. The busiest day was Saturday, when more than 190,000 votes were issued, and the weekend as a whole drew close to 326,000 voters.

The early turnout amounts to about 24 per cent of the roughly 3.79 million people enrolled to vote. The provisional enrolment rate stands at 93.6 per cent, slightly below the 2023 figure, but higher than it was before same-day enrolment was introduced. "Voting has got off to a good start, and there are still six more days to vote," Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne said. Around 600 voting places will be open daily this week, with that number rising to more than 2,300 on election day, Saturday 7 November.

Advance voting has become a central feature of New Zealand elections. In 2020, during the pandemic, early voting reached its highest level ever, as voters were encouraged to cast their ballots as soon as possible to avoid crowds on election day, and the 2026 pace is still behind that record year. Even so, the daily count has exceeded the 2023 figure on every day since advance voting opened, suggesting that the habit of voting early, formed during the pandemic, has endured.

The strong early turnout comes despite the government introducing controversial new rules that ended enrolment 13 days before the election, with critics arguing the earlier cut-off could deter participation. Previously, voters could enrol and vote on election day itself, a system known as same-day enrolment that was introduced for the 2020 election. Under the new rules, anyone not enrolled by the deadline cannot vote, and critics, including opposition parties and electoral experts, warned that the change could leave thousands of people, particularly young and first-time voters, unable to take part. The government has argued that closing the roll earlier gives the Electoral Commission more time to prepare accurate rolls and count votes quickly.

New Zealand elects its parliament under the mixed-member proportional system, known as MMP, which it has used since 1996. Voters cast two votes: one for a local electorate candidate and one for a political party. The party vote largely determines how many seats each party wins in the 120-seat parliament, and a party needs at least 5 per cent of the party vote, or to win an electorate seat, to enter parliament. Governments are usually formed by coalitions, since no single party has won an outright majority under MMP.

The 2026 election is being fought by the governing coalition led by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon of the National Party, in partnership with ACT and New Zealand First, against the opposition led by Labour leader Chris Hipkins. The coalition came to power after the 2023 election, when National won the largest share of the vote and formed a three-party government. Hipkins became Labour leader and prime minister in early 2023, succeeding Jacinda Ardern, and led the party into opposition after its defeat later that year.

Latest polls show no clear winner or coalition grouping in the tightly contested election. National and Labour have been running neck and neck through the campaign, with the Greens polling strongly and the smaller parties, ACT, New Zealand First and the Opportunity Party, all in a position to influence the shape of the next government. With neither of the two main blocs holding a clear majority in the polls, the Opportunity Party, which is hovering around the 5 per cent threshold, could hold the balance of power, and negotiations after election day may decide who governs. Pollsters caution that their surveys are snapshots of opinion at a moment in time, not predictions of the result.

The campaign has been dominated by the cost of living, the state of the economy, health services, housing and education, with both major parties promising to ease pressure on household budgets. National has pointed to its management of the economy and its tax changes, while Labour has promised more support for health and housing and argued that the government's changes have made life harder for ordinary families. The race has also featured arguments inside the governing coalition over strategic voting, with National urging its supporters to give it the party vote while its partners have encouraged voters to split their ballots.

Results on election night will be based on votes counted on the day, with special votes, including those cast overseas and by voters enrolled after the roll closed, counted later and able to shift the final seat allocations. In past elections, the special vote count has moved seats between parties, so the final shape of parliament may not be known until the official results are declared in the days after 7 November. What the early voting figures already show is that New Zealanders are turning out in large numbers, and that the final days of the campaign will be fought over a contest that remains too close to call.
