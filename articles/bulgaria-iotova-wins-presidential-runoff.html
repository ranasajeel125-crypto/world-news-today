Iotova Wins Bulgaria's Presidential Runoff, Rival Concedes

world · 2 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Initial results put Iotova on about 58% to Gyurov's roughly 38%; turnout was estimated at 27-29%, up from the record-low 23% of the first round.

Acting President Iliana Iotova is the apparent winner of Bulgaria's presidential runoff, according to initial results of the vote count on Sunday, with former interim Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov conceding defeat, the Associated Press reported.

According to counts by several polling agencies, Iotova received about 58% of the vote, against roughly 38% for Gyurov. If confirmed by official results, she would become Bulgaria's first elected female head of state. Official results were not expected before Monday.

“This is how the Bulgarian citizens have voted,” Gyurov said as he conceded, adding that the results had to be accepted as they stood.

Flanked by Prime Minister Rumen Radev, a jubilant Iotova declared victory, saying her message had focused on “incomes, peace and the challenges that lie ahead”. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated Iotova and said she looked forward to continuing joint work “for a strong Bulgaria in a strong Europe”.

The vote was the first major test for Radev's Moscow-friendly Progressive Bulgaria party, which endorsed Iotova. Iotova, 62, took over the presidency earlier this year from Radev, who stepped down to run in parliamentary elections, and is set to become the first elected woman president of the country of 6.4 million people.

The race for the largely ceremonial post — which still carries roles including commander-in-chief of the armed forces — pitted Iotova, an ally of Radev who favours dialogue with Moscow and opposes military aid to Ukraine, against Gyurov, a banker and former caretaker prime minister backed by the pro-Western opposition.

Exit polls put voter participation at about 27 to 29%, slightly higher than the first-round race last week, which had a record-low turnout of 23%. In the first round, Iotova won about 35% of the vote, with Gyurov on about 32%, a much narrower gap than the 20-point lead opinion polls had predicted for Iotova.

The election was held against a backdrop of a series of drone attacks on commercial vessels in the Black Sea that have raised security concerns in the EU and NATO member state.

The official count confirmed the scale of the victory. With all the votes counted on Monday, the Central Election Commission said Iotova secured 57.6 percent, while Gyurov received 39.5 percent. With 99.95 percent of polling station protocols processed, Iotova won 57.58 percent of the November 1 vote. The ticket of Iotova and her running mate Kiril Valchev received 1,093,852 votes, or 57.566 percent, while Gyurov and his running mate Georgi Kandev received 750,251 votes, or 39.483 percent, according to the commission's official runoff results.

Parallel counts from five major polling agencies had placed Iotova between 56.6 percent and 58.2 percent, while Gyurov received between 38.6 percent and 40.6 percent. Bulgarian National Television put Iotova at 57.4 percent against 39.6 percent for Gyurov. Iotova and Valchev declared victory at the National Palace of Culture in Sofia alongside Prime Minister Rumen Radev.

Gyurov conceded defeat on election night. "We accept the results, no matter how surprising they are," he said, adding that the results had to be accepted as they stood. He also said: "Nothing was in vain, and the best is yet to come; we have laid a solid foundation for development." His running mate Kandev said the fight does not end with this election, and Gyurov's supporters framed the vote as a warning to the government rather than an endorsement of it.

The first round, held on October 25, had been much closer. None of the 27 presidential candidates secured the required majority of votes: Iotova received 34.76 percent (528,951 votes), Gyurov 31.95 percent (486,232 votes), and Kostadin Kostadinov, a representative of the nationalist Revival party, received 12.54 percent. The gap of less than three points was far narrower than the 20-point lead opinion polls had predicted for Iotova, and it set up a runoff that many expected to be tight. In the end, Iotova's vote rose sharply between rounds, while Gyurov's vote rose by about 54 percent, from 486,232 to 750,251 — not enough to close the gap.

Turnout remained low. Exit polls put voter participation at about 27 to 29 percent, slightly higher than the first-round race, which had a record-low turnout of 23 percent. According to exit polls cited by Bulgarian National Radio, voter turnout in the second round was 27.2 percent. The low turnout reflects a wider disengagement in Bulgarian politics, where voters have been called to the polls repeatedly in recent years amid political instability.

Iotova is expected to be inaugurated on January 22 and to begin a full five-year term, with Kiril Valchev as vice president. She said she would work for national consensus by bridging political rifts, and her victory message focused on "incomes, peace and the challenges that lie ahead".

The vote was the first major test for Prime Minister Rumen Radev's Moscow-friendly Progressive Bulgaria party, which endorsed Iotova, and the result hands a win to Radev, who backed her and whose party leads the government. The stakes go beyond domestic politics. Iotova favours talks with Moscow and opposes military aid to Ukraine, while Gyurov ran with the support of Bulgaria's pro-Western, reformist parties. Her victory is expected to add political momentum to Bulgaria's existing cautious stance regarding military aid to Ukraine and sanctions against Russia.

The election was held against a backdrop of a series of drone attacks on commercial vessels in the Black Sea that have raised security concerns in the EU and NATO member state. The attacks on merchant ships off Bulgaria's Black Sea coast have killed at least 24 seafarers, according to Reuters, and they have sharpened the debate about Bulgaria's security and its place in the European Union and NATO.

The presidency in Bulgaria is a largely ceremonial post, but it still carries important roles, including commander-in-chief of the armed forces, and the president can shape public debate and foreign policy. Iotova, 62, took over the presidency earlier this year from Radev, who stepped down to run in parliamentary elections. Her election as Bulgaria's first elected female head of state marks a historic moment for the country of 6.4 million people, even as the low turnout and the sharp political divide over Russia and Ukraine show how contested her mandate will be.
