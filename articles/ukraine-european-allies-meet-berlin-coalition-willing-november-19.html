Ukraine's European Allies Meet in Berlin in Show of Support as Trump and Putin Talk in China

world · 19 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

European leaders meet in Berlin on Thursday to coordinate support for Ukraine, just as the US president and the Russian president meet on the sidelines of the APEC summit in China.

Ukraine's main European allies are meeting in Berlin on Thursday in a show of support for Kyiv, bringing together the leaders of Germany, France, Britain, Poland and Italy with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskiy and NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, Reuters reported.

The meeting of the so-called Coalition of the Willing, hosted and chaired by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and organised with French President Emmanuel Macron and British Prime Minister Andy Burnham, will also be attended by US President Donald Trump's special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. Irish Taoiseach Micheál Martin and Luxembourg Prime Minister Luc Frieden are among the other European leaders travelling to Berlin for the talks.

German officials said the meeting would focus on reaffirming a commitment to robust and legally binding security guarantees for Ukraine, which would come into force following a ceasefire. The leaders are also expected to take stock of diplomatic efforts, the continuation of international support for Kyiv, and the possible deployment of multinational forces once hostilities end.

The timing is deliberate. Only hours before the Berlin meeting was due to start, Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin were expected to meet on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen, China, where both are attending the gathering with Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ukraine is expected to be among the subjects discussed, and the Kremlin said one hour had been allocated for a three-way meeting of Trump, Putin and Xi, while a separate Putin-Trump encounter would take place after the APEC plenary session, with no full-scale bilateral negotiations planned.

German officials said the Berlin meeting was timed so that Ukraine's European allies could give a quick, joint response to any announcements coming out of Shenzhen. The summit in China has fuelled concerns in Europe that decisions affecting the war and the future of the continent could be taken without European input, Reuters reported.

Zelenskiy said the Berlin meeting would provide an opportunity to discuss the negotiations with Witkoff and Kushner and to hear details of the talks that Trump has held in China. "Ukraine clearly states its desire for peace, and we always work constructively," he said.

Burnham said the Berlin meeting would signal that Ukraine's European allies maintain their firm support. "At the Coalition of the Willing, Ukraine's friends and partners will make clear that our steadfast support for Ukraine will not waver and we are united as ever in securing a just and lasting peace that sees an end to this senseless war and ensures conflict does not return to our continent," the British prime minister said.

Britain used the occasion to announce a new £260 million support package for Ukraine, including more than 1,000 long-range strike drones, some capable of striking targets beyond 1,000 kilometres — roughly the distance between London and Berlin — as well as surveillance and logistical drones, the British government said. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said the package would boost Ukraine's defence against Russian attacks on civilians.

The meeting follows a series of moves by Ukraine's European allies to strengthen their backing for Kyiv. On Wednesday, the European Commission proposed a new €45 billion ($49.5 billion) loan to Ukraine to help fill a funding gap of €70 billion for next year. Funding for Ukraine will also be on the agenda of the European Council meeting on 26 and 27 November, and Luxembourg's prime minister has called on all 27 EU member states to share the burden of financing Kyiv.

The Ukraine Defense Contact Group, the coalition of countries coordinating military help, also agreed this week to supply more arms to Kyiv to enable it to keep fighting over the winter, after its 37th meeting in Brussels on 17 November. A major military exercise in Poland by the military arm of the Coalition of the Willing, involving 2,800 troops, also began on 9 November to test how Western troops could support peace in Ukraine after any agreement to end the fighting.

The gathering in Berlin comes at a moment of visible strain between the United States and its European allies. The two sides, close partners for decades after the Second World War, have increasingly moved apart as the Trump administration has scaled back support for Ukraine and insisted its NATO partners pay more for their own defence. On Tuesday, Germany and Britain rejected US criticism of Ukraine's campaign of deep strikes on Russian oil infrastructure and commercial warehouses — the same campaign Britain's new drone package is designed to sustain.

European countries, which provide the bulk of aid to Ukraine, have largely been sidelined from the US-led talks with Moscow. They sent representatives to talks in Abu Dhabi last week, but Kremlin officials said they had not agreed to European officials joining future talks. The Berlin meeting is, in part, Europe's answer to that exclusion: a demonstration that the continent's biggest powers can still act together, with or without a seat at the American table.

The urgency is sharpened by the war on the ground. Thursday's meeting follows an intensified campaign of Russian attacks on power and other infrastructure in Ukrainian cities as winter approaches, and Zelenskiy has warned that Moscow is planning new strikes on energy infrastructure. The weapons and money announced this week are aimed at helping Ukraine survive the winter, not only at shaping a future peace.

A coalition built for the day after: the Coalition of the Willing was formed to plan what comes after a ceasefire, and that is where the Berlin talks matter most. Security guarantees that only take effect once the guns fall silent are easy to promise and hard to make credible — they need troops, money, weapons and a legal framework agreed in advance. The exercise under way in Poland is the practical side of that work, testing whether Western forces could actually deploy to support a peace. The political side is what the leaders must settle in Berlin: who guarantees what, and with what force behind the promise.

The harder question is what the guarantees are meant to deter. Russia has shown no sign of accepting a European troop presence in Ukraine, and the Kremlin has already resisted European participation in the talks themselves. A guarantee that Moscow rejects before it is even written risks becoming a statement of intent rather than a shield — which is why the presence of Witkoff and Kushner in Berlin matters. If the American envoys carry the Europeans' position back to Trump, and Trump carries it into his meeting with Putin, the Berlin declaration could shape the Shenzhen conversation. If not, Europe will have stated its position clearly, and then watched the decisions being made elsewhere.

What happens next: the leaders in Berlin will be watching Shenzhen as closely as Shenzhen watches them. Any announcement from the Trump-Putin meeting, or from the three-way session with Xi, will be measured against the guarantees and aid packages agreed in Berlin. The next formal test comes at the European Council on 26 and 27 November, when EU leaders must decide on the €45 billion loan and on how the funding burden is shared. Beyond that, the question is whether the US-led talks produce a proposal Europe can support — or one it is asked to underwrite without having shaped.

Why it matters: the Berlin meeting is about more than Ukraine. It is the clearest test yet of whether Europe can act as a security power in its own right, rather than as a junior partner in an American-led process. The continent provides the bulk of the aid to Kyiv, its leaders are promising legally binding guarantees, and its troops are already rehearsing for a peacekeeping role — yet the decisions that could end the war are being discussed in Shenzhen, without them. How Berlin answers that contradiction, and whether Washington listens, will shape not only the future of Ukraine but the future of the transatlantic alliance itself.
