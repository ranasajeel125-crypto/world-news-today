US Strategy Links Myanmar Peace Talks to Rare-Earth Access

world · 2 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The strategy document says the US mission will work toward a ceasefire presenting a pathway to a lasting peace agreement by 2030.

Washington aims to use peace diplomacy in Myanmar as a way to expand US influence and potentially gain access to the country's rare-earth deposits, according to a recently published State Department strategy document, Reuters reported.

The document, an Integrated Country Strategy for the US Mission to Burma, was approved on 31 August and posted on the State Department's website on 16 October. It is unusually explicit about how Washington envisions US companies gaining a foothold through involvement in negotiations involving the country's many armed groups, which include pro-democracy groups and ethnic insurgents who have in some cases fought the military for decades seeking autonomy.

The US mission to Myanmar will work toward a ceasefire that "presents a pathway to a lasting peace agreement by 2030," the document says. It says the work includes facilitating "at least three multistakeholder forums that produce concrete agreements or policy proposals advancing fiscal federalism and territorial demarcation in Burma, which will create opportunities to access Burma's bountiful rare earth resources and combat scam centers." The strategy does not spell out whether the US intends to lift sanctions or address other compliance concerns that deter investment in Myanmar. The State Department did not respond to a request for comment on the document.

"We are pursuing a policy that welcomes dialogue with all stakeholders involved in the conflict as we encourage Burma to make progress on key US priorities, including addressing scam centres, improving the domestic operating environment for US businesses and building towards a genuine peace process that stabilises the country," a State Department spokesperson said.

Reuters reported last month that US officials have quietly held talks with Myanmar's nominally civilian government, run by the former general who led a 2021 coup that triggered a civil war the United Nations estimates has killed 100,000 people and displaced more than 3.5 million. The re-engagement, involving the State Department's top intelligence official Michael Vance, marks a departure from years of US policy aimed at isolating the military. The talks have included discussions of how to gain access to rare-earth minerals mainly located in conflict-affected areas and currently exported to China.

Vance, the Assistant Secretary leading the State Department's Bureau of Intelligence and Research, took part in a meeting with Senior General Min Aung Hlaing in Naypyidaw, Myanmar's capital, in September. The talks covered the release of an American businessman and a crackdown on online fraud rings. Additional meetings were later held in Tokyo. A US Indo-Pacific Command threat assessment dated 1 November summarised the shift as US diplomacy toward Myanmar expanding "from limited engagement to increased direct outreach".

The strategy sets out four main objectives. The first is protecting US citizens from transnational threats coming from Myanmar, such as online fraud centres, drug smuggling and visa overstays. The second is resolving the US trade deficit with Myanmar, by tripling American exports and securing at least three major commercial or investment deals worth more than $5 million each for US companies. The third is supporting conditions for an inclusive peace process aimed at a durable ceasefire and nationwide dialogue by 2030. The fourth is expanding US diplomatic, economic and public engagement to increase American influence relative to regional powers, above all China.

The rare-earth angle matters because these minerals are critical inputs for electric vehicles, wind turbines and advanced weapons systems, and China dominates both their production and processing. The United States has been working for years to diversify its supply chains to reduce dependence on China, and Myanmar has substantial rare-earth deposits — but the major mines are concentrated in regions destabilised by the civil war. To access these resources, Washington must deal not only with the government in Naypyidaw but also with the ethnic armed groups that control the mining areas. Currently, Myanmar's rare earths are mainly exported to China.

The US maintains financial sanctions on coup leader-turned-president Min Aung Hlaing, others in the military-backed government and businesses connected to them, but has long stressed it wants to see legitimate, responsible business in the country. The Trump administration has removed several Myanmar nationals and companies from its sanctions list. Even so, sanctions, the civil war and the absence of a functioning legal framework for mining in contested territory all deter investment, and the strategy document does not explain how those compliance concerns would be addressed if US companies were to enter the sector.

The shift has also put Washington at odds with its allies. The European Union has maintained a diplomatic boycott of the junta since the coup, and a Deutsche Welle report in late October asked whether the US was undermining that boycott by resuming contact with the military-backed government. The contrast illustrates a broader divergence: where European policy has treated isolation as the price of the coup, the new American approach treats engagement — including engagement with the generals who seized power — as a tool for securing commercial and strategic gains.

The approach also carries risks for the people the peace process is supposed to help. Myanmar's armed groups include pro-democracy forces and ethnic insurgents who have fought the military for decades, and many of them control the very territory where the rare earths are mined. A peace process that produces agreements on fiscal federalism and territorial demarcation would, in effect, decide who controls the revenues from those mines. Critics argue that tying a peace deal to resource access risks turning negotiations into a competition over mining wealth rather than a settlement of the political grievances that fuel the war. Supporters of engagement argue that isolating the junta for five years has not ended the conflict, and that a deal which gives all sides a stake in stability is more likely to stop the killing than continued isolation.

For now, the strategy is a plan rather than a deal. No sanctions have been lifted wholesale, no mining contracts have been announced, and no ceasefire has been agreed. What the document does show is that the United States now openly links its peace diplomacy in Myanmar to the minerals under the country's soil — and that, after years of isolation, Washington is once again talking to the generals, and to the armed groups, in the hope that a ceasefire can open the way for American business to compete with China in one of the world's most important rare-earth supply chains.
