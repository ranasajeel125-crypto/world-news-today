Pope Leo Begins 12-Day Latin America Tour Weeks After Lung Cancer Surgery

world · 6 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Leo, 71, had the lower lobe of his right lung removed last month; the Vatican says he has completed his recovery and needs no further treatment. The tour runs from 6 to 17 November.

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Montevideo, Uruguay, on Friday for a 12-day, three-country journey across Latin America, his longest and most far-reaching trip yet, and his first major overseas visit since undergoing lung cancer surgery.

Along the way, Leo will take 14 flights, including two helicopter rides, give 36 speeches and participate in around 50 events, visiting 10 cities, including Cusco, Peru, which sits more than 11,000 feet above sea level. He will celebrate nine public Masses, visit seven cathedrals and preside over two stadium prayer vigils. The tour takes in Uruguay, Argentina and Peru and runs from 6 to 17 November, covering around 19,000 miles, in what is one of the most intense papal trips of recent years.

Last month, Leo underwent surgery to remove the lower lobe of his right lung after doctors found a tumour during a routine scan. The operation took place on 13 October, and the growth was later confirmed as cancer. His surgeon, Dr Stefano Margaritora, said afterwards that there were "no clinical reasons why this visit should not take place", and doctors have said he does not need further treatment. Vatican officials say he has completed his two-week recovery period and requires no further treatment. His doctors are "very confident", the Vatican's new communications chief, Maria Montserrat Alvarado, said, adding that he will have "plenty of medical care on board should something happen".

The trip will take the pope to the former Inca capital of Cusco and the Amazon rainforest, and he will also revisit his old diocese of Chiclayo for a private visit to a chapel he helped rebuild. Cusco, at more than 11,000 feet above sea level, has 35% less oxygen per breath than at sea level, and the trip, especially Cusco's altitude, has raised questions about his health and the precautions being taken while there. Vatican officials told reporters that the pope will have a medical team available to him for the trip but stressed that no special measures are being taken. "We're speaking of a 71-year-old person in good health," the Vatican spokesman said ahead of the visit. The Vatican press office director, Matteo Bruni, confirmed that there was no particular accommodation or particular equipment being used, and said that no therapy was required and the pope was clinically fit to undertake the trip. Alvarado said the pope "personally confirmed this trip with no changes", and that he was looking forward to returning to Latin America.

Uruguay's left-wing President Yamandu Orsi presided over the welcome ceremony outside Montevideo's main cathedral, after a proposed papal address to parliament was scrapped following public criticism. Crowds of up to 30,000 people, according to the Vatican, lined the streets as Leo drove past in his Popemobile. The pope touched down in Montevideo after a 13-hour flight from Rome, telling reporters he feels "fairly good", and the formal welcome and speech took place at the Plaza Independencia, attended by President Orsi and government officials.

Uruguay is the region's least Catholic and most secular country, and it has not had a papal visit since 1988. Leo is expected to try to remind the 3.5 million people of their strong Catholic heritage stemming from the times of Spanish colonial rule. His packed three-day itinerary in Uruguay includes a visit to a neighbourhood with heavy gang violence, a Mass in Paysandu, about 375 kilometres from Montevideo, and another Mass in the city of Florida, about 100 kilometres from the capital, where he will mark the 8 November feast of the Virgin of the Thirty-three, a small wooden icon of the Virgin Mary that is intricately connected to Uruguay's independence movement.

Leo wasted little time addressing Uruguay's secular traditions and its liberal laws on abortion and euthanasia. Speaking in Spanish, he emphasised the importance of protecting family life and criticised both laws, saying: "It is everyone's responsibility to ensure that each person can live with dignity, receive the care they need, and be accompanied until the natural end of his or her life." He also commented on the country's suicide rate, the highest in Latin America, while reflecting on his own challenges with fighting lung cancer and the "many men and women who lack the means" for a timely diagnosis. The experience convinced him that healthcare should be "accessible to all", he said. Abortion was decriminalised in Uruguay in 2012, and assisted dying was legalised in 2025, both of which run counter to Catholic teaching.

By Sunday evening, Leo will be in neighbouring Argentina, arriving in Buenos Aires on a visit that fulfils a wish of his predecessor, Pope Francis, who had hoped to return to his homeland but never made the journey. It will be the first papal visit to Argentina since St John Paul II in 1987, and more than a million people are expected to attend his Mass in Buenos Aires. Argentina is the land of the Argentine pontiff, Pope Francis, who never visited his homeland as pope, and Leo's visit there carries a particular emotional weight.

The final stop is Peru, where the visit is being billed as a homecoming. As a missionary priest and later Bishop of Chiclayo, Leo spent two decades in the country, became a naturalised Peruvian in 2015, and has said the country shaped his life more than anything else. He will meet President Keiko Fujimori in Lima and visit several cities where he lived, including Cusco and towns in the Amazon, as well as Lima, Chiclayo, Santa Cruz de Succhabamba and Pucallpa. He is expected to draw huge crowds in Peru, with estimates of around 1.2 million people expected to attend his final Mass of the trip at Las Palmas Airbase in Lima.

Latin America is home to around 40% of the world's 1.4 billion Catholics, and it is the region where the Catholic Church faces its greatest challenges and its greatest opportunities. The Church has lost ground to evangelical Protestantism in many countries, and it faces criticism over its handling of sexual abuse and its stance on social issues. At the same time, it remains a powerful force in the lives of hundreds of millions of people, and a papal visit is a major event in any country it touches. For Leo, who is 71 and has just survived cancer surgery, the trip is a test of his stamina and his determination, and a chance to show that he intends to lead the Church from the front, even at an age and in a condition that would have kept many men at home.
