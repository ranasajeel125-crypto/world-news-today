Court Clears Army to Detonate Five Tons of Toxic Explosives in Iowa on Wednesday

world · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown holds 68 barrels of degraded lead azide that the Army says could explode on its own — and that nearby residents have been told to shelter from when it is detonated on Wednesday.

A federal appeals court on Monday cleared the way for the US Army to detonate 68 barrels of degraded lead azide — nearly five tons of unstable, toxic bomb-making material — at an Iowa ammunition plant on Wednesday morning, ending a court-imposed pause hours after President Donald Trump ordered the operation held until the court and the state’s incoming governor signed off.

A three-judge panel of the 8th US Circuit Court of Appeals lifted the temporary hold it had placed on an emergency permit from the Environmental Protection Agency, finding that the agency had “adequately incorporated safeguards to minimize the dispersal of lead dust into the environment and mitigate the risks associated with potential exposure pathways.” The judges said the environmental groups that sued to stop the blast — the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club — had failed to show they were likely to prevail.

In a statement late on Monday, the Army said it would proceed with the controlled detonation on Wednesday morning as previously scheduled, in coordination with state, local and federal authorities. “This is a consensus decision,” acting Army Secretary Adam Telle posted on social media. “Every expert agency agrees, and both Iowa’s current and incoming Governors say the call belongs to the experts.”

The ruling followed an extraordinary intervention by the president earlier in the day. President Trump, the Army’s commander in chief, wrote on social media that he had ordered the military not to proceed “until such time as the Eighth Circuit agrees that we can go forward,” and made the decision “fully subject to the incoming Governor Elect, Rob Sand, agreeing to the operation.” Mr Sand, a Democrat who does not take office until January, replied that the decision was fully up to the Trump administration and should be made “based on the information they have from the people they brought in to do the job months ago and have been on the ground in Middletown for weeks.”

The material at the centre of the dispute is lead azide, a volatile compound used to detonate military munitions. Staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown discovered in March, during routine stockpile surveillance, that dozens of barrels had degraded in long-term storage. The chemical had recrystallized inside its containers, leaving it extremely unstable and sensitive to even slight friction, movement, freezing or shock — and too dangerous to move off site.

The Army says a controlled detonation, with the barrels destroyed where they sit under layers of earth and concrete, is the only safe way to dispose of the stockpile. The alternative, officials warn, is worse: an uncontrolled, spontaneous explosion with no safety measures in place. In court filings over the weekend, Army munitions specialist Col. Aaron Teller warned that falling liquid levels inside the barrels make the solution more likely to freeze, increasing the sensitivity of crystals forming around the rims and lids. “Even a small amount of friction can break the crystal formation, causing an explosive chain reaction,” he wrote. The EPA has said there is an immediate risk as temperatures in Iowa are expected to drop below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 1 Celsius) this week.

The detonation was originally scheduled for Friday 20 November, but has now been delayed three times after an outpouring of public concern about the health and environmental effects. The Army says Wednesday — the day before Thanksgiving — offers the most favourable weather. The EPA’s emergency permit, which took effect on 13 November and runs until 11 February 2027, requires containment barriers, weather restrictions, advance public notification, air monitoring and sampling, and post-detonation cleanup. The Federal Aviation Administration has restricted flights around Middletown from Monday through Wednesday.

The health risk is the reason the plan has drawn such fierce opposition. Officials acknowledge that lead is toxic and have advised people to stay indoors when the detonation happens and to wash down outdoor surfaces with water afterwards. The Army has warned that the blast could send a plume of toxic lead dust as far as 100 miles, across a swath of Iowa, the Mississippi River and into parts of Illinois. The American Lung Association has advised people living close to the site to remain indoors for 24 to 48 hours. Some residents near Middletown have already begun sealing their windows and doors; others farther away have raised concerns on social media about which way the wind will carry the plume.

The reaction to Monday’s ruling split along the lines of the lawsuit itself. Rachel Rintelmann, the Earthjustice managing attorney representing the environmental groups, said: “Today, the 8th circuit decided to let the Army proceed with its plan, and we respect that decision. But the Army created this emergency through neglect, and it owes the people of Iowa more information about the planned detonation and more safeguards for their health.” Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson, by contrast, said the detonation must take place “so we can move on with our lives.” Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds activated the state emergency management office on Friday, and the state has set up a dedicated website with information about the operation. Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe said his state’s agencies were setting up additional monitoring of air and water quality, but stressed: “This is a federal operation. The states of Iowa, Missouri and Illinois do not have a say on when this is conducted.”

Why it matters: the court was asked to weigh two bad options — the certain harm of a controlled blast against the unpredictable harm of an accident. “We are mindful of the very real risks presented by the Army’s planned detonation of the stores of degraded lead azide,” the judges wrote. “However, we must acknowledge the realities of the situation: the degraded explosives present a risk of an uncontrolled and unplanned explosion, which would occur without the benefit of the safety measures that the Permit puts in place.” As one chemistry professor put it, the Army is in a position with “a large amount of very dangerous material and not very many good options.” Wednesday morning will show whether the controlled option works as planned.
