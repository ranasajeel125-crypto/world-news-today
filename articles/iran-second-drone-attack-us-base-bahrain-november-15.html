Iran Launches Second Drone Attack on US Base in Bahrain in Three Days

world · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Explosions were heard over Bahrain in the early hours of Sunday as Iran said six of its drones targeted a US air-defence radar at the al-Riffa helicopter base; Bahrain said its air defences intercepted the attacks.

Iran launched a drone attack against a US military base in Bahrain early on Sunday, its second attempt to target the Gulf kingdom in three days, the BBC reported. The country’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) said it targeted the US air defence radar facility at the al-Riffa air base using six drones. Bahrain’s military said its air defence systems intercepted and destroyed the Iranian air attacks “before they could achieve their objectives”.

Explosions were heard over Bahrain at about 4am local time (01:00 GMT), an AFP journalist in the kingdom reported, with residents reporting a second series of blasts at about 6:30am. Sirens sounded across the capital, Manama, and Bahrain’s interior ministry issued two public alerts — the first at about 4:30am — urging residents to remain calm and proceed to the nearest safe location. It was the first time the kingdom had issued such nationwide alerts since September.

The Revolutionary Guard described the operation as the “second phase” of its retaliation for what it called hostile US actions. In a statement carried by Iranian state broadcaster IRIB and posted on Telegram, the Guard said: “The US air defence radar system at the Al-Riffa helicopter base in Bahrain was targeted by six drones belonging to the IRGC Aerospace Force.” The statement also declared: “The Strait of Hormuz is closed, and any aggression by the American enemy will be met with an even harsher and more forceful response.”

Bahrain’s Defence Force said its air defences had intercepted and destroyed several Iranian aerial attacks before they reached their targets. The kingdom’s foreign ministry condemned what it called “a resumption of Iran’s aggression against Bahrain and other countries in the region”, saying Bahrain “rejects the use of its territory to settle accounts and holds Iran fully responsible for the attacks and their consequences”.

Whether the radar was actually hit remains unconfirmed. Neither the United States nor Bahrain confirmed that the al-Riffa installation had been struck, and no damage or casualties were reported by either government. The US military, which commented within hours of Friday’s attack, had not commented on Sunday’s claim at the time of reporting, and the Guard’s claim of a successful strike was carried without independent evidence.

The attack followed a near-identical claim on Friday. The Guard said then that it had targeted and destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain, in retaliation for what it described as an American attack on a communications tower in Sirik, on Iran’s southern coast near the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command rejected that account, saying American forces shot down two Iranian one-way attack drones launched toward Bahrain, with no injuries or damage. Friday was the first time since early September that Iran had attacked US military assets in a Gulf state.

Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baghaei, defended the strikes at a news conference, saying Iran had taken no action against the sovereignty of the Gulf states and describing the attacks as “purely defensive in nature” and aimed only at US military bases and assets used against Iran. Al Jazeera’s correspondent in Tehran reported that Iran had held back through weeks of intermittent US strikes before firing at Bahrain twice in three days.

The stakes are high because of where the attacks are happening. Bahrain hosts the headquarters of the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet, and the Riffa base is used by the US military. The war began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, and the two sides have been locked in a stand-off over the Strait of Hormuz, the world’s most important oil chokepoint, ever since. The US and Iran reached a preliminary agreement in June to end the war and reopen the strait, but it collapsed within weeks after Iranian attacks on shipping resumed and the US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports. October recorded 37 vessel attacks, the highest monthly total of the conflict, according to Lloyd’s List.

Diplomacy, for now, has stalled. Iranian negotiators had been discussing with mediators a proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if the US met seven conditions, reportedly including the lifting of the naval blockade and the release of frozen Iranian assets, the BBC reported. But President Donald Trump said this week that the United States was no longer interested in a deal and ordered Vice-President JD Vance, who had been leading efforts to restart negotiations, to turn his attention to domestic politics instead.

What happens next will turn on whether either side treats the second strike as a line crossed. Bahrain has now used its air defences in anger for the first time since September, and its government has publicly accused Tehran of using Bahraini soil to settle its score with Washington — language that raises the political cost of a third attack. Tehran, meanwhile, has warned that any further American action will be met with a harsher response, and has described its strikes as retaliation rather than escalation. With no talks scheduled and no deal on the table, the space for a miscalculation — a drone that gets through, a radar that is actually hit, a ship struck in the strait — is growing with each exchange.

Why it matters: two attacks on the same country in three days mark a clear break with the pattern of the past two months, when Iran largely refrained from striking American installations in the Gulf and concentrated on disrupting shipping instead. Bahrain is not a peripheral target: it hosts the US Fifth Fleet, and an attack there is an attack on the centre of American naval power in the region. The competing accounts — Tehran claiming a radar destroyed, Manama claiming every drone intercepted — also show how hard it has become to establish what is actually happening in a war fought increasingly through claims on Telegram and denials from military commands. Until independent evidence emerges, the one fact both sides agree on is the most important one: the drones are flying again, and the lull is over.
