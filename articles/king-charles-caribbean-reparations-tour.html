King Charles Heads to the Caribbean as Calls for Slavery Reparations Grow

world · 27 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

The tour runs from October 27 to November 4 and ends with the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua and Barbuda.

world · 27 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

King Charles heads to the Caribbean this week on a tour which comes against the backdrop of increasingly vocal demands from the region that Britain pays reparations for its role in the slave trade.

Charles and his wife Queen Camilla will first visit the Bahamas and Guyana, before heading to Antigua and Barbuda for the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM), the biennial get-together of the international organisation which he heads. The tour runs from October 27 to November 4. The king began the nine-day tour on Tuesday in the Bahamas, where he acknowledged "the great suffering and injustices of the past" at a youth rally in Nassau, and confirmed that the legacy of slavery would be discussed by Commonwealth leaders at the biennial summit of 56 nations next week. He told the rally that some of what is best in Bahamian culture today, the most joyful, the most hopeful, the most bright, was born out of darkness.

The Bahamas is the first stop, and Charles will stay there for three days. It is his first visit to the Caribbean as monarch, and his first official visit to the Bahamas since 1973, when the country became independent in July of that year and he attended the ceremonies that ended 300 years of British rule as Prince of Wales. The Bahamas remains one of seven Caribbean nations that keep King Charles III as monarch and head of state, and government officials in the Bahamas and in Antigua and Barbuda have signalled that they want to become republics.

From the Bahamas, Charles travels to Guyana, arriving on October 29 for a two-day state visit. Guyana is a former British colony that had more than 100,000 slaves when the trade was abolished in 1807, and it gained independence from the UK in 1966, with a president as its head of state. He is expected to meet President Mohamed Irfaan Ali and elected leaders of Amerindian villages, and to visit rainforest conservation projects. President Ali told ITV News that while Charles had rightfully recognised slavery as the darkest days in human history during a visit to Barbados in 2021, when he was still Prince of Wales, an apology should be his next step, saying the former empire had exploited the region, destroyed lives and created underdevelopment.

At a previous meeting in 2022 of the leaders of the Commonwealth, a group of 56 nations which were mainly former British colonies, Charles expressed deep personal sorrow over slavery, and at the last CHOGM, in 2024, he acknowledged that the most painful aspects of our past continue to resonate. The issue is certain to be prominent once again in Antigua and Barbuda, where the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting runs from November 1 to 4 and is expected to cover climate resilience, trade and development as well as reparations.

Last month, CARICOM, the Caribbean Community, a group of 23 member and associate member states which includes the Bahamas, Barbados and Jamaica, said it would push for reparations to be on the CHOGM agenda. "The Caribbean has consistently asserted that reparations is fundamentally about justice," CARICOM's Secretary-General Carla Barnett said in a speech last month. "It is about recognition, accountability and transformation, and about building a just and equitable future for the descendants of those who, as the chattel of colonial owners, endured untold suffering, loss of life and freedom."

While Buckingham Palace has said the monarch would not shy away from Britain's involvement in the transatlantic slave trade during his tour, he would not formally apologise for it as some have wanted, in keeping with British government policy which also excludes making any financial reparations. The palace said such questions were matters for governments to address, but that the king would not shy away from the darkest days of our past. "It is important to note here the work his majesty himself has done over many years both to acknowledge the wrongs of the past and to find creative ways to right inequalities that endure," a palace spokesperson said. UK Foreign Office sources have told CNN that there is no legal case for reparations because slavery was not illegal at the time it was active.

Shirley Botchwey, the secretary-general of the Commonwealth, which encompasses some 2.7 billion people, said last week the time had come for discussions about reparations, be they financial or in some other form such as the return of artefacts. As for an apology from the king, who remains the head of state of a number of Caribbean nations including both the Bahamas and Antigua and Barbuda, she told Sky News: "It wouldn't hurt to have that." She said it was important to determine the form that reparations should take, and that that was something that would emerge eventually. She described the Commonwealth as an important anchor in a world that is almost spinning out of control, and said this CHOGM would be one like never before, with the meeting in St John's set to focus on investment and trade opportunities.

Jamaica, which is not on the itinerary for this trip, made its stance clear in September, when it filed a $10 billion slavery reparations petition in London, addressing it to the king. On September 7, a Jamaican delegation led by Culture Minister Olivia Grange delivered the petition to Buckingham Palace, using a legal route that dates back to the empire. It asked the king to refer three questions to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London, which is still Jamaica's final court of appeal: whether the enslavement of Africans in Jamaica was lawful under English law, whether it breached international law, and whether Britain has a legal duty to compensate for the harm it caused. The petition is due to be heard by the Privy Council in March next year. The UK has, so far, refused to formally apologise or pay reparations for slavery.

From the 15th to the 19th century, at least 12.5 million Africans were abducted and sold into slavery by European merchants, with Britain responsible for transporting an estimated 3.2 million people, making it the second most active European nation after Portugal. The 77-year-old king, who is receiving treatment for an undisclosed form of cancer, will also highlight climate threats facing low-lying islands during the visit, saying that our world cries out for healing. He will meet organisations working in the field, including a coral restoration project in Antigua and Barbuda and rainforest conservation in Guyana, and he will hold an audience with the Bahamas' prime minister and governor general. Many of the countries affected by climate change are not polluters, Botchwey noted, and yet they feel the brunt of its effects. For Charles, who has long championed environmental causes, the tour is an opportunity to look forward, meet young people and discuss pressing issues like climate change, even as the past, and the question of what Britain owes for it, follows him at every stop.
