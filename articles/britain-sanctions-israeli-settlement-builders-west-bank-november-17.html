Britain Sanctions Israeli Settlement Builders in Push to Protect Two-State Solution

politics · 17 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said the message on the E1 settlement project was clear: “do not bid, do not build, and do not profit from the destruction of the two-state solution.”

Britain announced on Tuesday that it would impose sanctions on eight Israeli construction companies involved in building settlements in the occupied West Bank, as part of its effort to protect the idea of a two-state solution, Reuters reported.

The firms targeted for asset freezes and director disqualifications include Har Hevron Development Company Ltd, Hemdat Shomron Ltd and S.A.G. (Velvel) Construction and Development Ltd, which build and develop homes in the West Bank. The government said the measures would disrupt the companies' access to finance and their ability to support further construction in the territory. Settlements in the occupied West Bank are illegal under international law, and the British government has said they undermine efforts to secure a just and lasting peace in the Middle East.

The government announced the sanctions package shortly before the final deadlines for the E1 tender, a settlement project that would bisect the Israeli-occupied West Bank and cut it off from East Jerusalem, fragmenting the land Palestinians hope will form the heart of a state. “Protecting the two-state solution is the North Star of this Government’s approach,” British foreign secretary Ed Miliband said in a statement. “That is why my message on E1 is clear: do not bid, do not build, and do not profit from the destruction of the two-state solution.”

As part of the measures announced on Tuesday, the government also imposed sanctions on Tzav 9, an Israeli activist group which it said had regularly blocked humanitarian aid trucks delivering food, water and fuel to the Gaza Strip.

The sanctions are the latest in a series of measures by Britain targeting the settlement policy, and they underscore Israel’s growing diplomatic isolation among some traditional allies, Reuters reported. The BBC reported that the package is the first since the government announced a tougher approach to Israeli actions in the territory. While earlier measures were coordinated with international partners, Britain acted alone on Tuesday in sanctioning the Israeli companies.

Britain’s relationship with Israel has deteriorated significantly over the past three years because of disagreements over the way that Israel has conducted its war against Hamas and the Israeli government’s plans to expand settlements in the occupied West Bank.

The tension spilled into the open in September, when Israel threatened to close the British Consulate building in East Jerusalem in response to London’s decision to impose trade sanctions on Israeli settlements in the West Bank. British and Israeli diplomats last month reached a last-minute agreement to avoid the closure of the consulate.

Britain joined several other countries in 2025 in recognising a Palestinian state as a means of advancing the two-state solution, and in September announced plans to block imports from Israeli settlements in the West Bank. In 2025, Britain also imposed sanctions on two far-right Israeli cabinet ministers, accusing them of repeatedly inciting violence against Palestinians in the West Bank.

The Israeli embassy in London did not immediately respond to a request for comment, Reuters reported.

The timing of Tuesday’s announcement is the point. E1 is the settlement project that, more than any other, would change the map: building there would split the West Bank in two and separate it from East Jerusalem, the city Palestinians hope will be the capital of a future state. Miliband has said the E1 development would render a two-state solution unviable. By announcing the sanctions just before the final tender deadlines, London is aiming its message not only at the construction firms already building in the West Bank, but at any company considering a bid for E1 — do not bid, do not build, do not profit.

The move also marks a shift in method. Earlier British action on settlements focused on individuals linked to settler violence and on coordinated measures taken with allies. Tuesday’s package targets the companies that do the building, and it does so unilaterally. The government says the asset freezes and director disqualifications will disrupt the firms’ flows of finance and their ability to support building in the West Bank. The government is also developing separate legislation to ban the import of settlement goods and the advertising of settlement properties in Britain, the BBC reported.

What happens next: the E1 tender deadlines fall in the coming days, and the first test of the sanctions will be whether any company still bids. The longer test is whether the measures bite in practice — asset freezes and director disqualifications only work if the targeted firms rely on British finance or British-based directors, and London acted alone this time, so much will depend on whether other allies follow. The September plan to block settlement imports is still being turned into law, and the consulate dispute showed how quickly each British step can draw an Israeli response.

Why it matters: the two-state solution has been declared dead many times, but it remains the stated policy of Britain and most of its allies, and E1 is the project that would make it hardest to revive. By sanctioning the builders rather than only the violent settlers, and by doing it days before the tender deadlines, London is trying to raise the cost of the project before the concrete is poured. If the sanctions deter bidders, they will have done something no statement has managed. If they do not, the next step — an import ban, and pressure on allies to join — will show whether Britain is willing to go further than words.
