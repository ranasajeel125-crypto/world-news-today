World Court to Rule in Rohingya Genocide Case on 19 November

world · 3 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Judges will start reading out the judgment on 19 November at 8:00 GMT — only the third time ever the court has handed down a judgment in a genocide case.

The International Court of Justice will rule on 19 November in its landmark case against the government of Myanmar, which is accused of committing genocide against the minority Muslim Rohingya, the court said on Tuesday.

ICJ judges will start reading out their judgment on 19 November at 8:00 GMT. The case at the ICJ, also known as the World Court, was brought by Gambia in 2019.

Gambia argued that a 2017 offensive by Myanmar government forces that forced at least 730,000 Rohingya into neighbouring Bangladesh constituted a genocide. Rohingya refugees recounted instances of killings, mass rape and arson, and a UN fact-finding commission has concluded the offensive included “genocidal acts”.

Myanmar denies accusations of genocide and has said the offensive was a legitimate counter-terrorism campaign.

This will be the first time in over a decade, and only the third time ever, that the ICJ hands down a judgment in a genocide case. The outcome is likely to shape future genocide cases at the ICJ, including South Africa's 2023 case accusing Israel of committing genocide in Gaza, a charge Israel denies.

The ICJ is the UN's highest court and deals with disputes between states and alleged breaches of international treaties including the 1948 Genocide Convention. Genocide has rarely been proven in international courts because judges have to find crimes were committed with the specific intent “to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group”. ICJ rulings are final and without appeal.

When the judges delivered their verdict on Thursday, November 19, it was historic. The International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar had committed genocide against the Rohingya minority ethnic group. After seven years of deliberations, ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa delivered the long-awaited ruling in a verdict which took three hours to read out at the Peace Palace in The Hague. Nine of the 15 judges found that Myanmar had committed genocide, and the court also voted in favour of a landmark reparations order against the country.

The ruling, spanning 191 pages, found that Myanmar's security forces "killed thousands of Rohingya" in Rakhine State, committed widespread sexual violence against them, as well as acts of "extreme violence against children", and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to escape in large numbers to Bangladesh. The judges determined that "the estimate of 10,000 Rohingya violently killed during those clearance operations is supported by the evidence." By a majority of its judges, the court found that the country had committed six violations of the Genocide Convention, adopted by the United Nations in 1948 in the aftermath of World War II and the Holocaust.

The court found Myanmar responsible for three of the five categories of genocidal acts listed in the Genocide Convention. The judges concluded that Myanmar's actions in 2017 had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Muslim minority population, meeting the legal threshold for genocide. In the absence of a genocidal plan or a written order for destruction, The Gambia had relied on circumstantial evidence, and the judges found that the "only reasonable inference" from the pattern of killings, mass executions, widespread sexual violence, the burning of hundreds of villages and the spread of hate speech was an intent to destroy a substantial part of the group.

The ruling is the first time the ICJ has held a state responsible for genocide. It is also only the fourth time since the Nazi Holocaust that an international court has ruled that the legal threshold to prove genocide has been met, and the court's first major ruling on the Genocide Convention in more than a decade. The decision is final and cannot be appealed.

The court ordered Myanmar to cease all acts of violence and to investigate those culpable. Myanmar must take immediate and effective steps to investigate all persons suspected of culpability, prosecute and punish the guilty, and provide guarantees that this will never happen again. Importantly, the court ruled that Myanmar must institute restitution and reparations — something campaigners have long pushed for. The court said it could not determine the amount of compensation, but ordered Myanmar to submit a report within two years outlining a plan, and to allow the safe and dignified return of Rohingya refugees. The Gambia had also sought full citizenship for the Rohingya and their right to return home.

The case was brought by The Gambia, a predominantly Muslim West African nation, in November 2019, backed by the 57-member Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. Gambia's justice minister, Dawda Jallow, who was in The Hague for the ruling, said: "The victory belongs to the Rohingya. They survived, they testified, and today they were believed." Arsalan Suleman, partner at legal firm Foley Hoag and lead counsel for The Gambia, said the ruling "represents an important step toward accountability and honours the courage and perseverance of the victims and survivors who have spent years seeking justice."

In Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, where hundreds of thousands of Rohingya refugees live in camps, refugees held placards ahead of the verdict. "This is a huge day for the Rohingya people and all people of Myanmar," said Matthew Smith, a campaigner with the advocacy group Fortify Rights, who stood in the Peace Palace along with Rohingya survivors and the Gambian delegation as the ruling was read out. He said the ruling "will provide much-needed momentum to efforts to hold the perpetrators and architects of the genocide individually criminally accountable."

The ruling also resolves many uncertainties surrounding the very definition of genocide, the specific intent that characterises it and how that intent can be proven. The court's reasoning shows that genocide is not a one-off event, but a slow progression that leads to the destruction of a group — in this case, the Rohingya, who the court said had been persecuted for decades.

What happens next is uncertain. The ICJ cannot enforce its rulings, but they do carry weight. Myanmar is legally obliged to comply with the judgment, which is final and cannot be appealed, but questions remain over whether it will do so. If Myanmar refuses to implement the ruling, The Gambia could refer the matter to the UN Security Council and seek action under Article 94 of the UN Charter. The two-year deadline to report on reparations does not require Myanmar to complete all reparations or pay compensation by then, but it must outline the measures it is taking.

The decision is also said to have implications far beyond Myanmar, including for South Africa's case accusing Israel of genocide in Gaza, filed in 2023. For the Rohingya, the verdict is a landmark: after nearly a decade since the 2017 crackdown forced hundreds of thousands to flee, the world's highest court has ruled that what happened to them was genocide.
