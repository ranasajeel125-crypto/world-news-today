Democrats Win Control of US House in Midterm Elections, Projected to Retake Chamber from Republicans

politics · 4 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

CNN and Decision Desk HQ projected a Democratic House majority on Tuesday night; control of the Senate had not been projected at the time of the House call.

Democrats will win back control of the US House of Representatives in Tuesday's midterm elections, CNN and Decision Desk HQ projected, ending Republican control of the chamber and setting up divided government for the final two years of President Donald Trump's term.

CBS News projected Democrats would win at least 218 seats — the number needed for a majority in the 435-seat chamber — with Republicans on at least 160, the BBC reported. The final size of the Democratic majority will take longer to settle as counting continues in western states.

The result positions House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries to become speaker when the new Congress convenes in January. Democrats pitched the midterm elections as an opportunity to elect lawmakers who would serve as a check on the Trump administration, while the White House has said it expects Democrats to increase oversight of the president's Cabinet.

Among the early flips, Democrat Jamie Ager won North Carolina's 11th Congressional District, the first House seat to change hands in the cycle, and Democrat Rebecca Bennett defeated incumbent Republican Thomas Kean Jr. in New Jersey's 7th District, according to live results coverage.

Control of the Senate had not been projected at the time of the House call. Democrats had also won Senate races in Georgia, North Carolina and New Hampshire, and were running tight races in battlegrounds including Kansas, Texas, Ohio and Michigan, according to live results coverage.

Responding to the Democratic gains on Tuesday night, President Donald Trump said: "I wasn't on the ballot."

The scale of the Democratic victory grew as counting continued. Democrats were projected to win at least 224 seats and were leading in about a dozen others, according to USA Today, harnessing voters' frustration over high costs to win seats deeper in Republican territory than election forecasters had predicted. By the following day, Jeffries said Democrats expected to win at least 255 House seats once all vote counts were final, giving the party what he described as a historic majority. A week later, the Associated Press reported that Democrats had won 257 of the House's 435 seats and Republicans 171, with seven races still to be called.

The wave materialised early on November 3, as Democrats flipped an open seat in Kentucky and won seats in Florida that had been redrawn to favour Republicans. As the night went on, Democrats won races that had not seemed competitive until the final days of the campaign. In North Carolina's 9th Congressional District, voters ousted Republican Representative Richard Hudson, the chairman of the National Republican Congressional Committee. Democrats swept all the competitive seats in Pennsylvania, Iowa, Texas, California, Wisconsin and New Jersey, and won in at least 21 districts that Donald Trump had won by double digits or more — a reach into deep-red territory that the party's campaign chief described as unprecedented in the 21st century.

The outgoing 119th House had 218 Republicans, 214 Democrats, one independent and two vacancies. All 435 seats in the lower chamber were on the ballot in Tuesday's midterm race. Democrats had needed to net only three seats to take back the majority, and they cleared that mark and then some, with the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee reporting that 254 races had been called in its favour by the morning after the election, with 35 races too close to call or outstanding.

Jeffries celebrated the result at an election-night event and again at a press conference in Washington on November 4, calling the results an "American tsunami" and promising to deliver on affordability. "The American people are eager for a Congress that will again be an independent and co-equal branch," the party's leaders said, arguing that voters had sent a message that they wanted a change in direction. Jeffries, who is set to become the first Black Speaker of the House when the new Congress takes office in January, said the results showed Americans wanted a change in direction, and promised an affordability focus for the new majority.

The victory also extended to the Senate. Democrats claimed a majority in the Senate as well as the House, taking control of both chambers of Congress. The party won Senate races in Georgia, North Carolina and New Hampshire on election night, and later won in Alaska, where Democrat Mary Peltola defeated Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan, giving the party a commanding Senate majority.

For Jeffries, the path to the speaker's chair is now clear. House Democrats were due to vote to choose their leaders for the new Congress, with Jeffries expected to win the party's nomination for speaker, facing no declared challenger. If he wins the full House vote on January 3, the New York Democrat, who succeeded Nancy Pelosi as his party's House leader in 2023, would become the first Black speaker in United States history. He would replace outgoing Republican Speaker Mike Johnson of Louisiana, who has held the post since 2023. The position would make Jeffries third in line for the US presidency, should the president and the vice president be removed from office.

The 120th Congress is slated to be sworn in on January 3, 2027. At noon on that day, the old Congress ends and the new one begins, and in the House chamber the speaker vote comes first. Democrats will also vote on the rest of their leadership team: Katherine Clark of Massachusetts is running for majority leader, which would make her the first woman to hold the post, and Pete Aguilar of California is running for majority whip. With a large majority behind him, Jeffries is unlikely to face a contested fight for the gavel.

The result reshapes the final two years of President Trump's term. With Democrats in control of both chambers, the White House faces a Congress able to increase oversight of the president's Cabinet, block Republican legislation and investigate the administration. Trump, responding to the Democratic gains on Tuesday night, said: "I wasn't on the ballot." The midterm outcome, driven in large part by voters' frustration over high costs, ends two years of Republican control of the House under President Trump and gives Democrats the power to set the legislative agenda in the chamber for the first time since 2023.
