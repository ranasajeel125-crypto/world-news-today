Shops Looted in Frankfurt as Germany's Gen Z Protests Turn Violent

world · 1 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Frankfurt police reported “violent riots” in the city centre, smashed shop windows and Molotov cocktails thrown at emergency services.

Rioters looted shops in central Frankfurt late on Saturday and clashed with police there and in other German cities after largely peaceful mass protests over the cost of living, the BBC reported.

Thousands had earlier joined demonstrations organised by the campaign group Gen Z regelt, meaning "Gen Z takes charge", in 34 cities including Berlin, Frankfurt, Munich, Cologne and Hamburg, protesting underfunded schools, the high cost of living, public spending cuts and compulsory military service. The movement called for greater investment in education, healthcare, climate protection and social services, affordable housing and a fairer distribution of the burden, and opposed a return to military conscription at a time when the government is raising defence spending. "We are here today to fight for peace and freedom, rather than for war and coercion," Gen Z regelt spokesperson Lina Friedrich said at a rally in Berlin. "What matters most to us is better support for the health and education systems. We are against compulsory military service and we are in favour of a fairer distribution of the burden."

Turnout fell short of what the organisers had hoped. By Saturday morning, nearly 180,000 people had signed up to take part, and initial projections put the expected turnout at about 182,000, but the organisers estimated that around 120,000 people actually participated. Authorities in Frankfurt, Munich and Berlin reported as many as 18,000 protesters, roughly half of what the organisers had estimated on their website.

While the daytime protests were largely peaceful, the demonstrations turned violent as night fell. In a post on X, Frankfurt police said that "violent riots" were occurring in the city centre, that the windows of several shops had been smashed and that Molotov cocktails had been thrown at emergency services. Around 8,000 people had marched peacefully in Frankfurt earlier in the afternoon, but after the main demonstration ended, small masked groups moved through the city centre, smashing shop windows and entering businesses along the Zeil, one of Germany's busiest shopping streets.

A large section of Frankfurt's main shopping street was closed off by police, AFP reported, and several arrests were made. In later updates, officials said the windows of a gun shop had been smashed and that two weapons had been seized after a suspect was arrested, while a Reuters witness reported at least two stores looted and several more damaged. According to Hesse's interior minister, 17 shops were damaged and five looted, 54 suspects were arrested and more than ten officers were injured. A trainer shop and a perfume store were also targeted. Frankfurt's police chief, Stefan Muller, spoke of a new dimension of violence and a turning point for the force, saying officers were attacked in ambushes with pyrotechnic launchers and Molotov cocktails, and protesters hit police with stones, injuring several officers, one of them severely. Police appealed for photographs and video recordings to assist investigations into property damage, theft and assaults on officers.

Elsewhere, police used water cannon to disperse protesters who targeted emergency workers with fireworks, stones and bottles. In Cologne, police fired water cannon after saying officers had been targeted with fireworks; in Berlin, demonstrators threw bottles and eggs while officers used pepper spray in some places; and in Hamburg, barricades were set alight. In Bonn, a police officer sustained minor injuries.

Roman Poseck, interior minister of Hesse state, of which Frankfurt is the biggest city, said that around 100 clearly identified left-wing extremists had infiltrated the Frankfurt demonstration, calling its ending shameful. German authorities said Saturday's demonstrations, which coincided with Halloween festivities, had been largely peaceful, but warned of attempts by extremist groups to exploit the movement, and the federal interior ministry said parts of Germany's far-left extremist scene and some groups linked to foreign extremist movements had joined efforts to mobilise support. The Gen Z regelt movement explicitly rejected violence, and Germany's national school student umbrella organisation distanced itself from the initiative and announced plans for a separate protest on 20 November.

One of the protesters' central grievances is the new military service law. The Wehrdienstmodernisierungsgesetz took effect on 1 January 2026, and service stays voluntary, so the law is not a return to conscription. But if too few volunteers sign up, the Bundestag could later decide on a needs-based compulsory service, and protesters oppose both that step and the screening that would feed it. All 18-year-old men must fill in a questionnaire, and it is voluntary for women, and from 1 July 2027 men born in 2008 or later must attend a medical exam.

The unrest in Germany follows similar youth-led mass protests in France, where more than 320,000 people also demonstrated on Saturday, around 70,000 of them in Paris, in a third weekend of protests driven by complaints about an under-resourced education system, high living costs and an unpopular government. In Switzerland, hundreds of demonstrators clashed with police in Zurich, setting barricades ablaze before officers responded with water cannon, tear gas and rubber bullets. Demonstrations also took place in Portugal, Poland, Spain, Albania, Italy and Serbia, where student-led activism helped unseat the country's longtime leader at the ballot box. The wave of youth-led protests across Europe this year has been driven by the same complaints: rising living costs, inadequate educational facilities and government spending priorities that young people say leave them carrying the burden.
