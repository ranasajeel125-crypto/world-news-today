Iran Warns Ships Against ‘Fake American Routes’ as Tanker Attacks Escalate in Strait of Hormuz

world · 17 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Iran warned shipping against using “fake American routes” through the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, as more tankers were reported hit, Bahrain issued a fresh threat alert and a third US aircraft carrier arrived in the Gulf.

Iran’s armed forces warned shipping on Tuesday against using what they called “fake American routes” through the Strait of Hormuz, as a new wave of tanker attacks, a fresh security alert in Bahrain and the arrival of a third American aircraft carrier pushed the eight-month-old US-Iran war towards a new escalation.

The warning came in a statement from the Iranian army carried in Al Jazeera’s live coverage on Tuesday morning, as the channel reported that attacks on shipping in the strait were escalating. Bahrain issued a “potential threat” alert, and Iran and Oman discussed maritime security in the strait, according to the live updates. Al Jazeera also reported that tankers had been hit in the Strait of Hormuz and that a drone had raised the alarm at a gas field in Iraq.

Iran’s navy said on Monday that a third oil tanker had been disabled by a naval mine east of the Strait of Hormuz in the past 24 hours. The vessel, it said, had been sailing along an unauthorised route with its navigation systems switched off. The Revolutionary Guards’ navy said separately that two tankers had struck mines in the strait on Monday. Iran says the strait remains closed and will stay closed for as long as it deems necessary, and has warned shipping companies again not to sail unauthorised routes or switch off their navigation systems.

None of the Iranian claims about the mines has been independently verified, and the Guards did not identify any of the vessels by name, flag, owner or operator. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre said separately that a tanker sailing out of the strait on Monday was struck by an unidentified projectile, starting a fire in its engine room, and that its crew was evacuated. Iranian state television, as quoted by Bloomberg, put the number of vessels that had caught fire in the strait since Sunday at six.

The Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war, said in its assessment that the mine claims appeared designed to undermine shipping companies’ confidence in American escort operations and push vessels towards Iranian waters, and that it had seen no sign Iran had laid new mines on the southern route. US Central Command said in August that American forces had completely cleared the strait of mines.

The escalation at sea came as the USS Theodore Roosevelt arrived in the Gulf region on Sunday, joining the USS George H.W. Bush and the USS George Washington — the third American aircraft carrier in the region. Iran has ratcheted up its threats against US forces as the carrier group assembled, warning shipping companies against using the US-backed southern route through the strait, which it described as fully closed and a “minefield of fire”.

The US Embassy in Manama issued a security alert on Sunday evening urging Americans in Bahrain to exercise heightened vigilance, citing the threat of attacks by the Houthis and Iranian efforts to target American officials and facilities, and warning of possible flight cancellations and airspace closures. The alert followed two Iranian drone attacks on a US military radar site in Bahrain in three days — on Friday and again early on Sunday — which Bahrain said its air defences intercepted.

CNN reported on Tuesday that new US intelligence indicated Iran was developing plans that could target senior Trump administration officials, including with a so-called dirty bomb. The report added to the sense in Washington that Tehran was preparing to widen the conflict beyond the Gulf.

The attacks on shipping have become the war’s most persistent front. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said last Thursday that two supertankers carrying more than four million barrels of oil between them had caught fire overnight in the strait after crossing what the Guards called an unauthorised southern route, and warned that any ship cooperating with US forces on the same route would “meet the same fate”. The Guards have characterised the US-escorted southern route as illegal since declaring the strait closed on 26 October. October recorded 37 vessel attacks, the highest monthly total of the conflict, according to Lloyd’s List.

The war began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, and the two sides have been locked in a stand-off over the Strait of Hormuz, the world’s most important oil chokepoint, ever since. A preliminary agreement in June to end the war and reopen the strait collapsed within weeks after Iranian attacks on shipping resumed and the US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports. Diplomacy has stalled since then: Iran’s negotiators had been discussing a proposal to reopen the strait if the US met a list of conditions, but President Donald Trump said this week that the United States no longer needed a deal and pulled Vice-President JD Vance, who had been leading the talks, off the negotiations.

Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araqchi, was due in Oman on Tuesday for talks with his counterpart, according to Iranian state media — the only diplomatic channel still visibly open. Oman holds the southern coast of the Strait of Hormuz and has played a role as mediator in the crisis. Tehran has said a deal is still possible and that its two-day plan to reopen the strait has the backing of the ECO regional grouping, but Washington has not answered.

What happens next will turn on whether the third carrier’s arrival steadies the escort route or becomes the trigger for the retaliation Tehran has been threatening. With three American carriers in the region, Bahrain on alert, tankers burning and no talks scheduled, the space for a miscalculation — a mine that is real, a drone that gets through, a ship struck with its crew aboard — is growing with each exchange.

Why it matters: the strait is the world’s most important oil chokepoint, and every attack on a tanker there raises the cost of shipping, the price of oil and the risk that a war fought so far through claims and denials produces a casualty count neither side can walk back. The warning about “fake American routes” is also a message to the shipping industry itself: Tehran is telling owners and crews that American escorts cannot keep them safe, and Washington is betting its carriers prove otherwise. Until one side’s account of what is happening in the strait can be independently checked, the one fact both sides agree on is the most dangerous one: the tankers are still burning.
