Trump Says He and Putin Had 'Very Good Talks' on Ukraine, but Warns No Security Guarantees Without a Deal

world · 20 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

US President Donald Trump speaks at a White House news conference after his trip to Asia, where he met Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen.

US President Donald Trump said on Friday that he and Russian leader Vladimir Putin held "very good talks" on Ukraine during their meeting in China, but he said he did not want to discuss security guarantees for Ukraine without a deal. The remarks, made at a White House news conference after his trip to Asia, were his first public assessment of the talks since he returned home.

"We're having very good talks. I think there's a real good chance that we can get it done," Trump said at the news conference, where he had met with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meeting in Shenzhen, China, this week.

Trump said he had spoken to Putin, whom he called a friend, separately during the summit. He said the Russian leader had "clearly expressed his desire to see the war between Russia and Ukraine end by diplomatic means. And I spoke with our representatives, who are doing a good job, but it's a tough job. It's a tough job."

The president was blunt about the obstacle he sees between the two sides. "The problem is President (Volodymyr) Zelenskiy and President Putin have tremendous hatred, and that's never easy," he said, referring to the Ukrainian president and his Russian counterpart.

Trump's optimism was delivered on the same day that the war on the ground told a very different story. Hours after the three leaders issued a peace declaration following their meeting in China, Russia launched one of the largest missile and drone attacks of the war on Ukraine on Friday, pummelling electricity facilities and killing eight people, according to Reuters.

The attack struck Kyiv itself. Russian ballistic missiles hit three bridges over the Dnipro River in the capital, killing three people and injuring at least 15, the city's mayor said, in the biggest attack on the crossings linking the capital's two banks since the start of the war. France has called for a meeting of the UN Security Council after the strikes, and the Dutch government summoned the Russian ambassador after the country's embassy in Kyiv was slightly damaged when a projectile landed near the building.

The contrast between the summit's language and the strikes that followed it is now the central question hanging over the diplomacy. A peace declaration signed in Shenzhen was followed within hours by one of the heaviest bombardments of the war, aimed at the power grid Ukraine needs to get through the winter. For Kyiv and its European allies, that sequence is evidence that declarations alone change nothing on the battlefield.

What the Shenzhen meeting actually produced was modest. The three leaders agreed on a symbolic peace declaration, but they announced no plans for arms control talks long sought by Trump, and they showed no signs of advances on issues such as Iran, Ukraine and Taiwan, or on narrowing gaps over trade and artificial intelligence, according to a Reuters analysis of the summit.

Trump himself gave the meeting a glowing personal rating. He told NBC News that, "on a scale of zero to 10, I think it was a 12," offering the kind of upbeat assessment he often makes after such encounters. He has framed his willingness to engage directly with leaders such as Xi, Putin and North Korea's Kim Jong Un as decisive, high-profile diplomacy of a kind his predecessors avoided.

The summit came at a difficult moment for Trump at home. He went into the unprecedented three-way meeting seeking to reassert himself internationally after being politically weakened, with his Republican Party's loss of control of Congress in this month's midterm elections. Some analysts said the outcome of the Shenzhen summit may actually diminish Trump's stature on the world stage as he enters the lame-duck phase of his presidency, while bolstering two of Washington's main adversaries.

Xi was able to burnish his diplomatic credentials by hosting a big-power gathering, greeting Trump directly at the airport with an elaborate military welcome in a gesture without precedent. Putin was able to present Russia, a comparative economic lightweight, as appearing on par with the world's two most powerful leaders without making major concessions.

The three-way meeting drew comparisons, particularly in Russia, with the 1945 Yalta Conference between the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union that formalised spheres of influence and set the stage for the post-World War Two order. The comparison unnerved smaller economies, which worry that the fate of countries such as Ukraine could be discussed over their heads by the great powers.

The question of security guarantees, which Trump set aside on Friday, is the issue Kyiv and its European allies care about most. A day earlier, leaders of the Coalition of the Willing met in Berlin with Zelenskiy, NATO chief Mark Rutte and US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, to discuss robust, legally binding guarantees that would take effect after a ceasefire, and a possible multinational force deployment. Britain announced a 260 million pound package including more than 1,000 long-range strike drones, and the European Commission proposed a 45 billion euro loan to fill Ukraine's funding gap.

Trump's position, that guarantees should not be discussed without a deal, leaves that work in limbo. Ukraine wants guarantees agreed in advance, so that any ceasefire cannot simply be broken later; the US president wants a deal first, and is wary of committing American backing before one exists. Until that gap is bridged, the European planning in Berlin can only prepare for a settlement whose shape remains undefined.

Russia's all-out invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 has led to Europe's bloodiest conflict since World War Two. Nearly five years on, the war has settled into a grinding pattern of front-line fighting and long-range strikes on cities and energy infrastructure, with diplomacy repeatedly producing declarations that the fighting then contradicts.

What happens next depends on the talks Trump says are under way through his representatives, and on whether the "very good talks" in Shenzhen produce anything more concrete than the peace declaration. There is no published timetable, and no date has been set for a further meeting of the three leaders. Ukraine, meanwhile, heads into winter with its electricity facilities under attack, and its allies in Europe are pressing for the guarantees Trump has so far declined to discuss.

Why it matters: Trump's upbeat account of his talks with Putin is the most hopeful signal from Washington about an end to the war in months, and his claim that Putin wants a diplomatic end to the fighting will be tested against what Russia does next. But the missile strikes that followed the peace declaration within hours, and the president's refusal to discuss security guarantees without a deal first, show how far the diplomacy still has to travel. The test of Shenzhen is not what was said in the room, but whether the next weeks bring fewer strikes on Ukrainian cities, or more of them.
