Saudi-Led Coalition Intercepts Three Houthi Missiles Headed for Riyadh

world · 21 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (Middle East Desk)

Emergency alerts sounded in the Saudi capital for the third night in a row as the war with Yemen's Houthis escalates.

The Saudi-led coalition intercepted three ballistic missiles headed towards Riyadh in the early hours of Saturday, Al Jazeera reported, after Saudi Arabia issued and quickly lifted emergency alerts for the capital for the third night in a row.

The interception was reported in Al Jazeera's live coverage in the early hours of Saturday, 21 November, in a recap of the overnight developments. The alerts were the third consecutive night of warnings for Riyadh, coming two days after a Houthi attack damaged the Public Investment Fund Tower in the capital's King Abdullah Financial District.

Thursday's strike on the tower — the headquarters of Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund and the tallest building in the country — blew out windows across seven to eight floors and bent parts of its metal facade, according to Reuters, and the financial district was closed to visitors. No one was injured, Saudi civil defence said, but the strike was the second major landmark hit in the Saudi capital in 11 days, after the Houthis said they had hit the Aramco Tower in the financial district on 8 November.

Friday brought a further escalation on both sides. The coalition said its air defences intercepted five drones and one ballistic missile on Friday morning — four drones and the missile over the kingdom's southern region, and a fifth drone over the Riyadh region. The coalition also said it had struck the Houthi-held Red Sea port of Hodeidah and targets in Sanaa, Saada and Taiz, hitting facilities it said were used to smuggle weapons and transport ballistic missiles.

The Houthis threatened a Saudi power station, Red Sea ports including Jeddah, and Riyadh's financial centre after the coalition's strikes on Hodeidah and Sanaa, Al Jazeera reported. Yemen's internationally recognised government defended the strikes on Hodeidah port, accusing the Houthis of using its facilities to receive smuggled Iranian weapons and conduct military operations.

The renewed fighting has shattered a 2022 UN-brokered truce that had largely halted the exchange of attacks between the Saudi-led coalition, which backs Yemen's internationally recognised government, and the Houthis, who control the most populous parts of the country. The fighting broke out again after the Houthis captured strategic areas along Yemen's Red Sea coast last month, and it has become another volatile front in the wider regional confrontation involving Iran, whose allies the Houthis are.

The human cost in Yemen is mounting fast. The United Nations children's agency, UNICEF, said more than 330,000 people, including an estimated 174,000 children, have been displaced since the beginning of the renewed military escalations. The World Health Organization said the death toll from the latest escalation, which began in August, had reached 2,260, with 10,519 wounded as of November 16, and warned the number of casualties was likely to increase as renewed fighting continued around densely populated areas, particularly in Taiz and Lahij.

The campaign has widened beyond the battlefield in recent weeks. The Houthis have targeted Saudi airports, oil facilities and office buildings, grounding flights and setting global oil markets on edge. Attacks on Riyadh airport in October killed 18 people over four days, Saudi authorities said, including 13 on October 10. The group has also targeted the kingdom's oil infrastructure, including the East-West pipeline that Riyadh uses to reroute crude to its Yanbu port on the Red Sea, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most important oil chokepoint.

Earlier in the week, Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council issued a decision designating the Houthi group a terrorist organisation, saying anyone who cooperates with the group will face prosecution and punishment, with penalties that could include execution or prison terms of 30 years or more. The designation came as Yemeni government forces announced about 715 air strikes against the Houthis on several fronts on Thursday.

What happens next will be measured in alerts and in retaliation. Three straight nights of emergency warnings in Riyadh show the capital's defences are being tested almost daily, while the coalition's strikes on Hodeidah, Sanaa, Saada and Taiz show it is trying to hit the missile and drone capabilities behind the attacks. The harder question is whether either side has a route back to the truce that held for four years, or whether the war that resumed in August is now settling into a longer and more destructive phase.

Why it matters: three ballistic missiles aimed at the Saudi capital on a third straight night of alerts, two days after a missile damaged the kingdom's tallest skyscraper, shows the Houthi campaign against Riyadh is not letting up. Each interception tests Saudi air defences that have stopped many, but not all, of the missiles fired — and each night of warnings brings the war in Yemen closer to the heart of the kingdom's financial and political centre.
