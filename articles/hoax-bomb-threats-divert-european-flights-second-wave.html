Hoax Bomb Threats Divert European Flights for Second Time in a Week

world · 6 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

An Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military air base; no explosives were found on any plane searched.

Airlines across Europe received a new wave of bomb threats on Thursday, the second this week, forcing flights to make unplanned landings and others to be escorted by fighter jets, though none of the threats has proved genuine, Reuters reported.

At least 21 aircraft were targeted on Thursday, the BBC reported, with some counts putting the figure higher. An Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome, carrying 151 passengers and six crew, was diverted to the Evreux-Fauville air base in Normandy after a suspected bomb threat and landed safely. France's air force said it scrambled military aircraft as soon as the threat was reported, and the office of French Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said fighter jets were sent to intercept the plane and escort it to the Evreux airbase, west of Paris, after the pilot did not cooperate with authorities as expected. A bomb squad was in place at the base and the passengers and crew were moved to safety.

Italian fighter jets escorted a Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome, which landed as scheduled, after an anonymous bomb-threat email was sent to Aeroporti di Roma and other companies at Fiumicino airport. Bulgarian jets escorted three Ryanair flights bound for Sofia. A Wizz Air flight from Milan to Warsaw was rerouted to Vienna, and Czech police said four planes heading to Prague had been targeted. Flights bound for Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, France, Italy, Poland and Romania were affected.

No explosive device has been found on any aircraft searched this week, and no one has been injured. The British government called the incidents "malicious hoax threats", and threats received by Heathrow and Birmingham airports were assessed as not credible.

Some of the threat emails were signed by a group calling itself the "Fighters of the Male State", threatening to "turn the skies red". European officials warned there is currently no evidence linking the group to the threats, and no one has claimed responsibility. Officials have not attributed blame to any state or organisation, and as of 11 November, responsibility for the threats had not been publicly established, and it remained unclear whether the incidents shared a common source.

The disruption follows a first wave on Tuesday, when at least seven flights and a cruise ship were targeted, and a Monday incident in which a Wizz Air flight from London to Sofia was diverted to Budapest after a bomb threat. No explosives were found in any of those cases either. On 3 November, bomb threats affected at least seven flights and a cruise ship in Europe, and on 5 November, further threats disrupted nearly 30 passenger flights, leading to diversions, security searches and the deployment of military aircraft.

The threats continued on Friday. At least 17 flights had been disrupted by midday, and the European Union's transport commissioner, Apostolos Tzitzikostas, said there were 11 more on Friday, after Eurocontrol, the European air traffic authority, said it was aware of bomb threats against 14 flights on Thursday and seven on Tuesday. Eurocontrol said the threats had caused minimal disruption to aviation overall, noting that the 14 flights on Thursday were only 0.05% of all of the nearly 26,300 flights across the European network that day.

On Friday, Hungary said it had scrambled fighter jets three times to escort aircraft through its airspace following bomb alerts, including a Wizz Air flight from Brussels to Sofia and flights from Prague to Bucharest and Cyprus. Prague airport confirmed that nine flights had been affected by bomb threats on Friday morning, including flights from Denmark, Spain and Britain, and departures to the Netherlands and Britain, in addition to the ones to Cyprus, Italy and Romania. A Ryanair flight landed in Lisbon at 8am after a bomb threat was made by phone to the authorities, and a security operation was mounted as soon as the plane landed. The control tower at Fiumicino received an alert about a device aboard a Wizz Air flight to Rome from Paris, and the flight was diverted to Venice, where police cleared the area around the plane. In Denmark, police deployed to Copenhagen airport for a second consecutive day as a precaution and deemed there was no danger to passengers.

In Poland, authorities received a warning about a potentially dangerous object on board a Polish Airlines flight already taxiing on the runway at Warsaw Chopin Airport. The flight to Paris was prevented from taking off and passengers disembarked, but a search found nothing dangerous on board. A LOT flight from London Heathrow to Warsaw was forced to land at Warsaw-Modlin, the Polish capital's other airport, following a threat, and nothing was found on board. In Spain, police said three emailed threats arrived within 20 minutes, involving two Air Europa departures from Madrid to Paris and four inbound Iberia flights from Rome and Milan. Several aircraft were halted or returned to their stands in Madrid and passengers disembarked while the planes were inspected. In Romania, the intelligence agency said two planes had received bomb threats, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a layover in Bucharest.

The wave then spread beyond aviation. On 6 November, Ireland's public broadcaster RTE and Bulgaria's Nova TV received emailed bomb threats. On 11 November, approximately 6,300 Italian schools received emailed warnings, prompting evacuations and closures, and schools in Scotland, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland also received similar messages. On 12 November, a further wave of emailed threats reached 3,778 schools across Italy, with 77 schools evacuated, and a bomb threat targeting Calais-Frethun station in France caused domestic and international rail travel to be suspended in the morning.

Air France-KLM chief executive Ben Smith said on Thursday he had never previously seen threats reported in such a coordinated fashion. Aviation security experts say the apparent coordination and timing of the current incidents is unusual, though bomb threats against planes are not new. The threats occurred amid wider concern over disruption to European infrastructure, including cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems and drone incursions, and security experts were due to meet in Brussels the following week to discuss measures needed to deal with the rash of bomb threats.

For passengers, the experience was frightening even though no explosives were found. A bomb threat against an aircraft must be treated as genuine until it is proved otherwise, which means diversions, fighter jet escorts, security searches and delays, and for the airlines it means cancelled flights, extra fuel, crew time and the cost of inspecting aircraft. For the authorities, the challenge is to respond quickly enough to keep people safe, without allowing hoaxers to paralyse the aviation system. So far, the system has held: no one has been injured, no explosives have been found, and the vast majority of flights have operated normally. But until the source of the threats is found, Europe's skies will remain on edge.
