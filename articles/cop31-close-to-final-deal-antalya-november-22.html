COP31 Close to Final Deal as New Draft Lands in Antalya After Two Days of Overtime

climate · 22 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Climate Desk)

Delegates at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, where talks ran two days past their Friday deadline before a new draft deal was put forward early on Sunday.

Climate talks in Antalya approached a final deal in the early hours of Sunday after the COP31 presidency put forward a new package it said every country could agree on, ending two days of overtime in which the summit had looked close to collapse. "We believe that we have put forward a text which all the parties can agree upon," COP31 President Murat Kurum told a late-night plenary session, adding that the conference would reconvene later on Sunday morning to approve the deal.

The deal is not done yet. Delegations have until 0300 GMT on Sunday to raise any objections to the package, with a session scheduled at 0600 GMT to begin approving it, according to Reuters. Under the United Nations climate process, decisions are generally taken by consensus, so a single formal objection can still stop a deal — and, as the presidency itself has acknowledged, there is always time for last-minute changes on the floor of the closing plenary. What changed overnight is that, after days of deadlock, the presidency believes it finally has a text that can pass.

Negotiators ran past a Friday deadline, straight through Saturday and into the early hours of Sunday. The new version of the deal text, published at 3am local time on Sunday, refined an earlier draft that had been heavily criticised by some delegations for lacking ambition because it contained no reference to ending the use of fossil fuels. It is the third version of the summit's main political text: a 94-paragraph document, following a 144-paragraph first draft and an 80-paragraph second iteration, according to Carbon Brief, which analysed the text as it was published.

The new text followed intensive consultations between Australia, which is leading the talks, Turkey, which is hosting the meeting, and nearly 200 delegations from many of the world's richest, poorest, largest and smallest nations, Reuters reported. Australia's task, supported by Turkey, has been to find a deal that balances the needs of nations most vulnerable to climate change with those whose economies depend on either the production or consumption of fossil fuels — the fault line that has defined this summit from its first day.

On the two questions that deadlocked the talks, the new text is a study in careful compromise. It still contains no direct reference to the transition away from fossil fuels, and no mention of the Brazil-led global roadmap on moving away from fossil fuels. But at the start of the text it "recalls... in its entirety" the outcome of the first global stocktake agreed at COP28 in Dubai, which included the call for a "just, orderly and equitable" transition away from fossil fuels. In the language of climate diplomacy, that recall keeps the Dubai commitment alive in the decision without restating it — a formula the presidency hopes oil-producing states can accept and ambitious states can live with.

On finance, the picture is similar. There is still no sign in the draft of the $120 billion annual target for adaptation finance that developing countries had sought and that the second draft dropped. Instead, the text "agrees to discuss... an implementation pathway" towards tripling support for adaptation under the climate finance work programme, and in a new addition it "recalls" that other countries are encouraged to provide or continue to provide climate finance "voluntarily". It encourages "continued efforts" to scale up the resources of the loss and damage fund, which compensates countries hit hardest by climate disasters.

The text keeps the presidencies' flagship electrification goal — raising electricity's share of total global energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, from roughly 21 percent today — though Carbon Brief described the wording as weak. As in the previous draft, it does not stipulate that the electricity should come from renewable sources, leaving the door open for fossil-fuelled power plants to count towards the target. On the science, the text reaffirms last year's agreement to "limit both the magnitude and duration" of the overshoot above 1.5 degrees Celsius, and decides to establish a "high-level dialogue" at COP32 on the science of 1.5C and overshoot. It also "acknowledges" that the "global transition towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development is irreversible", and decides to establish a two-year work programme on mountains and climate change.

Kurum said he believed concerns around fossil fuels had been addressed, as had those around helping developing countries finance their adaptations to climate change. But the road to this point was ugly. A draft deal published on Thursday removed earlier references to ending fossil fuel use, and observers said Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers had opposed the language. The same draft dropped the proposed $120 billion target for adaptation finance. By Saturday, the European Union's top climate official was warning that the summit would produce "a good deal or no deal at all". "It's either going to be a good deal or it's going to be no deal," EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra told reporters. "We will find out."

The frustration inside the conference hall spilled into the open on Friday night. During a late-night session, delegates passed a protest banner reading "failure", while a demonstrator interrupted Kurum as he spoke, shouting, "you are killing the future, you are killing the future". Kurum pleaded with the hall: "Yes, we are tired, and we have put in a great deal of effort — but we still have the capacity, right here in this hall, to convey truly meaningful messages to the world." Earlier, he had told reporters: "I call on every country to act responsibly. There is no benefit for anyone to ruin this process by getting hung up on words, phrases."

On Saturday evening, with the talks more than 24 hours into overtime, a group of EU member states, as well as Panama, were making a last-ditch effort to restore a fossil fuels mention to the text, against objections from petrostates that had wiped the term from the first revision. Panama's Environment Minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, said COP31 had been "hijacked" by petroleum. Africa Group members, meanwhile, were pushing for a stronger commitment from rich countries to support their management of climate change impacts after the $120 billion adaptation target was dropped.

The money at stake helps explain why the finance fight has been so bitter. The draft text itself acknowledges that adaptation finance needs are now running at $305 billion a year. The loss and damage fund, established at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, remains so underfunded it can hardly function, with $2.8 billion in requests to date against $486 million received, plus a 25 million euro pledge from the European Union at this year's summit. The second draft still backed efforts to "at least triple adaptation finance by 2035", but named no dollar target — and the third draft keeps it that way, deferring the detail to an "Antalya-to-Addis Ababa Roadmap" that pushes implementation to next year's COP32 summit in Ethiopia.

Some negotiators from developing countries suspected the delays themselves were a tactic. "This delay strategy which has been used at previous COPs could be so at the end when things are put on the table you accept because you are fed up, exhausted, and want to go home," said Isatou Camara, Gambia's finance negotiator. Brenda Mwale, Malawi's loss and damage negotiator, put the doubt more bluntly: "The fact there is loads of unfinished business at this COP shows there's a big problem here. The question is are we in a leadership crisis? Was there really a vision for this COP?" As workers dismantled catering stands and exhibits at the venue, delegations shuttled between meeting rooms to discuss tactics, while others rested as they awaited a new version of the negotiating text.

The science hanging over the talks has only grown darker. This year's COP is the first since the UN's environmental body confirmed that the rise in global temperature was set to blow past 1.5 degrees Celsius, the threshold all countries agreed over a decade ago to try not to exceed, and beyond which extreme weather impacts intensify. A UN report last month found the world is on track to exceed that limit. October 2026 was 1.8 degrees Celsius warmer than the mid-1800s, according to Berkeley Earth. The draft agreement acknowledges that projected overshoot and calls for faster deployment of technologies that remove greenhouse gases from the atmosphere to help bring temperatures back down — though some delegations opposed including carbon-removal technologies, arguing that doing so could open the door to continued fossil fuel use.

The talks have also been shaped by who is not in the room. The United States, the world's largest historical greenhouse gas emitter, is absent from the talks for the second year in a row after President Donald Trump ended cooperation on climate policy, calling global warming a hoax. The negotiations have been further complicated by war and rising living costs, which are competing for the attention of policymakers and the public. Recent climate summits have regularly overrun without collapsing, eventually producing agreements — but the last major breakdown in UN climate talks came in Copenhagen in 2009, when countries failed to adopt the Copenhagen Accord by consensus, a precedent every delegation in Antalya has had in mind.

What happens next will be decided within hours. If no delegation objects by 0300 GMT, the approval session due at 0600 GMT on Sunday will begin the formal adoption of the package, and the summit that was scheduled to end on Friday will finally close — with its main decisions, including the detailed finance roadmap, pushed to COP32 in Addis Ababa in November 2027. If an objection lands, the presidency faces a choice between reopening the text and risking the collapse Hoekstra warned of. Either way, the verdict on whether this package is a deal worth having will come from the countries it affects most: the small island states and least developed countries that need the money, and the petrostates that fought hardest over the words.

Why it matters: a climate summit that ends with a deal nobody loves is still a summit that ends with a deal — and after two days of overtime, protest banners reading "failure" and open talk of no deal at all, that is what Antalya now appears to have. But the price of consensus is visible in the text itself: no direct fossil fuel language, no dollar figure for adaptation, and the hardest choices deferred to a roadmap that leads to Addis Ababa. The 1.5C limit the world promised itself a decade ago is already set to be breached, and the money vulnerable countries need — $305 billion a year for adaptation alone — remains a promise rather than a plan. Whether the recall of the Dubai stocktake is enough to keep the transition away from fossil fuels alive, or whether it is the moment the words quietly slipped out of the process, will be decided not in Antalya but in what governments actually do next.
