Russia and Ukraine Say Emergency Alert Apps Hit by Cyber-Attacks

world · 2 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Ukraine's air force said the false nuclear alert disappeared and there was no radiation threat; Russia's ministry said it was working to restore its app.

Russia and Ukraine have each reported that their emergency alert apps were hacked within hours of one another, sending false warnings of nuclear and chemical attacks to civilians who rely on the systems for alerts during the war.

Ukraine's Air Force said late on Sunday, 1 November 2026, that the country's public air raid alert app had been hacked. The app for a short time showed Kyiv and other areas subject to a nuclear alert, but the abnormality later disappeared. "The air alert app appears to have been subjected to enemy influence," the air force said on Telegram. "There is no radiation threat! Stay calm and follow our updates on our channel!"

The false alerts began just before 11 p.m. local time on Sunday, warning of high radiation levels, a chemical threat and public disturbances in Kyiv, and of street combat in some areas. One message, written in Russian, said: "Hi! There will be a nuclear threat across Ukraine. Do not worry, the Oreshnik is incoming," a reference to Russia's intermediate-range ballistic missile, which is capable of carrying a nuclear warhead. Other messages were written in English with spelling and grammatical errors, and some mixed Russian and Ukrainian. The alerts startled people across the country, and air raid sirens sounded in streets, including in Zakarpattia in western Ukraine. The false alerts were cleared about an hour later.

Ukraine's state-run Centre for Countering Disinformation urged users to monitor other emergency channels on social media and rely on physical alerts while the cyberattack was being addressed. The Air Force's channel warned users not to click on links in suspicious notifications, amid warnings that the attack could involve a phishing operation. Ukraine's Centre for Strategic Communications, known as SPRAVDI, blamed Russia for the hack in a post on X, pointing to the use of the Russian language in one of the fake messages. Yurii Ihnat, a spokesman for Ukraine's air force command, told Ukraine's private 1+1 television that the cyberattack had no impact on Ukraine's security and defence operations because the app is separate from military command systems.

The developers of the app, which was made in collaboration between the Digital Transformation Ministry and the security technology company Ajax Systems, said in a statement that unauthorised persons had interfered with the application, that push notifications had been disabled and that the application had been temporarily suspended. By midnight local time, Ukraine's State Emergency Service said the nationwide alert system was back to operating normally, though citizens were told to keep monitoring information from local authorities and to listen for signals played on loudspeakers. At around 2 a.m., the Air Alert app said operations were gradually being restored while the display of targets was temporarily suspended, and that the team was implementing additional security measures to protect the app.

Hours later, Russia's emergencies ministry said its mobile app was hit by a massive cyberattack that resulted in false information being spread to the public, state news agency TASS quoted the ministry as saying early on Monday. The ministry was working to restore the app, TASS said, without providing additional details about the attack or the false information that was circulated.

In a statement, Russia's Emergency Ministry said hackers had used its mobile app to send a false alert across 33 of Russia's 89 regions at approximately 2:45 a.m. Moscow time. The fake message told Russians that nuclear warheads had been launched against all Russian regions and advised people to stock up on three weeks of food and shelter in their cellars. Reports of the alerts came from St. Petersburg to Tyumen, including Moscow and Belgorod. Atesh, a pro-Ukrainian group, posted a screenshot of the alert message on its Telegram channel early on Monday with the message: "The Ministry of Emergency Situations app has been hacked!" The Moscow ministry played down the impact, saying the app was not one of its main public warning system channels but a supplementary source of information, and that its main system was unaffected. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said Russian government agencies' electronic resources were under constant threat of cyberattacks, adding that the website of the president of the Russian Federation was subjected to cyberattacks many times a day, every day.

Neither report named a specific attacker, and Reuters said it was unable to verify which groups were responsible. A Russian Telegram channel said a hacking group called Hitcore had claimed responsibility for the breach of the Ukrainian system, but that could not be independently verified. There is no evidence linking the two countries' incidents, and it was not clear whether the attacks late on Sunday were related.

The incidents struck the warning systems that civilians on both sides rely on for alerts during the war, which has seen repeated strikes on infrastructure and, in recent days, Russian attacks that cut power to hundreds of thousands of Ukrainian households. Ukraine's Air Alert app is used across the country to warn people in real time of incoming Russian air attacks, and residents depend on it daily amid missile and drone strikes. The Russian app warns users of incoming drone attacks.

The timing added to the tension. The fake threats began on the same day as a localised ceasefire in the Russian-occupied Kherson region came to an end on Sunday. The truce, which the United States said was brokered by first lady Melania Trump, was a six-day pause and the longest so far in Russia's more than four-year war on Ukraine. This appears to be the first time either Ukraine's or Russia's official alert apps have been hacked, and the episode showed how a warning system designed to save lives can itself become a weapon for spreading panic if its security fails.
