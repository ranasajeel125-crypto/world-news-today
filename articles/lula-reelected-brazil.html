Lula Wins Fourth Term as Brazil's President; Bolsonaro Refuses to Concede

world · 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Rafael Costa (Latin America Correspondent)

Lula, 80, overturned the first-round outcome to win a fourth term as Brazil's president.

Leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won Brazil's presidential election, the country's electoral authority said on Sunday, defeating right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro in a runoff to secure a fourth non-consecutive term.

Lula, 80, overturned the outcome from the first round of voting, in which Bolsonaro had picked up 47% of ballots cast to Lula's 45%. With 100% of the vote counted, Brazil's electoral court announced that the soon-to-be 81-year-old Lula had a lead of nearly 4.4 million votes, 51.8% of the total vote, over his opponent. Lula's fourth term begins on 5 January 2027.

Lula pulled off a political comeback that no Brazilian presidential candidate has managed in nearly four decades. He became the first presidential candidate in Brazil's modern democratic history to lose the first round of voting but win the runoff, and the first president to win a fourth term. The former metalworker, who turns 81 on Tuesday, will be among the oldest sitting leaders in the Americas, alongside President Trump and Nicaraguan President Daniel Ortega, both 80.

The comeback was built on turnout. Lula added more than 10 million votes in the three weeks between the two rounds of voting, while his rival gained just 3.6 million. He persuaded millions of Brazilians who had skipped the first round to cast ballots for him in the second round, despite heavy rains that pounded much of the country. Abstention fell from 21.08% in the first round to approximately 19.2% in the runoff, bringing nearly three million more Brazilians to the polls.

Geography helps explain how Lula overturned his first-round deficit. In Minas Gerais, the state with Brazil's second-largest electorate, he went from losing by nearly 590,000 votes to winning by about 99,000, a swing of roughly 690,000 votes in the margin between the two candidates. Although Flavio Bolsonaro retained his lead in the states of Sao Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, Lula won their capitals, Brazil's two largest cities, and reversed the first-round result in the city of Rio. His gains were reinforced by victories in all nine northeastern states and higher voter turnout.

Bolsonaro, the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, refused to concede defeat. He called into question a late-Sunday decision by a Lula-appointed Supreme Court justice that extended voting by two hours over alleged voter transport irregularities, and said he would ask for the result to be annulled. "It is impossible to recognize the election results, given the circumstances under which things unfolded," Bolsonaro told journalists. His petition was undermined by the fact that his campaign had also sought a voting extension due to heavy rains in the conservative-leaning south.

Yet the scale of the loss, with Bolsonaro trailing Lula by over 4 million votes, makes it unlikely his efforts to annul the vote will succeed, analysts said. By Monday, Bolsonaro's Liberal Party announced that it acknowledged the results, indicating that a formal appeal to the electoral tribunal would not be filed. "We recognize the presidential election results," party head Valdemar Costa Neto said, according to CNN Brasil.

Bolsonaro's refusal to concede could spark a wave of protests in a country where faith in the electoral system, and in judicial independence, has been strained in recent years, observers said. Jair Bolsonaro plunged Brazil into a deep democratic crisis after the election in 2022, when his supporters stormed government buildings in protest against the result. He later received a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup. Flavio Bolsonaro's refusal to recognise the result echoed his father's stance four years earlier, when he too refused to accept defeat by Lula.

The campaign was dominated by concerns over the cost of living, crime and corruption, and it was fought against the backdrop of a wider rightward shift in Latin America. Lula's victory bucks a trend in which increasing numbers of right-of-centre presidential candidates allied with Trump have been winning, and it leaves Mexico and Brazil, Latin America's two most populous nations, with left-of-centre leaders. Lula, a vocal critic of US President Donald Trump, won a result that provides relief to critics of widening US influence in Latin America.

Leaders across Latin America congratulated Lula on his victory, with Mexico's President Claudia Sheinbaum saying "the people of Brazil won" and Paraguay's President Santiago Pena saluting "this great democratic journey." China also congratulated Lula, saying it was willing to work with Brazil to continue advancing the building of a China-Brazil community with a shared future.

Lula's victory is historic, but his room to govern is limited. He won with a more conservative Congress and a razor-thin margin, and he faces governing a country that remains deeply divided. Right-wing parties will hold 303 of the 513 seats in the Chamber of Deputies, a commanding majority that will make it hard for Lula to pass legislation without negotiation and compromise. The margin of victory was larger than in 2022, when he defeated Jair Bolsonaro, but the political landscape he faces is more hostile.

Lula's long political career is one of the most remarkable in modern Latin American politics. A former metalworker and union leader from the industrial suburbs of Sao Paulo, he first became president in 2003 and served two terms until 2011, overseeing a period of economic growth and a large expansion of social programmes that lifted millions of Brazilians out of poverty. He left office with record approval ratings, but his reputation was later damaged by a corruption scandal, and he spent time in prison before his convictions were annulled and he returned to win the presidency again in 2022.

Now, at 80, he has won a fourth term, an achievement no other Brazilian president has managed. His supporters see the victory as a vindication of his commitment to democracy, national sovereignty, the protection of the Amazon and social justice, and as proof that the far right can be beaten at the ballot box. His critics see a president who won narrowly, who faces a hostile Congress, and who must now deliver on the cost of living, crime and corruption that dominated the campaign.

What happens next will depend on whether Lula can build a working relationship with Congress, whether Flavio Bolsonaro's supporters accept the result, and whether the economy improves enough to ease the pressures that drove so many Brazilians to the polls. For now, Brazil has narrowly avoided the return of the far right, and the left has another chance to govern the largest country in Latin America.
