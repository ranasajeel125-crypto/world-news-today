Airstrikes Blamed on Saudi Arabia Kill at Least 27 Near Yemen's Capital, Houthis Say

world · 7 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

At least 27 people were killed and 36 wounded in what the Houthi-run Saba news agency said were Saudi airstrikes on a village near Yemen's capital Sanaa on Saturday, in one of the deadliest reported attacks since fighting between Saudi Arabia and the Iran-aligned group resumed in July, Reuters reported.

The Houthi-run health ministry said the strikes hit homes in Bani Hushaysh, a Houthi stronghold east of Sanaa, and that most of those killed were women and children. A spokesman for the ministry, Anees al-Asbahi, told the Associated Press he expected the toll to rise as search and rescue work continued. The toll did rise through the day: al-Asbahi first put it at 22, then told Houthi-run Al-Masirah television it had reached at least 25, with civilians still buried under the rubble, before the health ministry's statement to Saba put it at 27 killed and 36 wounded. The strikes hit the village of al-Shariya in Bani Hushaysh district, and the Houthis said at least six homes were destroyed.

Reuters said it could not independently verify the account. Saudi Arabia's Center for International Communication did not immediately comment, saying it would look into Reuters' inquiries. The ministry blames Saudi Arabia for the strikes; Riyadh has not confirmed carrying them out. Al Jazeera, which broadcast footage released by the Houthis claiming to show the aftermath, said it was unable to independently verify the footage, and the Saudi-led coalition, which supports Yemen's UN-recognised government, has yet to comment.

Abdul-Aziz al-Dailami, director of Sehatuna Hospital in Sanaa, said many victims remained trapped under the rubble, including first responders. "The strikes keep coming. Just as rescuers arrive, another strike hits. We have even lost some of our own medical responders, who have been martyred," he said, adding that many of the wounded arrived in very critical condition with head, chest and leg injuries and fractures. A woman was filmed comforting her son as he lay on a stretcher inside an ambulance after being injured in the strike, and footage showed men navigating through the complete destruction of mud-brick buildings, pulling at rubble in search of survivors.

Later on Saturday, Houthi military spokesman Brig. Gen. Yahya Saree said the group had carried out missile and drone operations against targets in Saudi Arabia in response. In a statement posted on X, Saree said the group launched six operations using a large number of ballistic and cruise missiles and drones, targeting Aramco oil facilities, Riyadh airport and Saudi troops and military facilities on both sides of the border. The Houthis said the attacks were a response to what they called Saudi aggression, citing a strike on vendors and shoppers at Al-Hadara Market in Al-Wazi'iyah district, Taiz governorate, on Friday morning, and the strikes on civilians in their homes in Bani Hushaysh on Saturday afternoon.

Houthi officials also said at least 30 people were killed on Friday in a strike on a market in Taiz province. Footage released by the Houthis and made available through AFPTV showed the aftermath of reported strikes on a market in Al-Waziyah, in Yemen's southwestern Taiz province, on November 6. The Houthis also said nine strikes had targeted telecommunications networks in Saada governorate. Taken together, the two days of strikes in Taiz and Sanaa provinces made the weekend one of the deadliest since the fighting resumed.

The renewed fighting between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis has shattered a 2022 UN-brokered truce that largely halted attacks between the Saudi-led coalition and the group, which controls Sanaa. The Houthis have since launched missile and drone attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities, while Riyadh has carried out airstrikes in Yemen in support of its Yemeni allies. The truce, brokered by the United Nations in 2022, had brought the longest period of relative calm in a war that began more than a decade ago, and its collapse has returned Yemen to open warfare between the Houthis and the Saudi-backed government.

The fighting, a spillover from the wider US-Iran conflict, has revived a complex 12-year war in Yemen that has caused one of the worst humanitarian crises globally and fragmented the Arab world's most impoverished country. The number of people displaced since the latest round of fighting began has climbed to more than 200,000, the International Organization for Migration said in October. Yemen was already one of the poorest countries in the Arab world before the war, and years of fighting have destroyed its infrastructure, its economy and its health system, leaving millions dependent on humanitarian aid.

The war in Yemen began in 2014, when the Houthis, a movement from the north of the country, seized Sanaa and drove the internationally recognised government from the capital. Saudi Arabia intervened in 2015 at the head of a coalition, in support of the government, and the war that followed became one of the world's worst humanitarian disasters, with hundreds of thousands dead from fighting, disease and hunger. The 2022 truce offered a way out, and negotiations for a lasting settlement had made halting progress. The resumption of fighting in July, and its escalation in November, has put that settlement further away than ever, and the strikes on Bani Hushaysh, on a market in Taiz, and on the telecommunications networks of Saada show a war that is once again being fought against civilian targets as well as military ones.
