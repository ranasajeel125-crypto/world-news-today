Houthi Strike Hits Riyadh's Tallest Skyscraper, Home of Saudi Arabia's Sovereign Wealth Fund

world · 20 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Middle East Desk)

The Public Investment Fund Tower dominates the King Abdullah Financial District in Riyadh, the showcase of Saudi Arabia's economic ambitions.

Riyadh's tallest skyscraper, the headquarters of Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, was damaged on Thursday in a Houthi attack, in the latest blow to the kingdom in its escalating war with the Iran-aligned Yemeni group. Saudi Arabia's civil defence said the attack caused material damage to several floors of the building and nearby buildings, but no injuries were reported.

Images distributed by state media identified the building as the Public Investment Fund (PIF) Tower, in the King Abdullah Financial District. The 80-storey tower stands 385 metres high and dominates the district, which acts as a showcase for Saudi Arabia's economic ambitions. Reuters reported that the strike blew out windows across seven to eight floors of the tower and bent parts of its metal facade. The financial district was closed to visitors following the attack.

The Houthis said they had targeted Riyadh's financial centre twice on Thursday, first at dawn and again in the evening. Their military spokesman, Yahya Saree, said in a post on X that the group had targeted Riyadh's international airport and the capital's financial district with cruise missiles. Three sources familiar with the matter told Reuters that the building had been struck by an incoming Houthi missile. Journalists from the AFP news agency in the Saudi capital reported hearing a powerful blast in the early hours of Thursday.

The Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis said it had addressed a missile threat launched towards Riyadh. Its spokesman, Major-General Turki al-Maliki, said in an online statement that the initial assessment indicated damage from debris, with the extent of the material damage still being surveyed. Hours later, he said air defences had intercepted and destroyed four ballistic missiles launched by the Houthis towards the southern region, bordering Yemen, and that the coalition had destroyed a ballistic missile launchpad used in the attempted attacks on Riyadh.

Saudi Arabia's Foreign Ministry condemned the attacks on civilian sites, including the tower. In a statement on X, it said the kingdom affirmed its full and legitimate right to take all necessary measures to defend its sovereignty, safeguard its national assets, and protect the security and safety of its citizens and residents. It called on the international community to fulfil its responsibilities, implement United Nations Security Council resolutions and condemn the attacks.

The attack set off alarms across the country. Saudi civil defence issued separate brief warnings for Khamis Mushait governorate and Abha city, in the south-west, before saying the danger had passed, and at least seven emergency warnings were issued in various parts of the country on Thursday. A Saudi aircraft also carried out air raids on the Houthi-controlled Yemeni capital, Sanaa, earlier on Thursday, the group-run Saba news agency reported, without providing further details.

Thursday's strike was the second major landmark hit in the Saudi capital in 11 days. On November 8, the Houthis said they had hit the Aramco Tower in Riyadh's financial district, causing visible damage to several floors, though Saudi authorities said the damage followed an interception. In October, the group said it struck the Aramco headquarters in Dhahran, in the east, which three people told reporters caused extensive damage. Attacks on Riyadh airport in October killed 18 people over four days, Saudi authorities said, including 13 on October 10.

The day before the tower strike, the Houthis claimed responsibility for targeting several sites in Saudi Arabia, including the international airport and the financial centre in Riyadh, as well as a site in Jizan and the Khamis Mushait airbase in the south, where alerts were sounded. The group has waged a sustained campaign of drone and missile attacks on Saudi airports, oil facilities and office buildings in recent weeks, grounding flights and setting global oil markets on edge.

The fighting is intensifying on both sides. The latest attacks coincided with an announcement by Yemeni government forces that about 715 air strikes were carried out against the Houthis on several fronts on Thursday. Earlier in the week, Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council issued a decision designating the Houthi group a terrorist organisation, saying anyone who cooperates with the group will face prosecution and punishment, with penalties that could include execution or prison terms of 30 years or more.

The renewed conflict has shattered a 2022 UN-brokered truce that had largely halted the exchange of attacks between the Saudi-led coalition, which backs Yemen's internationally recognised government, and the Houthis, who control the most populous parts of the country. The fighting broke out again after the Houthis captured strategic areas along Yemen's Red Sea coast last month, and it has become another volatile front in the wider regional confrontation involving Iran, whose allies the Houthis are.

The human cost in Yemen is mounting fast. On Thursday, the United Nations children's agency, UNICEF, said more than 330,000 people, including an estimated 174,000 children, have been displaced since the beginning of the renewed military escalations. The World Health Organization said the death toll from the latest escalation, which began in August, had reached 2,260, with 10,519 wounded as of November 16, and warned the number of casualties was likely to increase as renewed fighting continued around densely populated areas, particularly in Taiz and Lahij.

The choice of target matters as much as the damage. The PIF is chaired by Saudi Arabia's de facto ruler, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, and is one of the largest sovereign wealth funds in the world, with more than $900 billion in assets under management. It is the central institution financing the crown prince's plans to diversify the Saudi economy away from oil and attract international investment. The King Abdullah Financial District around it hosts the headquarters of the Saudi stock exchange along with multinationals such as Goldman Sachs, luxury shops and green spaces, built to present Riyadh as a global financial capital. A missile reaching the tower at its centre is a direct challenge to that image, and to the idea that the capital's showcase district is safe.

The strike also raises the stakes for energy markets. The Houthis have already targeted the kingdom's oil infrastructure, including the East-West pipeline that Riyadh uses to reroute crude to its Yanbu port on the Red Sea, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most important oil chokepoint. Attacks on airports, pipelines and now the financial district show a campaign aimed not only at military targets but at the economic assets the kingdom depends on, and each successful hit tests the limits of Saudi air defences that have intercepted many, but not all, of the missiles fired.

What happens next will be measured in retaliation and in repairs. The coalition's air raids on Sanaa and the scale of Thursday's strikes against Houthi positions suggest the response will be heavy, while the Houthis have shown no sign of halting a campaign that has now reached the two most prominent towers in Riyadh's financial district within 11 days. The district's reopening, and whether the tower's damage can be repaired quickly, will be the first visible test of how much the attack has cost the kingdom. The harder question is whether either side has a route back to the truce that held for four years, or whether the war that resumed in August is now settling into a longer and more destructive phase.

Why it matters: the PIF Tower strike brings the war in Yemen into the heart of Saudi Arabia's financial showcase, hitting the institution that funds the kingdom's economic transformation. It is the second landmark tower damaged in Riyadh in 11 days, and it comes as fighting shatters a four-year truce, displaces hundreds of thousands of people and kills thousands. For Riyadh, the attack is a test of its defences and its image as a safe place to do business; for the region, it is another sign that the war with the Houthis is widening, not ending.
