COP31 Collapses Without a Deal as EU and Others Reject Final Draft in Antalya

climate · 22 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (Climate Desk)

Delegates at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, where two weeks of talks ran two days past their Friday deadline before collapsing on Sunday with no political agreement.

United Nations climate talks in Antalya collapsed on Sunday after the European Union and others rejected a final draft deal, ending the COP31 summit with no political agreement. COP31 President Murat Kurum told the closing plenary that the deal was not approved. "It is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognise that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted," he said.

The collapse was a sharp reversal. In the early hours of Sunday, the presidency had put forward a new package it said every country could agree on, and delegates were given until 0300 GMT to raise objections ahead of an approval session due at 0600 GMT. The objections came. Kurum told delegates there would be no further attempt at a text. "There will be no new text from now on. We will not be carrying out work on a new text in an environment where there is no consensus," he said. "There is nothing to do, we did all that we could. Since there are 196 countries, they will make the decision and we will respect their decision." Reuters reported that Kurum later left the stage in the middle of speeches being delivered by country representatives.

The deal fell on the question that had deadlocked the summit from its first day: fossil fuels. The fourth and final draft of the summit's main political text contained no reference to phasing out oil, coal and gas. Countries were instead asked only to "recall" the outcome of the first global stocktake agreed at COP28 in Dubai, which included the call for a "just, orderly and equitable" transition away from fossil fuels — a formula that kept the Dubai commitment in the decision without restating it, but which ambitious countries said was not enough. Ireland's environment minister, Darragh O'Brien, said the European Union could not support the proposed deal, in part because it made no mention of the fossil fuels that scientists say are driving global warming. Britain and the EU formally rejected the proposal, and Britain's energy secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh, called the failure to reach agreement "immensely disappointing". The British government had criticised the final draft, arguing that it "moves us backwards on the science".

The draft was not empty. It acknowledged for the first time that global warming is on course to exceed the 1.5 degrees Celsius threshold countries had sought to preserve, while calling for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse the overshoot. It kept the presidencies' flagship electrification goal — raising electricity's share of total global energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, from roughly 21 percent today — though without stipulating that the electricity should come from renewable sources. And it still carried no dollar figure for adaptation finance: the proposed $120 billion annual target for helping poorer countries adapt to climate change, dropped from an earlier draft, was never restored. Some minor and technical issues were approved during the closing session, but the failure to agree on the main "cover decision" left the summit effectively ending in no deal.

The summit had already become the longest climate change summit in more than three decades, running two days past its scheduled Friday finish as delegates booked later flights in an attempt to reach a last-ditch compromise. Two weeks of negotiations had produced four versions of the main text: a 144-paragraph first draft, an 80-paragraph second iteration, and then the overnight package published at 3am local time on Sunday, refined once more into the final version put to the closing plenary. Each revision narrowed the language on fossil fuels rather than strengthening it, after Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers objected to the original call for a faster phase-out. Panama's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, said COP31 had been "hijacked" by petroleum.

The frustration inside the conference hall had been spilling into the open for days. On Friday night, delegates passed a protest banner reading "failure", while a demonstrator interrupted Kurum as he spoke, shouting, "you are killing the future, you are killing the future". On Saturday, the European Union's top climate official warned that the summit would produce "a good deal or no deal at all". "It's either going to be a good deal or it's going to be no deal," EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra told reporters. "We will find out." On Sunday, the answer was no deal.

The collapse comes in a year in which the impacts of global warming have been among the most destructive on record, from extreme heat in Europe to glacial floods in Nepal — but also a year in which wars and public outcry over the cost of living have pulled political attention away from climate action. The United States, the world's largest historical greenhouse gas emitter, was absent from the talks for the second year in a row after President Donald Trump ended cooperation on climate policy. A UN report published last month found the world is on track to exceed the 1.5C limit, and October 2026 was about 1.8 degrees Celsius warmer than the mid-1800s, according to Berkeley Earth.

The failure to agree a political declaration in Antalya does not wipe out the obligations agreed by previous summits or remove the technical and legal frameworks governing multilateral action against climate change. The 2015 Paris Agreement, the Dubai stocktake and the related finance and reporting systems all remain in place, and the detailed work deferred to an "Antalya-to-Addis Ababa Roadmap" now leads to next year's COP32 summit in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027. But the summit that was supposed to turn the promises of Paris and Dubai into delivery instead ends with the hardest choices — on fossil fuels and on the money vulnerable countries need — still unresolved. The last time UN climate talks failed to end with a deal adopted by all was in Copenhagen in 2009, a precedent every delegation in Antalya has had in mind.

What happens next will be decided far from Antalya. The countries that rejected the text — led by the European Union, Britain and Panama, with backing from developing nations demanding stronger action on fossil fuel transition and climate finance — must now decide whether the collapse becomes a turning point or simply a lost year. The draft itself acknowledged that adaptation finance needs are now running at $305 billion a year, while the loss and damage fund established at COP28 remains so underfunded it can hardly function. Small island states and least developed countries leave Antalya with the same needs they arrived with, and no new money to meet them.

Why it matters: a climate summit that ends with no deal is a rare event — it has not happened in this form since Copenhagen in 2009 — and it leaves the world's climate process without a fresh political agreement at the very moment the science says the 1.5C limit is about to be breached. The text on the table acknowledged that overshoot for the first time, yet could not agree to name the fuels driving it. The collapse does not cancel the Paris Agreement or the Dubai commitment to transition away from fossil fuels, but it does mean those promises now rest entirely on what governments do at home, with no new collective push behind them. Whether Antalya is remembered as the summit where the process finally broke, or as the shock that forced it to change, will be decided not in a conference hall in Turkey but in the year of politics that follows.
