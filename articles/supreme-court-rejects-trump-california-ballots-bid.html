Supreme Court Rejects Trump Bid to Set Aside California Ballots

politics · 3 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The one-sentence order gave no explanation and recorded no noted dissents, leaving California's existing ballot procedures in place for Election Day.

politics · 3 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Supreme Court on Monday quickly rejected a bid by President Donald Trump's administration to temporarily block California from counting mail-in ballots from more than 20,000 registered voters who federal officials allege are likely non-US citizens, Reuters reported, acting on the eve of the 3 November midterm elections.

The justices denied the Justice Department's emergency request just hours after it was filed, in a brief, one-sentence order that offered no explanation of the court's action, as is common when it handles emergency matters. No justice publicly dissented, and the court did not ask California for a response to the administration's request, a step that usually signals serious consideration of an emergency case. The unusually fast decision, and the absence of a request for a response brief, underscored that the administration's case was a reach that had been rejected by every court to consider it.

The administration had demanded that California separate any ballots cast by people on a list of 20,000 registered voters amassed by the US Department of Homeland Security from other ballots, and not count them until those voters are determined to be US citizens. Lower courts had already refused the same request: US District Judge David Carter denied it on 28 October, and the San Francisco-based 9th US Circuit Court of Appeals refused it on Friday in a 2-1 decision. The appeals court said forcing California to use the federal list could prevent valid ballots from being counted and jeopardise the Los Angeles County election, and that there was no feasible way to identify and segregate the ballots of specific individuals without potentially catastrophic effects on the entire system. In dissent, Circuit Judge Patrick Bumatay called the government's request reasonable, saying it would not cancel any vote but would allow time to verify the eligibility of suspected noncitizens.

The list at the centre of the case was assembled by US Citizenship and Immigration Services and Homeland Security Investigations after federal officials obtained California voter-roll data during the summer. The administration's case was based on an uncertain methodology for matching names on the voter rolls against immigration records, and an unknown number of mail ballots in question were likely already counted by the time the case reached the Supreme Court. Critics of the suit argued that the methodology was unreliable and risked flagging eligible citizens, while the administration argued that it was entitled to ensure that only citizens voted.

California Governor Gavin Newsom, a Democrat, called the decision a welcome sight, adding that Californians can feel secure in knowing the state will carry out a free and fair election. "After repeatedly losing in the lower courts, the highest court in the land firmly slapped down the Trump DOJ's baseless attack on California's electoral process — not even entertaining it," Newsom said in a statement. Lawyers for groups opposed to the administration's suit said every court to hear the case had now rejected the attempt to cast a cloud of suspicion over eligible voters' ballots on the eve of an election.

The decision represented another defeat for Trump as his fellow Republicans fight to retain control of Congress in Tuesday's midterms, Reuters reported. It was also the latest in a series of election cases the court has decided against the administration. In September, the justices rejected the president's controversial plan to change how mail ballots are sent to voters across the country, rebuffing a proposal that election officials warned would have had catastrophic consequences if allowed to take effect for this year's midterms. Most notably, the justices in September rejected the president's attempt to give the US Postal Service extraordinary power to withhold certain mail ballots. At the same time, the justices have sided with the administration in a series of other emergency docket appeals, which made the swift rejection in the California case all the more striking.

The emergency ruling does not end the underlying lawsuit, which continues in the lower courts. The Justice Department sued California in federal court on 15 October, claiming the state is unlawfully preventing enforcement of a 1996 US law that makes it a criminal offense for noncitizens to vote in federal elections, while California has said its safeguards against noncitizen voting are robust. The Supreme Court's decision leaves intact the lower-court rulings rejecting the administration's effort to change California's ballot procedures immediately before the election, and California can continue using its existing procedures for processing ballots and determining voter eligibility.

The case also raised a constitutional question about who controls elections. Also on Monday in Washington, another federal judge ruled against the administration's efforts to make last-minute demands of California election officials, finding they violated the US Constitution. "The Executive Branch has likely violated the Constitution's Elections Clause, which commits authority over election rules to the states and Congress alone," wrote Chief Judge James Boasberg. The 9th Circuit, in a 2-1 decision, concluded that the federal government has no power to dictate to states how to run elections. The Supreme Court did not address that question in its one-sentence order, but its refusal to intervene left the lower courts' reasoning in place for the election.

For California voters, the immediate effect was straightforward: the state was not required to create a special system to segregate the ballots identified by the Trump administration before the November 3 election, and the existing procedures for counting mail-in ballots remained in place as voters decided races that would help determine control of Congress for the last two years of Trump's second term.
