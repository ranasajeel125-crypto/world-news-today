France Braces for Fresh Unrest on Saturday, Deploying 70,000 Police

world · 31 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Interior Minister Laurent Nunez described Saturday, which falls on Halloween, as carrying a very high risk of unrest.

France will deploy a 70,000-strong police presence to contain a widely expected bout of fresh unrest on Saturday in the latest nationwide protests driven by anger over living costs, public services and President Emmanuel Macron's government.

The nationwide demonstrations, which fall on Halloween, come two weeks after riots and looting caught security forces off guard, leaving damaged shops, public buildings and businesses in cities across France. Interior Minister Laurent Nunez has vowed not to let a repeat happen.

"Everyone is on deck to ensure an extremely firm response to any trouble," Nunez told a news conference earlier this week as he set out details of security arrangements for a day he described as carrying "a very high risk" of unrest. Authorities are also alert to the possibility that rioters could use Halloween masks to conceal their identities, and Nunez noted that laws already allow police to ban face coverings when public order is at risk. Police also barred entry to the Place de la Republique and Les Halles, both hit by rioting and looting on 17 October, from 11pm to 6am on the Saturday and Sunday nights.

The protests gathered pace in late September as Macron's minority government launched 2027 budget talks. Facing public debt approaching 120% of economic output and rising borrowing costs, the government is under pressure to rein in spending. Students angry at the state of many schools, crowded classes and a university admissions system widely seen as unfair have been joined by trade unions and a re-emergent "Yellow Vest" movement demanding action on the cost of living. The movement also includes demands from climate activists and groups opposed to police violence and violence against children.

The protests began with mass demonstrations on 17 October that drew about 550,000 people around France, according to government estimates, and deteriorated into some of France's worst rioting in years, with rioters looting hundreds of shops. Demonstrations on 24 October drew 410,000 people, according to government estimates, and were largely peaceful, although police made more than 1,300 arrests. The government estimated that 320,000 people protested nationwide on Saturday 31 October, fewer than in previous protests but still a significant turnout, and authorities reported more than 560 arrests nationwide by Saturday night. The protests were overwhelmingly peaceful, with Halloween costumes lightening the mood, but gatherings in Paris and other cities ended in clouds of tear gas and scattered fires set by small groups on the margins.

The unrest continued into the following weekend. About 146,000 people rallied across the country on Saturday 7 November, including 46,000 in Paris, the Ministry of the Interior said, although unions put the figure at 250,000 protesters nationwide, with 75,000 in the capital. It was the fourth consecutive weekend of demonstrations. Clashes broke out between police and some protesters in the capital, despite a lower turnout than in previous weeks, and police used tear gas against individuals throwing projectiles and homemade fireworks at the security forces. A group of 200 individuals attacked officers as they were arresting a man who had set fire to a rubbish bin in a square in Paris, the AFP news agency reported.

The security operation for the protests has been on a large scale. The deployment of 70,000 police officers and gendarmes has been repeated for successive Saturdays, and the operation has included 110 mobile force units, 25 Centaur armoured vehicles, water cannons, helicopters and drones. Authorities have issued orders banning demonstrations in certain city centres, while organisers of declared protests have been asked to follow alternative routes. In Paris, the march was pushed to the capital's edge, and the Yellow Vests' planned rally at Place de la Concorde, near the lower house of parliament and the presidential palace, was banned, with police surrounding some 100 people who arrived and apprehending two of the movement's coordinators.

The Yellow Vest movement, which staged street protests eight years ago against Macron's government over planned fuel-tax hikes, has called for its rallies to go ahead despite the bans, and has urged "mass disobedience". The movement's return has added a harder edge to a protest wave that began with high school students, and it has revived memories of the winter of 2018-19, when Yellow Vest protests turned into some of the most serious unrest of Macron's presidency.

The protests have inspired youth-led protests across Europe, with demonstrations planned in more than 30 German cities on Saturday, and they have put pressure on a government that is already weak. Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu, who leads a minority government, has struggled to end the crisis, caught between concessions and tough talk. The government has been anxious since violence broke out on 17 October, and it has mobilised 70,000 police officers and gendarmes for successive Saturdays, with about 50 declared demonstrations taking place across the country.

The unrest has handed a political boost to far-right leader Marine Le Pen, who leads opinion polls ahead of April's presidential election, while the Chamber of Commerce estimates losses to retailers exceed 1 billion euros, with other estimates putting the total at double when uninsured damage and lost revenue are included. The protests have erupted as the country heads to presidential elections in five months' time, in which the far right sees its best chance yet to win power.

The underlying grievances are familiar ones in French politics: the cost of living, the state of public services, and a sense that the government is out of touch with ordinary people. Students complain about crowded classes and an admissions system they see as unfair. Workers complain about wages that have not kept pace with prices. Pensioners complain about the erosion of their incomes. And all of them complain about a government that, in their view, asks ordinary people to bear the cost of reducing a public debt that approaches 120% of economic output, while doing little to address the inequalities that the debt is said to require.

Whether the protests fade or grow will depend on the government's response, and on whether the movement can sustain its momentum through the winter. For now, France is braced for another Saturday of demonstrations, another deployment of 70,000 police, and another test of a government that has spent a month trying, and failing, to bring a social crisis under control.
