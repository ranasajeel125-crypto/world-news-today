Ousted Leader Sheikh Hasina Sets 16 December for Return to Bangladesh — Despite Death Sentence

politics · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Sheikh Hasina, ousted in August 2024, has named 16 December — Bangladesh's Victory Day — for her return from exile in India, and asked Dhaka to arrange a Biman Bangladesh Airlines flight. The government says she would be arrested on arrival.

Ousted Bangladeshi Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina plans to return home from more than two years of exile in India on 16 December, the anniversary of the end of her country's 1971 liberation war with Pakistan, her spokesperson told Reuters on Tuesday. It is the first time Hasina has set a specific date for her return — and it immediately raises the political and legal stakes in a country where her party is banned and where, Reuters reported, violence has risen since she sought new travel documents earlier this month.

Hasina has urged the Bangladeshi government to arrange her return on flag carrier Biman Bangladesh Airlines. “Since Sheikh Hasina was flown to India on a Bangladesh aircraft on August 5th, 2024, she now calls upon the government to arrange for her air transport from Delhi back to Dhaka,” the spokesperson, Syed Mozammel Ali, said in a statement. “Having exiled her contrary to the Constitution under such circumstances, it is now the responsibility of the government to make possible her return to Bangladesh on Victory Day on December 16th.”

The government has made clear it has no intention of doing so. A Bangladeshi minister told Reuters on Sunday that Hasina, who faces a death sentence, would be arrested and jailed on return, and that there had been no official discussions with India about sending an aircraft to bring her back. A Bangladeshi government spokesperson did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Tuesday.

Dhaka's position, as restated to Reuters, is that Hasina can only come home through extradition. “Hasina is a convicted criminal; a fugitive terrorist,” Humaiun Kobir, Bangladesh's minister of state for foreign affairs, said. “She can only return home by extradition treaty; in handcuffs, and under strict surveillance.” Aminul Islam, the chief prosecutor of the International Crimes Tribunal which sentenced Hasina to death, said it was unreasonable for a convicted fugitive to expect the government to arrange her return.

The sentence hangs over everything she is planning. Bangladesh's war crimes court sentenced Hasina to death in November 2025, in her absence, for ordering a deadly crackdown on the student-led uprising that ended her rule. She has denied the charges from exile, rejected the verdict, and said she is ready to fight the case once she is back. The United Nations has said as many as 1,400 people were killed during the unrest that forced her out of power.

Hasina has been signalling this return for weeks. Earlier this month, in an interview with the Associated Press, she said she would return to Bangladesh in December at any cost, and that even death could not prevent her from coming back home. “If death is my destiny, I would rather die on the soil of my own country, among my own people,” she said in emailed answers to the news agency's questions. “So, I will return to Bangladesh. That decision has not changed.” She did not say how she would travel.

Her aides are leaning hard on the symbolism of the date. “It is deeply symbolic that she has chosen 16 December, our Victory Day, for her planned return to Bangladesh,” Abu Obaidha Arin, an aide to Hasina, told the Associated Press. The choice is deliberate: 16 December marks the end of the 1971 liberation war with Pakistan, the founding moment of Bangladesh as an independent country — and Hasina is the daughter of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, the leader of that independence movement. To return on Victory Day is to present herself not as a fugitive coming home to face a court, but as the heir of the country's founding story.

That framing is exactly what Dhaka rejects. The government she is asking to fly her home is not the interim administration that took over after her fall: it is the elected government of Prime Minister Tarique Rahman, the son of her longtime political rival, former prime minister Khaleda Zia. Rahman was sworn in on 17 February 2026 after his Bangladesh Nationalist Party won a majority in the 12 February election, the first national vote since Hasina's government fell. Her party, the Awami League, was barred from contesting it and remains banned.

The path to that election began with her fall. Hasina fled to India on 5 August 2024, a Monday, as a student-led mass uprising ended her 15-year rule. The trigger, as the International Crisis Group has documented, was the Supreme Court's decision in June 2024 to revive job quotas for the descendants of “freedom fighters” from the 1971 war — an initiative widely seen as favouring supporters of her party. She first tried to crush the protests; the violence only turned a demonstration about quotas into a mass movement demanding the government's resignation.

After negotiations among the army, student leaders and political parties, an interim government led by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus was sworn into office on 8 August 2024. It re-established the International Crimes Tribunal — a domestic judicial body first formed in 2009 to investigate crimes committed during the 1971 war — to try those allegedly responsible for serious abuses under Hasina's regime. In May 2025 the interim government banned the Awami League's activities under the Anti-Terrorism Act until the tribunal had reached decisions in cases against the party and its leaders, and the Election Commission suspended the party's registration. The ban is still in force, and the new elected government has upheld it.

The practical obstacles to her return are just as stark as the political ones. Her passport and other documents were cancelled months after she fled, and she sought new paperwork only earlier this month. Kobir has said no passport or travel pass will be issued to her, telling reporters: “We are demanding her handover.” Without a valid passport, a commercial flight is not a simple matter — which is why her side is asking the government itself to arrange the aircraft. No official discussions with India about sending a plane have taken place, according to the minister who spoke to Reuters on Sunday, and India has given no public indication of how it would handle a return request.

What happens next turns first on whether 16 December produces an actual attempt to travel, or only a statement. If Hasina boards a plane to Dhaka, she would be flying into a country whose government has said in advance that she will be arrested and jailed — and into a capital where her planned return has already stirred unrest, with paramilitary troops deployed as tensions grew. If she does not travel, the date she has named becomes a different kind of test: a public promise, made in her own words, that her supporters and her opponents will both be watching.

Why it matters: for more than two years, the question of Hasina's return has been abstract — a vow from exile, a demand for extradition from Dhaka, a death sentence waiting in a court file. Naming 16 December, on the country's most symbolic day, turns it into a deadline. It forces three governments — Dhaka, Delhi, and the court in between — to answer the same question at the same time: does a former prime minister sentenced to death in absentia come home as a returning leader, as a prisoner in handcuffs, or not at all? The answer will say a great deal about how Bangladesh's new elected government handles the unfinished business of the uprising that brought it to power — and about whether the country's most divisive figure can still bend its politics from exile.
