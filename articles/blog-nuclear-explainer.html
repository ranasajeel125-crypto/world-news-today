Small Reactors, Big Ambitions: The New Nuclear Race, Explained

blog · 24 Oct 2026 · 7 min read · By Tom Becker (Technology Editor)

Nuclear power spent thirty years as the energy source the world loved to leave. Now it is at the centre of three separate races at once: the race to power AI data centres, the race to decarbonise heavy industry, and — as this week's NASA–Department of Energy agreement shows — the race back to the Moon and on to Mars.

The technology doing the diplomatic work is the small modular reactor, or SMR. Instead of one giant plant built on site over a decade, SMRs are factory-built units that can be shipped and assembled in multiples. Supporters say that turns nuclear from a construction project into a manufacturing product: cheaper, faster, repeatable. India and the UK discussed SMRs in their technology talks this month; the US wants a fission unit running on the lunar surface by 2030.

A small modular reactor is, by the definitions used by the International Atomic Energy Agency and the World Nuclear Association, a nuclear fission plant rated at up to roughly 300 megawatts of electricity per unit — about a third of a conventional large reactor — built from standardised components in a factory rather than assembled bespoke on site. A 300-megawatt unit can supply on the order of 200,000 to 300,000 homes. Microreactors are the smaller cousins, typically under 20 megawatts and sometimes small enough to be trucked or shipped in a container. The pitch is that factory production brings the learning curves of manufacturing to an industry that has historically built each plant as a one-off, and that smaller units can be added one at a time as demand grows, rather than requiring a single enormous bet.

The idea is not new, but 2026 is the year it started to become concrete. Only two small modular reactors are actually generating commercial power today: Russia's floating Akademik Lomonosov, operating since 2020, and China's HTR-PM demonstration plant at Shidaowan, in commercial operation since December 2023. No Western small modular reactor has sold a single kilowatt-hour yet. The furthest-along Western project is Ontario Power Generation's Darlington plant in Canada, where four GE Hitachi BWRX-300 units are under construction, with the first unit's grid connection targeted for the end of 2030. In the United States, TerraPower's Natrium reactor in Kemmerer, Wyoming, received its construction permit from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission on 4 March 2026 — the first US commercial reactor construction approval in nearly a decade — and construction began in April. The Tennessee Valley Authority received a construction permit for a BWRX-300 at Clinch River in September 2026. Trackers put roughly 9.8 gigawatts of advanced nuclear capacity now under contract for AI and data-centre buyers, the demand signal that pulled small modular reactors out of specialist nuclear coverage and into mainstream energy and tech reporting.

That demand is the first of the three races. Electricity demand in the United States has accelerated for the first time in two decades, driven primarily by data-centre expansion for artificial intelligence workloads. A single large AI data centre can consume as much electricity as a small city, and operators want power that is available around the clock, without carbon emissions, and close to where the computers sit. Google signed an agreement in October 2024 to buy up to 500 megawatts from Kairos Power reactors through 2035, and Amazon has taken a stake in X-energy, the developer behind the Xe-100 design. Nuclear's attraction is that it runs at full power day and night, unlike wind and solar, and produces no carbon dioxide while generating electricity. The caution is economic: the only small modular reactor project ever attempted in the United States, NuScale's Carbon Free Power Project, was cancelled in November 2023 after projected costs rose from about 3 billion dollars to 9.3 billion dollars and the target electricity price climbed from 58 dollars per megawatt-hour to 89 dollars. First-of-a-kind costs, the industry admits, will be far above the eventual factory-built price.

The second race is decarbonisation of heavy industry. Steel, cement, chemicals and refining need large amounts of high-temperature heat, which is hard to supply with electricity alone, and nuclear reactors produce heat as well as power. A reactor sited next to an industrial plant could supply process heat directly, replacing coal and gas burners. The same logic applies to district heating and to desalination, where a reactor's waste heat can boil seawater. These uses are further from reality than data-centre power, but they explain why governments from Canada to Poland to Indonesia have signed up for small reactor programmes.

Space is where the logic is simplest. Solar power fades with distance and dies entirely during the two-week lunar night. A compact reactor runs through the night, powers life support and instruments, and — in an electric-propulsion ship like Space Reactor-1 'Freedom' — can push a spacecraft to Mars far more efficiently than chemical rockets. NASA and the Department of Energy signed a memorandum of understanding in January 2026 renewing their commitment to jointly develop a lunar fission surface power system, with a reactor ready to launch by 2030, and signed a further agreement in October 2026 covering radioisotope and fission power systems. The most recent design calls for a 100-kilowatt-electric reactor, carried by a heavy-class lander able to deliver up to 15 metric tons to the surface. NASA announced in March 2026 that Space Reactor-1 Freedom, a nuclear electric propulsion rocket fuelled with high-assay low-enriched uranium, is scheduled to launch in December 2028 as a milestone towards a permanent Moon base. Russia and China have announced their own plans to cooperate on a lunar reactor, with a possible deployment between 2033 and 2035. The contest is as much about presence as power: a reactor that runs through the lunar night is what makes a permanent outpost possible, and the first country to build one sets the terms for everything that follows.

The obstacles are equally real. SMRs remain mostly unproven at commercial scale, costs per unit of electricity are still high on paper, and waste and proliferation questions have not gone away. Spent nuclear fuel stays radioactive for thousands of years, and no country has yet opened a permanent deep geological repository, though Finland's Onkalo facility is the closest. Reactor designs that use more highly enriched fuel, such as high-assay low-enriched uranium, raise questions about how fuel is handled and safeguarded. Regulators are being asked to license novel designs quickly, which makes safety advocates nervous for good historical reasons: the accidents at Three Mile Island in 1979, Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011 each reshaped public attitudes to nuclear power for a generation, and the industry's safety record is judged against them.

The honest summary: nuclear's revival is driven less by ideology than by arithmetic. AI's power hunger, industry's heat problem and spaceflight's energy maths all point the same way. Whether the industry can build fast and safely enough to justify the bet is the story to watch for the rest of the decade. The International Energy Agency projects 10 to 25 gigawatts of installed small modular reactor capacity globally by 2035 — a steady evolution rather than an overnight revolution, and roughly 1 to 3 per cent of global nuclear capacity. That may sound modest, but it would be the first time in decades that nuclear power grew by building many small plants instead of a few large ones, and the first real test of whether the factory model can finally make nuclear cheap.
