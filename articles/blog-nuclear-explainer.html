Small Reactors, Big Ambitions: The New Nuclear Race, Explained

blog · 24 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Tom Becker (Technology Editor)

Nuclear power spent thirty years as the energy source the world loved to leave. Now it is at the centre of three separate races at once: the race to power AI data centres, the race to decarbonise heavy industry, and — as this week's NASA–Department of Energy agreement shows — the race back to the Moon and on to Mars.

The technology doing the diplomatic work is the small modular reactor, or SMR. Instead of one giant plant built on site over a decade, SMRs are factory-built units that can be shipped and assembled in multiples. Supporters say that turns nuclear from a construction project into a manufacturing product: cheaper, faster, repeatable. India and the UK discussed SMRs in their technology talks this month; the US wants a fission unit running on the lunar surface by 2030.

Space is where the logic is simplest. Solar power fades with distance and dies entirely during the two-week lunar night. A compact reactor runs through the night, powers life support and instruments, and — in an electric-propulsion ship like Space Reactor-1 'Freedom' — can push a spacecraft to Mars far more efficiently than chemical rockets.

The obstacles are equally real. SMRs remain mostly unproven at commercial scale, costs per unit of electricity are still high on paper, and waste and proliferation questions have not gone away. Regulators are being asked to license novel designs quickly, which makes safety advocates nervous for good historical reasons.

The honest summary: nuclear's revival is driven less by ideology than by arithmetic. AI's power hunger, industry's heat problem and spaceflight's energy maths all point the same way. Whether the industry can build fast and safely enough to justify the bet is the story to watch for the rest of the decade.
