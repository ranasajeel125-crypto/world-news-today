China Stages Unannounced Pacific Drills in ‘Silent Intimidation’ of Taiwan, Officials Say

world · 13 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

More than 100 Chinese military and coast guard vessels were massed in the region early this month, Taiwan officials said — a deployment larger than last year’s “Justice Mission 2025” drills.

China carried out a large, unannounced military exercise across the western Pacific and around Taiwan in early November, in what officials in Taipei have described as a campaign of “silent intimidation” aimed at pressuring Taiwan and other governments before major international meetings.

A senior Taiwan security official, speaking on condition of anonymity because of the sensitivity of the matter, said China’s military and coast guard had at one point massed more than 100 vessels in the region early this month, conducting drills that included simulated attacks on other militaries and cities, including Taiwan’s.

The naval deployment was larger than “Justice Mission 2025”, China’s last formally named exercise targeting Taiwan nearly a year ago and one of the largest Chinese naval deployments to date, the official said. During the latest drills, a Chinese submarine also launched an intercontinental ballistic missile from the Yellow Sea into the Chinese mainland, the official said.

“It is a silent exercise — and in effect, silent intimidation,” the official told Reuters. “This is like a robber hiding a gun in the pocket of a coat — with the muzzle pointed at the bank teller. It scares the teller into handing over the money, while avoiding setting off the alarm and bringing in a large police response.”

China’s defence ministry did not respond to a request for comment. Taiwan’s defence ministry referred Reuters to comments made this week by minister Wellington Koo, who said China was carrying out “area denial” drills in the western Pacific involving an aircraft carrier and other naval formations. China has said the carrier is on “routine” exercises that are not directed at any country.

The drills involved coordinated naval forces from China’s eastern, northern and southern theatre commands, a scale that suggested Beijing was rehearsing not only a Taiwan blockade scenario but broader operations across the western Pacific, the Taiwan official said. That also suggested China’s effort to integrate its regional fleets into a single far-seas operational force.

The scale of the deployment has decreased this week, with around 70 vessels operating within the first island chain on Friday, according to two Taiwan security officials. Taiwan National Security Council Secretary-General Joseph Wu posted details of the latest deployments on X late on Thursday, saying that while the number of vessels had decreased, “the groups of Northern, Eastern & Southern Theater Commands plus Liaoning carrier strike group remain operating in waters east of the Philippines”. He added that Chinese air force activity also remained high near Taiwan.

The drills come as China, which views democratically governed Taiwan as its own territory, has stepped up its military pressure over the past five years, drawing condemnation from Taipei and heightening concern in Western capitals about the potential for conflict. China has never renounced the use of force to bring Taiwan under its control, though it says it would prefer “peaceful reunification”.

Chinese President Xi Jinping on Thursday gave a major policy speech about Taiwan, urging peaceful “reunification”, though making no mention of using force. On Friday, China’s widely read state-backed paper the Global Times warned that if Taiwan’s ruling party persists in “seeking ‘independence by relying on external forces or military means’, dragging the residents of Taiwan onto their war chariot, they will inevitably face a harsh reckoning”.

The background: unlike previous major Chinese exercises around Taiwan, which often came with public announcements, television footage and warnings against “Taiwan independence forces”, this time Beijing said it was routine training not directed against any country. A foreign intelligence source told Reuters the “silent drills without a name” appeared deliberate, allowing Beijing to apply military pressure while avoiding a diplomatic backlash.

Analysts see the timing as significant. The Institute for the Study of War in Washington said that with the United States distracted by the aftermath of the midterm elections and conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine, China appeared to be demonstrating its ability to blockade Taiwan any time it wished. Taiwan’s defence minister described the drills as “area denial” exercises — aimed at stopping US or other allied forces from coming to Taipei’s aid should Beijing attack. For now, few officials or regional analysts predict any immediate direct military action by Beijing: they see China’s military command chain as still recovering from the mass dismissal of generals in late 2025 and early this year.

What happens next: Beijing is hosting the APEC leaders’ summit in Shenzhen next week, where US President Donald Trump and Mr Xi are expected to meet for the third time this year, and Taiwan also attends APEC. With that meeting approaching, Beijing appears to be sending a clear signal of its military strength — and the audience extends well beyond Taiwan. The United States and its allies have stepped up coordination in response: after a meeting of the grouping known as the “Squad” in Manila late last month, the US, Australia, the Philippines and Japan agreed to establish a Combined Coordination Center aimed at easing joint planning in the event of a fight around Taiwan. Japan’s Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has described a potential Chinese move against Taiwan as a “survival” issue for Japan and has increased military spending.

Why it matters: even without a direct invasion, a deliberate Chinese naval blockade would be regionally disruptive, cutting off Europe, Australia and other developed economies from both Chinese and Taiwanese exports, as well as perhaps those of Japan and South Korea. Taiwan has spent the past year arguing to policymakers and corporate leaders in the US that its position in international supply chains makes it “indispensable” and vital to defend — a case strengthened, not weakened, by the artificial intelligence boom, which has further cemented the importance of the island and its chip industry. Businesses in Australia are already offering training on how to maintain supply chains during such a conflict or blockade. The drills may have been silent, but their message was meant to be heard: Beijing is intent on ratcheting up the pressure, hoping the US or others will pull back — and whatever happens next, this quiet show of force will help define the coming world at least as much as anything elsewhere.
