Appeals Court Again Rejects Trump Bid to Block More Than 20,000 California Ballots

politics · 30 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

In a 2-1 decision, the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals declined to block the ballots from being counted while the Justice Department's lawsuit against California proceeds.

A federal appeals court on Friday rejected the Trump administration's latest attempt to prevent California from counting mail ballots from more than 20,000 registered voters the federal government alleges are likely noncitizens, handing the administration its second defeat this week.

In a 2-1 decision, a panel of the San Francisco-based 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals declined the Justice Department's request to block the ballots from being counted while its lawsuit against California proceeds, leaving California's existing ballot-counting procedures in place just days before the 3 November midterm elections, when President Donald Trump's fellow Republicans are fighting to retain control of Congress.

U.S. Circuit Judges Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, and Gabriel Sanchez, appointed by President Joe Biden, pointed to a lower court's concerns about the reliability of the federal government's voter list. "There is a material risk that granting the United States its requested relief would result in disenfranchisement," Bennett and Sanchez wrote. The majority framed the administration's lawsuit as an attempt to violate the Constitution's Elections Clause, writing: "The consequence of the United States's request is not merely ballot segregation, but ultimately both the cancellation of the ballot and the removal of voters from the voting rolls." The court said that because Congress has not exercised the specific power granted to it under the Elections Clause to override California's established laws for challenging ballots or removing ineligible voters, the state legislature's statutory scheme remains the only method to do so. A third judge, Trump appointee Patrick Bumatay, issued a dissent, saying the administration's petition was a "reasonable request" that would not have cancelled any votes but would have provided time for the state or federal government to verify citizenship.

The ruling marks the second time this week that the 9th Circuit has rejected an emergency request from the Trump administration involving the ballots. The same three-judge panel had rejected an earlier appeal on Monday, saying the Justice Department had not given lower courts adequate time to weigh the matter, because the administration had sought intervention before the trial judge had even considered the case. The Justice Department returned to the appeals court after U.S. District Judge David O. Carter denied its request for a temporary restraining order on 28 October, saying the proposed order would "wreak havoc and disrupt the election".

Carter, a 1998 appointee of Democratic President Bill Clinton, issued his decision after a hearing on Tuesday in Los Angeles federal court. He concluded that the Justice Department was unlikely to succeed on multiple fronts, finding that the government likely lacks standing and a cause of action, that the federal criminal prohibition on noncitizen voting does not give the department authority to override California's ballot-processing rules, and that the requested remedy would likely violate several federal voting laws. "Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional election procedures and bend to the will of the Executive Branch," Carter said in the 28 October ruling. He said the proposed order could have a "chilling effect on election workers and voters alike", and that people who had met California's voter registration laws must not be deprived of their rights due to "unfounded allegations stemming from a procedure rife with uncertainty and errors". Carter noted that election officials have until 3 December — the date by which election results must be certified — to resolve the issue, suggesting there was no need for an emergency injunction.

The dispute stems from a Justice Department lawsuit filed on 15 October seeking to require California and Los Angeles County election officials to separate ballots connected to more than 20,000 voters the federal government identified as likely noncitizens. The department wanted those ballots held apart and not counted until officials verified that the voters were U.S. citizens. California officials have disputed the federal government's evidence and maintained that the state already has safeguards against noncitizen voting. The list at the centre of the case was assembled by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services and Homeland Security Investigations after federal officials obtained California voter-roll data during the summer. The Trump administration initially sent California its list of 20,000 "likely non-citizens" on 12 October, barely three weeks before the midterm election, and sued on 15 October, more than a week after mail ballots were sent to all registered voters, after the state refused to voluntarily sequester those votes. The department had given state officials a 48-hour deadline to review the list. In its lawsuit, the department invoked a federal law, 18 U.S.C. Section 611, that prohibits noncitizens from voting in federal elections. But the 9th Circuit said the federal law criminalising noncitizen voting does not spell out a procedure states must use to handle citizenship challenges to mail ballots, nor does it expressly authorise the Justice Department to take control of a state's election administration.

The appeals court also pointed to the National Voter Registration Act's prohibition against systematic voter-roll removals within 90 days of a federal election, saying the federal government's attempt to challenge an entire batch of about 20,000 voters raised concerns related to that restriction. The court highlighted the practical consequences of imposing a new ballot-segregation system only days before the election. "As the district court found — and we discern no clear error — there is no feasible way to identify and segregate the ballots of specific individuals without potentially catastrophic effects on the entire system," the court said. The Justice Department has not said how many of the more than 20,000 people on its list of possible noncitizens registered to vote in California have actually submitted ballots.

Friday's decision does not end the underlying lawsuit, but it leaves the disputed ballots subject to California's normal election procedures as voting continues toward Election Day. The district court will continue with the lawsuit alleging California has failed to remove alleged noncitizens from its voter rolls, with an expedited briefing schedule and a hearing set for Friday, 13 November. Carter wants all parties to submit a list of witnesses who can speak on the validity of the department's data and methodology. Separately, Los Angeles County election employees have received federal grand jury subpoenas ordering them to appear in court on 4 November, the day after Election Day, and provide documents connected to the dispute. The judge's ruling came as Trump expands efforts to search for alleged noncitizen voters, with subpoenas issued in at least 13 states. The administration then took the fight to the Supreme Court, which on Monday, 2 November, rejected the emergency request, just hours after the White House had asked the court to intervene, leaving intact the lower-court rulings and California's existing ballot procedures on the eve of the election.
