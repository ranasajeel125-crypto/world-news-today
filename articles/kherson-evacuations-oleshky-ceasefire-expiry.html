Ukraine Evacuates 167 Civilians from Kherson as Oleshky Ceasefire Nears Expiry

world · 1 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The evacuees included 22 children and 21 people with limited mobility; Kherson's governor urged residents to stay out of 'red zones' near the Dnipro.

world · 1 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Ukraine's military said it evacuated 167 people from threatened areas of southern Kherson region on Sunday as a temporary local ceasefire neared expiry — two days after busloads of residents were brought out of the embattled Russian-held town of Oleshky, Reuters reported.

The six-day local ceasefire in Oleshky and the surrounding area was reached after mediation efforts by US first lady Melania Trump and with the involvement of Pope Leo. It was due to expire at midnight Moscow time (2100 GMT) on Sunday, and is the longest local pause in fighting since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022. The ceasefire, which was in effect from October 27 to November 1, ended as planned, with no violations reported by either side, and no sign of an extension.

"Units of civil-military cooperation of the 'South' group of forces, in cooperation with representatives of local authorities, evacuated 167 civilians to safer points in the Kherson region," Ukraine's General Staff wrote on Telegram. It said the evacuees included 22 children and 21 people with limited mobility requiring special assistance. The military said the 167 had been evacuated from parts of the Kherson region that had been under daily shelling by Russian forces, on the Ukrainian-controlled western bank of the Dnipro River.

The governor of Kherson region, Oleksandr Prokudin, urged residents to be vigilant as the expiry of the ceasefire neared. "I also strongly urge you not to visit the so-called 'red zones' – areas and settlements near the Dnipro," he wrote on Telegram. "The intensity of Russian strikes there remains high, and the threat to life is extremely high."

In Moscow, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, quoted by Russian news agencies, said Russia had a positive view of how the operation was conducted in Oleshky, as well as of the coordination between the two countries' human rights commissioners. Ukrainian Human Rights Commissioner Dmytro Lubinets confirmed the end of the agreement, saying both sides had honoured their commitments throughout, and that the ceasefire had been an important step in joint humanitarian efforts with Russian Human Rights Commissioner Yana Lantratova, coordinated by the First Lady of the United States.

Pope Leo said he hoped cooperation between Russia and Ukraine in implementing the ceasefire in Oleshky would pave the way for broader peace efforts. Writing on X on Sunday, he said he hoped the cooperation between the parties, which made this necessary humanitarian act possible, would strengthen the desire for dialogue and lead to new concrete peace initiatives. Melania Trump, writing on X, said the ceasefire "has provided residents with the freedom to move, access to food, clean water, medicine, plus a renewed sense of dignity without the constant threat of attack". After the ceasefire ended, she called the mission a success, saying it had delivered critical aid to the region and enabled the evacuation of nearly 800 people. "On behalf of America, I commend Russia and Ukraine for their leadership and cooperation surrounding the Oleshky ceasefire," she wrote, adding: "I hope Oleshky serves as a foundation for lasting peace between both nations."

On Saturday, starved and exhausted residents of Oleshky crossed into Ukraine after a circuitous journey from the area, just a few kilometres from the front line. They first travelled by bus via Crimea to the Russian town of Anapa, before flying to Belarus from where they continued to Ukraine. Those wishing to leave Oleshky — some for Russia, some for Ukraine — were taken initially to Crimea in 25 buses and nine ambulances, Russian human rights commissioner Yana Lantratova said, before splitting into separate convoys. It was not known how many chose to stay in Russian-held Oleshky, which lies on the Dnipro River opposite the region's Ukrainian-held main town of Kherson. An estimated 2,000 civilians were in Oleshky when the temporary truce took hold, and some residents did not want to leave and decided to stay, even as the ceasefire was set to expire. Hundreds of people, and many pets, arrived back in Ukraine, and the evacuees were reunited with families after 1,711 days apart, in a scene that became one of the most striking images of the war.

The ceasefire has been holding since Tuesday without violations, according to both Ukrainian and Russian officials. It has allowed them to de-mine the roads and bring food, medicines and other supplies into the town. The pause was the longest multi-day truce between Russia and Ukraine since the full-scale invasion began, and it was limited to a single town and its surrounding area, but it showed that the two sides could still agree on humanitarian measures, and carry them out, when there was mediation from outside.

Military action continued in other parts of Ukraine on Sunday. Regional governors and emergency services reported one death in a strike in the Sumy region on the Russian border, and at least one in the central region of Vinnytsia. The end of the Oleshky ceasefire meant that Kherson, and the towns along the Dnipro, returned to war, and the governor's warning about the red zones was a reminder that the pause had been local and temporary. Whether the cooperation that made Oleshky possible can be repeated elsewhere, or scaled up into something broader, is the question that now faces the mediators, the Pope and the two governments. For the nearly 800 people who were evacuated, and for the residents who received food, water and medicine during the six days, the answer matters less than the fact that, for once, the guns fell silent long enough for them to leave.
