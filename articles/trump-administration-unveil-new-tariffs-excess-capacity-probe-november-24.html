Trump Administration Set to Unveil New Tariffs After Probe Into Excess Factory Capacity

business · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The probe covers most of America's largest trading partners, from China and the European Union to Vietnam and Mexico. No tariff rates or exemptions have been disclosed, and the White House and the US Trade Representative's office did not immediately respond to Reuters' queries.

The Trump administration is poised to imminently release the findings of a probe aimed at countering alleged excess industrial capacity in 16 major US trading partners, with new import duties on a wide range of goods expected, three people with knowledge of the matter told Reuters on Tuesday. The results of the investigation, carried out under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, are expected as soon as Tuesday or Wednesday, and the US Trade Representative's office is expected to propose new duties that would stack on top of previous tariffs already imposed during President Donald Trump's second term.

The announcement, when it comes, would mark the administration's next big move in a trade policy that has defined its second term — and its latest attempt to rebuild a tariff regime after the US Supreme Court struck down the global import taxes at its centre in February. The new duties would partly replace those taxes, giving the administration a fresh legal footing for levies that the court had found to rest on the wrong authority.

What the new duties will actually look like is still unknown. It was unclear what tariff rates would be charged on imports, or what exemptions may be granted, the people familiar with the matter said. The Wall Street Journal had reported earlier that the probe's results could be unveiled as soon as this week, but neither the US Trade Representative's office nor the White House immediately responded to Reuters' queries about the pending announcement.

The scope of the investigation is sweeping. The US Trade Representative has been examining what it called evidence of excess industrial capacity in most of the United States' largest trading partners: China, the European Union, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Switzerland, Norway, Singapore, Bangladesh and Cambodia. Together, those 16 economies account for the overwhelming bulk of American imports, so duties applied across the list would reach deep into the goods Americans buy every day.

The administration's central accusation is aimed squarely at China. It has singled out Beijing as the biggest source of excess industrial capacity, arguing that Chinese factories are flooding global markets with steel, aluminum, electric vehicles, appliances, solar products and other goods, driving down prices and undercutting producers elsewhere. China has rejected the accusations, saying its exports reflect competitive advantages — cheaper energy, scale and manufacturing know-how — rather than unfair state support.

The probe is also partly directed at Chinese investment in Southeast Asia, where Chinese firms have built factories in countries including Vietnam, Indonesia and Cambodia to manufacture goods for the US market — a route the administration sees as a way of avoiding tariffs imposed directly on China. Any duties that follow the probe could therefore hit not only Chinese exports but goods made in the region by Chinese-owned plants, widening the trade fight well beyond Beijing itself.

This is not the administration's first use of the tool. The US Trade Representative launched the excess capacity probe in March, alongside a separate investigation into weak or lax enforcement of anti-forced labor laws in 59 countries and the European Union. In July, the administration imposed import duties of 10% or 12.5% on goods from those 60 trading partners under the forced-labor probe. A Section 301 finding on excess capacity would give it a second, separate legal basis for duties on many of the same countries.

Section 301 itself is an old instrument with a familiar history. The law allows the US Trade Representative to investigate foreign practices it deems unfair and to impose duties in response, and Trump used it in his first term to levy tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods, setting off the US-China trade war of 2018 and 2019. The February Supreme Court ruling, by contrast, struck down tariffs imposed under a different law — the emergency-powers statute the administration had used to impose sweeping global levies. Section 301, with its formal investigation process, is the administration's path back to broad tariffs on firmer legal ground.

The politics of the move are complicated by the cost of the tariffs already in place. The administration's tariffs have in part contributed to higher prices on consumer goods in the United States, and a Reuters/Ipsos opinion poll conducted after November's midterm elections showed that 62% of respondents would support efforts to repeal the tariffs. With the midterm results fresh, a new round of duties risks sharpening a debate the White House has struggled to win: whether tariffs are protecting American industry or taxing American shoppers.

Nowhere is that tension sharper than in the relationship with China. Under a US-China trade truce due to expire on 10 January, the administration has capped second-term tariffs on Chinese goods at 20%, on top of duties of about 25% imposed during Trump's first term. The US has already imposed a 12.5% anti-forced labor duty on all Chinese imports, leaving only 7.5 percentage points of headroom before the 20% cap is reached. One of the people familiar with the matter told Reuters that the 20% cap would likely be maintained as long as the trade truce is extended — but if the truce expires, the excess capacity rate would snap to a higher level, giving Beijing a strong incentive to agree to an extension.

The two sides are also working on a separate effort to cut tariffs on $30 billion of non-strategic imports from each side, under a process known as the “Board of Trade.” It is unclear how any new Section 301 duties would interact with that process, and the uncertainty cuts both ways: a fresh round of tariffs could complicate the talks, or the talks could become the channel through which the new duties are softened or traded away.

For the 16 countries on the list, the stakes are high and the timetable is short. Exporters in Japan, South Korea, the European Union and Mexico — all close US allies and major suppliers — will be watching whether the duties land on them as heavily as on China, and whether exemptions are offered for goods that cannot easily be sourced elsewhere. Businesses that spent the year rerouting supply chains after the February court ruling now face the prospect of yet another redrawing of the tariff map, with only days' notice.

Why it matters: the excess capacity probe is the administration's most consequential trade decision since the Supreme Court struck down its global tariffs in February. If the findings lead to duties across 16 trading partners, they would reshape the cost of imports for American consumers and businesses, test the fragile trade truce with China weeks before it expires, and put the administration's central economic argument — that tariffs rebuild American industry — to a fresh test with voters who, according to the latest polling, are increasingly sceptical. What is announced in the coming days, and at what rates, will set the terms of that argument for the year ahead.
