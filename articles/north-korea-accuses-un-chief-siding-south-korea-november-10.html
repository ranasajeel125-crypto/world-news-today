North Korea Accuses UN Chief of Siding With South Korea Over Border Tensions

world · 10 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Kim Yo Jong, the influential sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, said Guterres took a “prejudiced and irresponsible” attitude by echoing South Korea's claim over the September blast.

North Korea expressed regret on Tuesday over comments by UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres urging the two Koreas to ease tensions through dialogue following a landmine blast at the border, accusing him of taking Seoul's side, state media reported.

Kim Yo Jong, the influential sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, said Guterres took a "prejudiced and irresponsible" attitude by echoing South Korea's unilateral claim over the September blast. "I express my regret over the fact that the top official of the UN, which regards impartiality and objectivity as its lifeline, took such a prejudiced and irresponsible attitude as echoing the ROK's unilateral and provocative sophism," Kim said in a statement carried by the KCNA news agency. ROK is short for the Republic of Korea, South Korea's official name.

"The UN secretary-general's behaviour of echoing words of the provokers and patronising them without having proper understanding of the fact is not helpful to the detente," Kim said. She accused Guterres of staying silent on military actions by the United States and its allies while condemning North Korea's exercise of its sovereign rights as "provocations" and "threats", and warned that if reckless military provocations by South Korea led to an undesirable incident in the southern border area, Guterres would never be free from the blame for it. She also said the UN chief's ignorant and irresolute attitude was pushing the UN into a state of malfunction.

Guterres had said the previous Thursday that he was encouraging the parties concerned to restore channels of communication to prevent unintended accidents or miscalculations, referring to the landmine explosion in the demilitarised zone. UN spokesperson Stephane Dujarric told reporters Guterres encouraged the two Koreas to resume dialogue, including on the military level to avoid unintended accidents. Kim dismissed the call as speaking for South Korea's "shameless" attempt to shift blame for the incident onto the North, and said it was contrary to the principle of strictly maintaining neutrality stipulated in the UN Charter.

The dispute centres on an explosion on 21 September 2026 inside the Demilitarised Zone, the heavily fortified buffer strip that has separated the two Koreas since the Korean War armistice of 1953. At about 10:51 a.m., 18 soldiers from a reconnaissance battalion under the South Korean Army's 25th Infantry Division were clearing a new patrol route in the western sector of the DMZ, near the former Suuksi ferry site along the Imjin River, when two explosions went off in succession. The first wounded a staff sergeant at the head of the column; the second struck as the battalion commander, a lieutenant colonel, moved to help him, severing the commander's foot at the ankle. A noncommissioned engineering officer suffered severe lacerations to the leg, and a third soldier was lightly injured. The two seriously wounded men were evacuated by helicopter to the Armed Forces Capital Hospital in Gyeonggi province.

The soldiers had been preparing for a joint on-site inspection with the United Nations Command, related to North Korea's fortification work along the Military Demarcation Line, by opening a new patrol route for the investigators. Since 2024, North Korea has been building along the northern side of the line — fences, tactical roads and minefields — in work it describes as fortifying its border. South Korea's military had identified terrain changes near the line and asked the UN Command to inspect the area the following week.

A joint investigation by South Korea's military and the UN Command concluded in early October that the explosion was caused by antipersonnel mines deliberately planted by North Korea south of the Military Demarcation Line. The military said on 28 September that a North Korean plastic antipersonnel mine was highly likely the cause, after the National Forensic Service detected traces of TNT on an injured soldier's body armour and found no metal fragments. The final findings, announced on 5 October, rested on three questions: where the blasts occurred, whose mine it was, and whether it had been deliberately planted. The sites of the first and second explosions, and a North Korean plastic antipersonnel mine found at the scene on 29 September, all lay about 10 metres south of the line, and the UN Command assessed that the mines had been deliberately placed and had been in place for at least 12 months. Fragments taken from the soldiers' bodies matched a North Korean plastic antipersonnel mine previously recovered from the Imjin River, and soldiers at the scene testified that the mine they saw was buried 5 to 10 centimetres deep.

It was the first time in 11 years that South Korean troops suffered serious injuries from a North Korean mine, following the wooden box mine incident of 2015, when two South Korean soldiers were maimed by mines in the DMZ and tensions rose sharply before a deal was reached to defuse the crisis. On 5 October, after notifying the North through the UN Command, the South Korean military began clearing the North Korean minefield located south of the line, its first concrete response to the blast, and said it would keep doing so unless the North removed the mines itself.

The dispute then moved to the United Nations. In a letter dated 22 October, South Korea raised the incident with the UN Security Council, calling on Pyongyang to apologise, remove the mines and take steps to prevent a recurrence. In response, the North also sent its own letter, accusing Seoul of fabricating the mine blast and claiming the incident had been staged by South Korea. Pyongyang has denied responsibility for the mine incident and said its troops were conducting an operation in the DMZ to fortify the border and had not violated the UN Charter or international laws.

Tensions have spiked since the blast, and the exchange of letters and statements has shown how little direct communication remains between the two Koreas. Inter-Korean dialogue has been frozen for years, and there are few working channels through which an accident at the border can be clarified before it escalates. That was the point of Guterres's appeal: with soldiers from both sides operating in a mined buffer zone and with no peace treaty ever signed to replace the 1953 armistice, an unintended incident can quickly become a political crisis. North Korea's rejection of that appeal, delivered in the sharp personal language Kim Yo Jong has used in past statements, suggests the mine dispute will remain unresolved, and that the border — mined, fenced and watched by both armies — will stay one of the most dangerous frontiers in the world.
