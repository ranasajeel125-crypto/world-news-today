Five EU Countries to Open Rwanda Return Hub for Rejected Asylum Seekers

world · 17 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The pilot facility would hold about 100 people, roughly 20 from each of the five countries, while their removal from Europe is arranged.

Five European Union countries have announced plans to open a joint “return hub” in Rwanda for people whose asylum claims have been finally rejected, in the most concrete step yet by European governments to move part of migration enforcement outside the bloc. Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands said on Monday that they were immediately starting talks with Rwanda on setting up the facility, with the aim of carrying out the first deportations in 2027, Reuters reported.

The announcement was made in Vienna by Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and his Danish counterpart, Mette Frederiksen, with Rwanda's interior minister, Vincent Biruta, present at the press conference. The Austrian government said in a statement that negotiations should move quickly so that the first deportations of rejected asylum seekers who cannot be sent back to their home countries can take place in 2027.

The hub would hold people who have no legal right to remain in the five countries — those whose asylum claims have been refused and who have exhausted every appeal — while their removal from Europe is arranged. Greek Migration Minister Thanos Plevris told broadcaster ERTNews that only people whose asylum applications have been rejected would be affected, not those whose cases are still pending.

The first phase is expected to be a pilot of about 100 people, with each of the five countries sending around 20, according to BBC reporting. The initial phase would focus on single men, the BBC reported, although Le Monde reported that the plan covers adults, men or women, without dependent families. The location of the facility has not yet been disclosed. No details have been published yet on funding, the size of the facility, or how people held there would access lawyers, contact their families, or challenge their transfer.

People sent to the hub would be offered a choice, according to the BBC: integrate into Rwandan society or return to their home country. Those who stay would receive a residence permit and have access to education, healthcare, the labour market and the legal system, the BBC reported. Rwandan Interior Minister Vincent Biruta said transferred people would also have the option of relocation to a third country. The five European countries hope to obtain support from the International Organization for Migration and the United Nations refugee agency, and the IOM said it was in discussions with the governments involved.

Stocker said the five countries wanted to relieve European asylum systems and stop traffickers arranging deadly trips across the Mediterranean, while Frederiksen called the plan a “game changer” for confronting illegal migration. German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said negotiations between the European capitals and Kigali were expected to wrap up in early 2027, with implementation set for later that year.

The plan has been made possible by the European Union's new Return Regulation, which entered into force on 16 October 2026 and replaces the 2008 Return Directive. The regulation creates a European Return Order, making a return decision issued in one member state enforceable elsewhere in the EU without reopening the case, and it expressly permits member states to establish return hubs in non-EU countries for people subject to return decisions. Such agreements may only be concluded with third countries that uphold human rights, international law and non-refoulement — the prohibition on returning people to territories where they face persecution, torture or other serious harm — and unaccompanied minors may not be transferred to return hubs. Where onward return is envisaged, independent monitoring is required.

The EU version differs in an important respect from Britain's abandoned Rwanda scheme. The British plan, announced in 2022 and dropped by the Labour government in 2024, would have sent people to Rwanda to have their asylum claims processed there. The EU hub would not process asylum claims at all; it would hold only people who have already received final return decisions while EU states arrange their removal to their countries of origin. Italy has separately opened centres in Albania for asylum processing, another example of European governments looking outside the bloc for migration enforcement.

The announcement is the first concrete use of the new returns framework, which EU interior ministers finalised last month and which the European Parliament approved earlier this year as part of the toughest overhaul of the bloc's migration rules in years. The regulation also raises maximum detention periods for return purposes to 24 months and creates the EU's first shared list of safe countries of origin. Officials have cited low return rates as the problem the reform is meant to fix: only around a fifth to a quarter of people ordered to leave the EU are actually returned, according to figures cited in reporting on the package.

The plan has already drawn questions from rights advocates and opposition politicians, who have asked how people transferred to Rwanda would be able to challenge their removal, how long they could be held, and who would be accountable if conditions in the hub fell short of the standards the regulation requires. The five governments say the facility would be an open centre where people receive support and have access to training, employment, education and health services, rather than a closed detention site. Whether that description holds in practice will depend on the agreement now being negotiated with Kigali, which has not yet been published.

Other governments are watching closely. The BBC reported that Downing Street has not ruled out the United Kingdom joining the scheme, saying it would watch how it develops — a notable shift given that the previous British government abandoned its own Rwanda plan. Several other EU states have expressed interest in similar arrangements, with Uganda, Uzbekistan and Albania among the countries previously discussed as possible hosts, according to reporting on the returns negotiations.

Why it matters: for the first time, European Union countries are putting the bloc's new returns law into practice by sending rejected asylum seekers to a facility on another continent. The pilot is small — about 100 people — but the principle is large: if the Rwanda hub works as its backers intend, it could become the template for a wider network of return hubs, and other governments, including Britain, may seek to join. If it stalls on legal challenges, costs or conditions on the ground, it will join the list of European migration schemes that promised more than they delivered. The negotiations in the coming months, and the first transfers aimed for 2027, will show which of those two stories this becomes.
