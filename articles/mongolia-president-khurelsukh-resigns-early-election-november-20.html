Mongolia's President Resigns Amid Corruption Claims, Setting Up Early Election

politics · 20 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (Politics Desk)

President Khurelsukh Ukhnaa attending a parliament session on his resignation in Ulaanbaatar, where lawmakers voted 96 to 8 to accept his request to step down.

Mongolia's parliament has approved the resignation of President Khurelsukh Ukhnaa, two days after he asked to step down amid accusations by rivals of corruption, setting the stage for an early presidential election. It is the first time a sitting Mongolian president has quit before completing a term, and it has lifted the lid on the political infighting inside the coal-rich nation.

The State Great Khural, Mongolia's parliament, voted 96 to 8 to accept the resignation on Thursday, according to the state news agency Montsame, with 96 of the 104 members present voting in favour. Under Mongolian law the parliament must approve a president's resignation, and its decision took effect immediately. Parliament must now call an election within four months, with parliament speaker Byambatsogt Sandag exercising presidential powers in the meantime.

Khurelsukh announced his decision on Tuesday morning, saying he would cooperate with an investigation so he could clear his name. “I will bring those who are defaming me before the relevant authorities and have my honour restored,” he said at a press briefing announcing his resignation, according to Montsame. “I have never lied, stole or deceived anyone,” he added. He has rejected the accusations against him.

The row began with a Facebook livestream on Saturday by Nyambaatar Khishgee, a former mayor of the capital, Ulaanbaatar, and a former justice minister. Nyambaatar said there was corruption involved in a railway project, and the opposition Democratic Party then began calling for Khurelsukh to go. Nyambaatar belongs to a rival faction of the ruling Mongolian People's Party, the party with which Khurelsukh has long been affiliated.

The allegation centres on the Tavan Tolgoi–Gashuunsukhait railway, a coal transport line built to carry coal from the Tavan Tolgoi mining area towards the Gashuunsukhait border crossing with China. Nyambaatar accused Khurelsukh of bypassing tender procedures seven years ago, while serving as prime minister, to award the railway construction contract to an associate at more than double its actual value, pocketing the difference alongside other party politicians. The contract was awarded to the Mongolian conglomerate Bodi International in 2019, when Khurelsukh was prime minister, and Nyambaatar put the alleged payments at $25 million. The opposition Democratic Party has called for the public release of advance sale contracts involving the state coal miner Erdenes Tavan Tolgoi. None of the allegations has been proven, and Khurelsukh denies any wrongdoing.

His departure showed the depth of the divisions in Mongolian politics, said Xu Tianchen, a senior analyst at the Economist Intelligence Unit. “Not only is there intense hostility between the ruling Mongolian People's Party and the opposition Democratic Party, but there are also factional struggles within the Mongolian People's Party itself,” Xu said. The resignation was driven as much by a fight inside the ruling party as by the opposition's demands.

The timing of the resignation has also drawn attention. Khurelsukh's term had been due to end in June 2027, and the constitution barred him from running again, so he was giving up only the final months of a single term. Some analysts have suggested the early exit may be a tactical move to weather the scandal and return later as party chairman or prime minister, though Khurelsukh himself has said only that he is stepping aside to prove his innocence.

Before leaving office, Khurelsukh called for a constitutional referendum to be held alongside the coming presidential election. He suggested either introducing a bicameral parliament that would elect the president, or shifting to a presidential system with greater executive powers. Parliament has yet to announce any referendum on the proposed changes, and no date has been set for the election itself, which must be announced within 20 working days of the vacancy and held at least 70 days after that announcement.

The resignation is the latest in a run of corruption scandals to topple Mongolia's leaders. Anger over accusations of corruption has repeatedly boiled over into street protests in recent years. In June 2025, then prime minister L. Oyun-Erdene stepped down after losing parliamentary support following corruption allegations that he denied. His successor, Zandanshatar Gombojav, resigned in March 2026 amid a parliamentary boycott by the opposition party and mounting infighting within the ruling party. Khurelsukh himself resigned as prime minister in 2021, following protests over the government's handling of the Covid-19 pandemic, and was elected president later that same year.

That pattern matters because of what is at stake in the railway contract at the centre of the claims. Mongolia is a landlocked country between Russia and China, and coal is one of its most important exports, sold overwhelmingly to China. A railway carrying coal from the vast Tavan Tolgoi deposits to the Chinese border is not a minor piece of infrastructure: it is part of the route by which the country's main source of income reaches its main customer. Allegations that such a contract was awarded at an inflated price, and that the difference was pocketed by politicians, go to the heart of how Mongolia's mineral wealth is managed.

The coming election will therefore be about more than choosing a successor. Voters will be asked to pick a president months earlier than planned, while the investigation Khurelsukh says he welcomes has yet to produce any finding, and while his call for a constitutional referendum hangs unanswered. The Democratic Party, which demanded his resignation and holds enough seats to force a parliamentary inquiry, will be pressing for the contracts and the money trail to be made public. The ruling party, meanwhile, must find a candidate to defend a presidency its own most prominent figure has just vacated under a cloud.

What happens next is set by the calendar. Parliament must call the election within four months, and the vote must be held by March 2027 at the latest. Until a new president is elected and sworn in, Speaker Byambatsogt carries out the duties of the presidency. Whether the investigation into the railway contract moves forward, and whether parliament takes up the referendum proposal, will shape whether the election is fought on the corruption claims themselves or on the wider question of how Mongolia should be governed.

Why it matters: a president resigning before the end of his term is a first in Mongolia's democratic history, and it is the third time in less than two years that corruption allegations have ended a leader's time in office. It exposes a ruling party split into factions that are now accusing each other in public, and it leaves the presidency in caretaker hands while the country prepares an election it was not expecting until 2027. The claims about the coal railway have not been proven, and the president denies them, but the political damage is already done: Mongolia's voters will now choose their next president against a backdrop of scandal, and the winner will inherit both the investigation and the question of whether the system that produced three fallen leaders in under two years needs to change.
