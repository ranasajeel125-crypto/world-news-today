Gunman Kills Six and Wounds 15 in Krakow's Main Market Square

Six people were killed and 15 injured when a gunman opened fire in the Main Market Square in Krakow, Poland, on Saturday afternoon, 7 November, police said. The attack, in one of Europe's most visited historic squares, was the deadliest mass shooting in Poland in living memory, and it prompted two days of national mourning.

Krakow police said a man drove onto the square at around 3 p.m. local time and began firing at random people from his car. The gunman, a 24-year-old Polish citizen from Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, a town about 150 kilometres northeast of Krakow, drove a black Subaru with local number plates into the square and fired from the driver's side window into the crowd. Gunshots were heard for over a minute, and the attack lasted around three minutes, during which the glass fronts of shops were shattered by bullets and a tram was shot at. People fled in panic as police sirens sounded, and a medical air rescue helicopter landed in the square as emergency services responded to the scene.

The suspect was wearing military-style clothing, including tactical gear and a bulletproof vest, police said, and used a semi-automatic rifle similar to a US AR-15. He was shot by officers during a gunfight about a kilometre south of the square, after exchanging fire with police, and was taken to hospital. Two police officers were among the injured, one of them in a critical condition with three gunshot wounds, Poland's interior minister said. The officers were later named as Senior Constable Jakub Kulik and Senior Constable Artur Morański. Police said the gunman was apprehended within about six minutes of the first emergency call, and that he was not a member of the Polish armed forces.

Early reports from the fire service, carried by TVN24, put the toll at two dead and at least 10 injured, Reuters reported. Police later confirmed five people had been killed; the toll rose to six after another victim died in hospital, police said. Four women and two men were among the dead. Three of the victims were Polish, one was a British national, and two had not been identified. Five foreign nationals were among the wounded, from Austria, Germany, Portugal, Russia and Britain. A witness who gave his name only as Konrad told the news website Interia: "I saw shots being fired from the driver's side window, and smoke was coming out. I ran to the police station in the main market square; when I burst inside, it was full of people hiding on the floor."

Prime Minister Donald Tusk said police were investigating the "tragic shooting" and that he was being updated by the interior ministry. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said everything indicated the gunman acted alone and held "very radical views." Police said they were not looking for a second person and had not confirmed a motive. Kierwiński later said the suspect appeared to be "fascinated by far-right ideologies" and held "ultra-nationalist views", and Tusk later told reporters in Krakow that the shooting was an act of "terrorism". The justice minister said the gunman had acted with the aim of seriously intimidating a large number of people, making it a terrorist offence. A massive armed police presence was deployed across Krakow on Saturday evening, and security measures were ramped up in Warsaw and elsewhere.

The investigation widened sharply in the hours after the attack. Counter-terrorism police searching the suspect's home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski found three bodies — two women and a man — and Tusk said there was much to suggest they were members of the suspect's immediate family. Police later confirmed they were his father, mother and sister, and the prosecutor's office said it was highly likely the same person had carried out those killings, though investigations were continuing. That brought the total death toll linked to the gunman to nine. The suspect, named by Polish public broadcaster TVP as Michał Oksiński, had no criminal record, held a legal gun permit, and was a member of a shooting club, where he was described as a highly skilled marksman. A number of weapons and ammunition were found in the car he was driving, and emblems on his clothing were being examined by experts. It was not immediately clear whether the firearm used in the attack, or the guns found in his car, were legal.

The gunman died in hospital at 4.30 a.m. on Monday, 9 November, two days after the attack, after undergoing multiple surgeries and remaining in critical condition. Thousands of Poles gathered in Krakow that evening to pay tribute to the victims, and Tusk took part in a silent march through the city. A period of mourning was declared in Krakow until 11 November, and two days of national mourning were announced across Poland. Blood donation appeals were launched for the critically injured police officer.

Police said the investigation was continuing. Public shootings are rare in Poland, where gun laws are stricter than in the United States. The Main Market Square, the Rynek Główny, is the largest medieval town square in Europe and the heart of Krakow's Old Town, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, lined with restaurants and cafes and busy with tourists and horse-drawn carriages. The attack has reopened a debate in Poland about gun licensing, online radicalisation and the security of public spaces, and about how a young man with a legal permit and a history of shooting-club membership came to carry out the worst mass shooting the country has seen.
