Brazil Chooses Between Lula and Bolsonaro's Son in Knife-Edge Contest

politics · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Rafael Costa (Latin America Correspondent)

Voters queue at a polling station in Brazil. The contest between the Lula and Bolsonaro camps has split the country.

Brazil went to the polls in a knife-edge election that pits President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva against the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro — a dynastic face-off that has divided Latin America's largest democracy down the middle.

For Lula's supporters, the vote is about protecting social programmes, the Amazon and Brazil's democratic institutions. For the Bolsonaro camp, it is about economic freedom, law and order, and ending what they portray as a lurch to the left. Polls going into the weekend suggested a contest too close to call.

The campaign has been fought as much on social media as on the stump, with both sides accusing the other of spreading disinformation. Brazil's electoral court has been under intense pressure to police online content while keeping the vote free and fair.

The result will shape far more than Brazil. The winner inherits an economy watched nervously by markets, a Congress that will constrain any radical programme, and a foreign policy that balances Washington, Beijing and the BRICS bloc. Counting is expected to be fast, but with margins this thin, both camps are preparing for a long night.

The result, when it came, was a narrow victory for the incumbent. On Sunday, October 25, Lula defeated Senator Flavio Bolsonaro in the run-off, winning 51.8 percent of the vote against 48.2 percent for his rival, according to the Superior Electoral Court, with more than 99 percent of votes counted. More than 64 million ballots were cast for Lula and more than 59 million for Bolsonaro, a margin of about 4.4 million votes. Shortly after 9pm Brasilia time, the electoral court called the race for Lula, and the 80-year-old president took to a flag-draped podium to give his victory speech, coughing and audibly hoarse, standing alongside his wife Rosangela and his vice president, Geraldo Alckmin.

The victory completed a remarkable comeback. Lula had come into the run-off as the underdog after losing the first round on October 4, when he earned 45 percent of the vote against 47 percent for Bolsonaro. It was the first time since the return of direct presidential elections in 1989 that a candidate had won the presidency after finishing second in the first round. Lula persuaded millions of Brazilians who had skipped the first round to cast ballots for him in the second, despite heavy rains that pounded much of the country.

The win gives Lula an unprecedented fourth non-consecutive term. He will be sworn in on January 5, 2027, when he will beat his own record as the oldest Brazilian president to take the oath of office since the country's return to democracy — he turns 81 two days after the election. If he completes the term, he will have served 16 non-consecutive years in office, matching Angela Merkel's tenure as German chancellor and placing him among a small group of modern democracy's longest-serving elected leaders.

The contest was a rematch by proxy. Lula narrowly defeated Jair Bolsonaro in 2022, winning 50.9 percent of the vote to 49.1 percent, and the former president was subsequently convicted and sentenced to 27 years in prison for plotting a coup to prevent Lula from taking office. Rather than fade away, the Bolsonaro movement rallied behind his eldest son, turning this year's election into another bruising battle to keep the family out of power. Flavio Bolsonaro, a senator for the Liberal Party, campaigned as the heir to his father's movement, aligned with Donald Trump's United States, and accused Lula of corruption, raising allegations involving the president's eldest son.

The campaign was dominated by concerns over the cost of living, crime and corruption, and it was fought bitterly online. Polling late in the campaign showed a divided electorate: an October 15 Datafolha survey put Lula ahead among women by 49 percent to 40 percent, while Flavio led among men by 52 percent to 40 percent. The spectre of a return to a more radical form of Bolsonarism, aligned with Washington, drove many Brazilians to turn out in large numbers to give the left another chance.

The aftermath was immediately contested. Flavio Bolsonaro refused to concede, telling supporters in Brasilia: "It is impossible to recognize the election result given how these events unfolded." He said his team would mount a legal challenge over the Supreme Court's last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours. Lula's Workers' Party had requested the extension after reports that some local governments had reduced the number of buses serving poorer voters, who tend to support the president. Bolsonaro argued that some polling stations were not able to reopen in time, harming any additional votes he might have earned. His allies also accused Lula's supporters of reducing bus availability in an attempt to dampen the president's working-class base — a charge the other side levelled in reverse.

The result has consequences well beyond Brazil's borders. Lula's win keeps Latin America's largest country on a more independent foreign-policy path, with an emphasis on national sovereignty, the protection of the Amazon, social justice and closer ties with the Global South and China, amid tensions with the US administration. Before polls closed on Sunday, US President Donald Trump said he was "monitoring" Brazil's presidential run-off.

At home, Lula faces a difficult Congress. The Bolsonaro family's Liberal Party emerged as the largest in both chambers, winning 121 of 513 seats in the lower house and a 28-strong bloc in the 81-seat Senate, leaving Lula increasingly dependent on deals with conservative and centrist lawmakers to advance his agenda. The narrow margin of his victory — and his rival's refusal to accept it — also means the new term begins under the shadow of a legal challenge and a deeply divided country, with the new president's promise to defend democracy, the Amazon and social programmes now tested against a Congress in which his opponents hold the largest bloc.
