Britain and EU Set to Agree Youth Mobility Scheme and Food Trade Reset at Brussels Summit

politics · 20 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Europe Desk)

Prime Minister Andy Burnham meets European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa in Brussels on Friday, a decade after Britain voted to leave the EU.

Britain and the European Union are set to agree on easing food trade and youth mobility in a reset of ties at a summit in Brussels on Friday, in the biggest step towards closer relations between the two sides since Britain left the bloc. The package also links the two sides' carbon markets, but it is expected to leave the thorniest questions, over university fees and Britain's acceptance of EU rules, for later.

British Prime Minister Andy Burnham is due to meet European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa at the summit, which was postponed from July following the resignation of Burnham's predecessor, Keir Starmer. The BBC understands the youth mobility scheme is expected to be approved at the meeting, with new deals on food trade and carbon emissions also on the table.

The most significant agreement in the package would allow cross-border trade in animals, plants and related products, such as sausages or seeds, without the certificates and controls currently required. Britain has estimated the change could add up to 5.1 billion pounds ($6.70 billion) a year to its economy, by cutting the red tape that has burdened food exporters since Brexit.

A further agreement would link the emissions trading systems of Britain and the European Union, exempting British and EU goods, such as steel, from each other's carbon border levies. The move would remove a growing source of friction for heavy industry on both sides of the Channel, where carbon charges threatened to add costs to cross-border trade.

The youth mobility scheme would let young people from each side live, work, study, volunteer or travel in the other's territory for up to three years. It would be open to people aged 18 to 31 on the first day of their stay, with a cap of 100,000 places a year in each direction, meaning up to 200,000 young people a year in total could take part.

The cap was the product of hard bargaining. Britain, mindful that immigration was a factor behind the Brexit vote, had proposed letting in only 50,000 young people a year, later raising its offer to 80,000. The EU wanted an uncapped scheme. Negotiators settled on 100,000, EU diplomats said. Downing Street has not commented on the details and said on Thursday that the talks were going "down to the wire" ahead of Friday's summit, and nothing is final until the leaders sign off.

The scheme matters because it restores a freedom that ended with Brexit. Before Britain left the EU, young Britons could live and work anywhere in the bloc without a visa, and young Europeans could do the same in Britain. That ended in 2021, and since then young people on both sides have faced work permits, visa fees and paperwork for even short stays. For a generation that grew up with free movement as normal, the new scheme is the first real reopening of the door.

The deal has already become a political fight at home. At prime minister's questions this week, Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch challenged Burnham over the prospect of allowing more young Europeans to work in Britain while young people already in the country struggled to find jobs. She claimed there were nearly 200,000 fewer vacancies since Labour took office and accused Burnham of preparing to agree to another 100,000 young people coming to Britain to compete for work.

Burnham defended the package as a growth measure. He said the agreement would help reverse a decade of weak growth by reducing red tape and the cost of exporting to Europe, and that it would bring food prices down, noting that retailer M&S says it will ease pressure on the price of the weekly shop. He also argued it would bring greater collaboration on immigration, including on Channel crossings.

The prime minister has gone further than any of his predecessors since Brexit in questioning the settlement itself. Burnham has said Britain should consider options for its future relationship with the European Union, including rejoining the bloc, and on Monday he said it was the "obvious truth" that Britain had lost more than it had gained from Brexit. After Friday's summit, he wants to open up a wider conversation about Britain's future relationship with Europe, possibly paving a path back to re-joining the trade bloc.

That ambition is also why the summit is expected to expose lingering divisions. The two sides have differed over tuition fees, with EU countries wanting their students to be eligible for "home fees" at British universities, rather than the international rate, which is typically some three times higher. Britain prefers to offer only "significantly reduced" fees. The two sides are now aiming to resolve the issue within six months, leaving it hanging over the reset.

Then there is the question of Britain accepting EU rules, a sensitive issue for Brexit supporters. A key UK demand is that the EU grant it special status under the bloc's "Made in Europe" rules, notably for cars. The EU has indicated it is willing to grant this, but only on condition that Britain align with its policies, which could mean Britain having to impose tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles. The draft joint statement had featured conflicting lines from Britain and the EU about the extent of economic coordination, a sign of how far apart the two sides remain on the deeper questions.

The context for the reset is a decade of distance and a world that has changed around both sides. A decade after Britain voted to leave the now 27-nation bloc, rancour over the divorce has faded and both sides have recognised the need for a closer alliance in a world order upset by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, US tariffs, Chinese control of critical minerals and war in Iran. The summit is an attempt to address a small part of the losses from Brexit with practical agreements, rather than to relitigate the referendum itself.

For businesses, the food trade deal is the most concrete gain. Exporters of meat, dairy, plants and seeds have faced veterinary checks, health certificates and border delays since Britain left the EU's single market, adding costs that hit small producers hardest. Removing those controls would restore something close to the old frictionless trade for those goods, and the government's estimate of up to 5.1 billion pounds a year in economic benefit reflects how much the friction has cost.

For young people, the mobility scheme is the headline. A three-year stay is far longer than a holiday visa and long enough to build a career, learn a language or complete a degree, and the 18-to-31 age range covers the years when most people first live abroad. The cap of 100,000 a year in each direction means the scheme will be large, but not unlimited, and demand is likely to outstrip places in its first years.

What happens next will decide whether Friday is a turning point or a modest repair. If the leaders sign off the package as expected, the agreements still need to be implemented, and the unresolved issues, university fees within six months and the rules-alignment question, will test whether the reset has momentum or stalls. Burnham's wider conversation about Britain's future relationship with Europe will also begin in earnest after the summit, and his willingness to talk openly about rejoining sets this reset apart from the cautious steps of his predecessors. For now, the summit offers something neither side has had for a decade: a deal that makes Britain and the EU measurably closer than they were the day before.

Why it matters: the Brussels package is the first substantial UK-EU agreement since Brexit that touches ordinary life, young people able to live and work across the Channel, cheaper food trade, and cleaner terms for steel and other industries. Its limits are just as telling: fees unsettled, rules alignment contested, and nothing signed until the leaders meet. The summit will show whether Britain and Europe are ready to rebuild the relationship, or only to patch it.
