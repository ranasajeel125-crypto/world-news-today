US Military Fires on Vessel Trying to Break Iran Blockade in Gulf of Oman

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

US Central Command said the vessel tried to leave regional waters after visiting an Iranian port; it estimates the months-long naval blockade has kept more than $25 billion of Iranian crude oil from being exported.

An American military aircraft fired on and disabled a commercial vessel that tried to break the US blockade of Iran on Saturday, US Central Command said.

US Central Command forces disabled a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman on 7 November, after the ship's civilian crew ignored repeated warnings and attempted to violate the naval blockade against Iran multiple times. A US military aircraft fired precision munitions at the stern of the St. Kitts-flagged M/V Huda, disabling the ship after Central Command forces directed the crew to abandon targeted spaces. The cargo vessel tried to depart regional waters after visiting an Iranian port. Central Command released video showing the strike on the ship's stern.

The statement did not provide the crew's response, identify the cargo or specify the ship's destination, and it did not specify the exact strike time. What it made clear was the enforcement posture: the blockade is being enforced against vessels departing Iranian ports, not only ships attempting to enter them. The action followed the vessel's civilian crew ignoring repeated warnings and making several attempts to breach the naval blockade. According to official US military data cited in reporting on the statement, blockade operations underway since July had resulted in US forces redirecting 147 commercial vessels that complied with interdiction protocols. Central Command estimated that its naval blockade has prevented Iran from exporting more than $25 billion in crude oil.

Building on the months-long naval blockade, the US military also said on Saturday it is launching a new task force to crack down on Tehran's movement of advanced weapons and other sanctioned material over its land borders and through the Caspian Sea. Task Force Iron Gate, as it will be called, will be based in Tampa, Florida, and will coordinate with other US government agencies. Beyond the movement of weapons, it will also focus on dual-use goods and illegal revenue streams, Reuters reported, citing a statement from Central Command seen ahead of its release. The task force will be made up of an internal team of around 20 civilian and military personnel. An accompanying graphic described its mission as "monitor, detect and disrupt" illicit movements.

"Strengthening CENTCOM's collaboration with US interagency partners will further constrain Iran's ability to finance terrorism, rebuild weapons factories, and replenish their military," Admiral Brad Cooper, the commander of Central Command, said in the statement. "The establishment of Task Force Iron Gate reinforces the success of our ironclad blockade against Iran at sea," Cooper added.

The task force aims to find out where weapons systems are coming in, how they could potentially be cut off, and how Iran is transporting its energy, Michael Mulroy, a former US deputy assistant defence secretary for the Middle East, told Al Jazeera. The Caspian Sea connects Iran with Russia and countries in Central Asia and the Caucasus, and it is one of the most difficult areas for Washington to target. US media have reported that Russia has sent drone components and explosives to Iran via the Caspian Sea, though such shipments remain difficult to independently verify. A recent Reuters investigation found that Iran had continued to import military-related equipment through a network involving maritime transportation from Russia, trucks from Central Asia and cargo aircraft from China, despite existing US restrictions — at least $2 billion in hardware, explosives and heavy-duty trucks between January 2025 and June 2026.

Analysts say a small intelligence-focused unit cannot seal Iran's borders or stop its extensive procurement networks without regional cooperation. "I see Task Force Iron Gate as a desperate attempt to extend the American naval blockade into Iran's continental supply networks without deploying American troops along its borders," Andreas Krieg, an associate professor of defence studies at King's College London, told Al Jazeera. According to Matthew Savill, director of military sciences at the Royal United Services Institute, the US mission would rely on a combination of satellite imagery, drones, intercepted communications, financial intelligence and intelligence-sharing. Analysts said the task force could coordinate sanctions enforcement, pressure foreign suppliers and work with partner countries to disrupt smuggling. Former Trump aide and top counterterrorism official Joe Kent, who resigned in March due to the US's war on Iran, said the new task force was an attempt by Central Command to subvert ongoing diplomatic efforts.

The incident comes as President Donald Trump has been threatening to carry out a new offensive against Iran, while Tehran has threatened broad retaliation against Gulf infrastructure, putting countries in the region on edge. On Sunday, the day after Central Command announced the task force, Trump posted on his Truth Social account that Iran was sealed off. It follows Iran's announcement on Saturday that it has lifted its self-imposed limit of about 2,000 km on ballistic missile ranges. Iran state media also reported on Saturday evening the sound of explosions on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, as well as several explosions in the port of Jask, and the Iranian military said late on Saturday that two tankers had hit mines in the Hormuz area and caught fire. Those reports have not been independently verified. The disclosure of the Huda strike came in the wake of at least two instances in which Central Command did not announce military strikes it had carried out in the region, and officials at the command declined to confirm strikes when asked about local reports of explosions.

Meanwhile, Qatar and other regional mediators have intensified efforts to prevent a return to full-scale fighting, even as the confrontation between Washington and Tehran escalates. The Huda incident, the missile announcement and the new task force all point in the same direction: a pressure campaign that began at sea is now being extended to land and to the Caspian, while diplomacy runs in parallel and the Gulf states watch for the retaliation Tehran has threatened against their infrastructure.
