2026 Set to Be One of the Four Hottest Years on Record, UN Report Says

climate · 9 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Every one of the past 12 years now ranks among the 12 warmest ever recorded, the WMO said; 2026 is all but certain to be the second year after 2024 to pass 1.5 degrees above pre-industrial levels.

2026 is on track to be one of the four hottest years on record, driven by a powerful El Niño and extending a warming streak of more than a decade, the United Nations' weather agency said on Monday.

The World Meteorological Organization's annual State of the Climate report showed that between January and September this year, the global average temperature was 1.47 degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial average, and that it could rise further because of a major El Niño, Reuters reported.

The report, which draws on nine data sets, showed that each of the past 12 years, including 2026, was ranked among the 12 warmest ever recorded since records began in around 1850.

"Every indicator here is telling the same story. We're seeing the heat trapped by greenhouse gasses is showing up in the ocean, in the atmosphere, in the melting of ice and the rising of sea levels," John Kennedy, the WMO's head of climate information, told reporters in Geneva ahead of the climate conference.

The planet is increasingly accumulating heat, the report said, with ocean temperatures continuing to rise this year from all-time highs reached in 2025. Global mean sea levels have also reached a record this year as glaciers and ice sheets melt and seawater expands as it heats, it said.

The European Union's Copernicus climate service has already said 2026 was all but certain to become the second calendar year after 2024 to exceed the threshold of 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels set in the 2015 Paris Agreement.

The findings land as countries gather in Turkey from 9 to 20 November for the next round of climate talks, COP31, in Antalya, where the WMO report provides the latest available data to inform their discussions.

The WMO State of the Climate Update 2026 was released on the first day of COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, as an authoritative, science-based report. It includes data, where available, up to September 2026, and therefore does not reflect the most recent impacts of El Niño. The final State of the Global Climate 2026 report will be issued in March 2027, incorporating scientific contributions from dozens of national meteorological and hydrological services, regional climate centres, United Nations partners and experts from the WMO community.

The headline figure is precise: the global mean near-surface temperature anomaly averaged from January to September 2026 was 1.47 degrees Celsius, plus or minus 0.13 degrees, above the 1850–1900 average used as the pre-industrial baseline. That figure is comparable to the whole-year averages for 2025 (1.43 degrees) and 2023 (1.46 degrees) and lower than the 2024 record (1.55 degrees). It is based on nine internationally recognised datasets. The past four years, 2023 to 2026, are the four warmest years on record, and the past 12 years have individually been the warmest on record.

The figure is likely to rise through the end of the year because of the warming effect of El Niño, which has been intensifying since August and is expected to be the strongest on record by December. La Niña conditions at the start of the year had a temporary cooling effect, which was reversed by the development of El Niño. The WMO described the situation as a very strong El Niño event set to unleash even more heat and extreme weather well into 2027, colliding with a planet already superheated by greenhouse gases.

El Niño is a natural climate pattern in which the surface waters of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean become unusually warm, shifting weather patterns around the world. It typically brings hotter global temperatures, changes in rainfall, and more extreme weather, including droughts in some regions and floods in others. When a strong El Niño follows years in which the planet is already running hot because of greenhouse gas emissions, its warming effect adds to an already elevated baseline, pushing global temperatures to new highs.

The oceans tell the same story. Ocean heat is the highest on record for the tenth consecutive year, and ocean temperatures continued to rise this year from all-time highs reached in 2025. The oceans absorb most of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases, and warmer oceans drive sea level rise, fuel stronger storms and disrupt marine ecosystems. Global mean sea levels have also reached a record this year as glaciers and ice sheets melt and seawater expands as it heats.

The 1.5-degree threshold matters because it is the level set in the 2015 Paris Agreement, under which countries agreed to pursue efforts to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees above pre-industrial levels. The European Union's Copernicus climate service has already said 2026 was all but certain to become the second calendar year after 2024 to exceed that threshold. A single year above 1.5 degrees does not mean the Paris goal has been breached — the agreement is judged over longer periods — but two of the past three years crossing the line underlines how close the planet is running to the limit.

The findings landed as countries gathered in Antalya, Türkiye, from November 9 to 20 for COP31, the 31st Conference of the Parties on climate. The WMO report provides the latest available data to inform their discussions, and its message was blunt: every indicator is telling the same story, with the heat trapped by greenhouse gases showing up in the ocean, in the atmosphere, in the melting of ice and in the rising of sea levels. The report's title captured the mood: the state of the climate in 2026, the WMO said, gives new meaning to the phrase "off the charts".
