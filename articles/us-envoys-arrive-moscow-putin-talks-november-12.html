US Envoys Arrive in Moscow for Talks with Putin After Abu Dhabi Talks End Without Breakthrough

world · 12 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Kirill Dmitriev, Mr Putin’s special envoy, met Mr Kushner and Mr Witkoff off their plane amid wet snow and accompanied them to central Moscow ahead of the talks.

US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff arrived in Moscow on Thursday for talks with Russian President Vladimir Putin, part of their latest push for a peace deal in Ukraine.

The Kremlin talks, expected to take place in the first half of the day, will be the 10th time that Witkoff has met Putin in person, and follow two days of trilateral peace talks in Abu Dhabi which finished on Wednesday with news of another planned major prisoner exchange but no public breakthrough in wider negotiations.

Kirill Dmitriev, Putin's special envoy, met Kushner and Witkoff off their plane amid wet snow at Vnukovo International Airport and accompanied them to central Moscow ahead of their talks with Putin. Dmitriev, who heads Russia's sovereign wealth fund and has become one of the Kremlin's main channels to Washington, posted a video of the pair arriving on social media, writing "Welcoming Steve and Jared to Moscow."

This week's Abu Dhabi talks were the first direct negotiations between Russia and Ukraine mediated by the US to try to end the four-and-a-half-year-old war in nine months, and, although Russian and Ukrainian negotiators reported some progress, the US side said differences remained on major issues, including territory. The talks in the capital of the United Arab Emirates were the first direct discussions between Russian and Ukrainian officials in nine months, as the push for peace stalled this year.

Despite public optimism from US President Donald Trump, there has been no sign of major compromises. Kyiv has strongly rejected Putin's demands to surrender the whole of the eastern region of Donbas in return for peace, and with talks deadlocked on land, US mediators have tried to steer discussions towards postwar security guarantees and reconstruction.

The Moscow meeting took place on the heels of the Abu Dhabi talks, and the envoys appeared keen to seize the moment, going straight to the Kremlin. The discussions were described by a US official as a "constructive and substantive discussion" about ending the Ukraine war, and the official said the talks "focused on potential pathways to ending the war, addressing the security concerns of both sides, and establishing the conditions for a durable peace." The Kremlin said the talks were useful, and added that the American side supported Putin's proposal for a joint humanitarian mission to a city in eastern Ukraine.

Putin indicated during the meeting that Russia will not compromise on its hardline demands to end the offensive, but offered a short-term ceasefire in a key frontline city. He proposed a five-day demining window for a humanitarian operation in Kostiantynivka, a city in the Donbas that has been heavily fought over, while rejecting Ukrainian buffer zones and warning that foreign peacekeepers in Ukraine would be considered legitimate military targets. Usually held behind closed doors, the Kremlin chose to televise a lengthy opening exchange between Putin and the US envoys.

Witkoff and Kushner left the Kremlin on Thursday afternoon and were set to travel straight to Dublin for talks with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, who was in the Irish capital to meet European leaders. The envoys are attempting to build on the recent trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi in their latest round of shuttle diplomacy, after prior back-to-back talks with Putin and Zelensky in September and a follow-up meeting between Witkoff and Putin in October in St. Petersburg.

The shuttle diplomacy reflects the structure of the peace effort. There is no single negotiating table at which Russia, Ukraine and the United States sit together. Instead, the US envoys move between Moscow, Kyiv and neutral venues such as Abu Dhabi, carrying proposals and testing positions, while the Russians and Ukrainians meet only occasionally, and only with American mediation. The Abu Dhabi talks were the first time the two sides had met directly in nine months, and the fact that they agreed on another prisoner exchange, even without a wider breakthrough, was presented as evidence that the channel was still alive.

The central obstacle remains territory. Russia demands that Ukraine surrender the whole of Donbas, the eastern region that includes the Donetsk and Luhansk provinces, in return for peace. Ukraine has strongly rejected that demand, and its position is that it will not give up land that Russian forces have not captured on the battlefield. The US mediators have tried to steer the discussion away from territory and towards postwar security guarantees and reconstruction, in the hope that progress on those issues might create momentum for a broader deal, but so far the gap on land has proved too wide to bridge.

A senior Russian diplomat said on Wednesday that Moscow could conclude non-aggression pacts with NATO member states as part of a new security architecture for Europe, though many European politicians say they oppose any discussion of Europe's overall security before real progress on Ukraine, including a ceasefire, is made. The suggestion is part of Russia's long-standing demand for a new European security order, one in which NATO's eastward expansion is reversed or constrained, and it is a demand that European governments have consistently rejected.

For Ukraine, the talks in Moscow are viewed with scepticism. Ukrainians did not believe the talks would yield results, according to Al Jazeera's correspondent in Kyiv, and the fear in Kyiv is that the US, in its eagerness for a deal, might press Ukraine to accept terms that would leave it vulnerable to a future Russian attack. The emphasis on security guarantees is meant to address that fear, but Ukraine and its European allies have yet to be convinced that any guarantee offered by Russia, or by the United States, would be credible.

For the United States, the talks are part of President Trump's effort to end a war he has promised to bring to a close. The president has spoken publicly with optimism about the prospects for a deal, but the envoys' own account of the talks, that differences remain on major issues, suggests the optimism is not yet matched by progress. The fact that Witkoff has now met Putin 10 times, and that the talks continue, suggests the administration believes the channel is worth maintaining, even without a breakthrough.

What happens next depends on the meeting in Dublin, where the envoys will brief Zelensky on what Putin said, and on whether the prisoner exchange agreed in Abu Dhabi goes ahead. The war is now nearly five years old, and it is Europe's worst conflict since the Second World War. The talks in Moscow and Abu Dhabi have not ended it, but they have kept a diplomatic process alive that, only a few months ago, appeared to have stalled completely.
