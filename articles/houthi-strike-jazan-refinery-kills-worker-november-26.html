Houthi Strike on Saudi Arabia's Jazan Refinery Kills One Worker as the Gulf Energy War Escalates

world · 26 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Jazan refinery, on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast near the Yemeni border, can process up to 400,000 barrels of crude a day. The strike came a day after the Houthis claimed an attack on Ras Tanura.

A projectile struck Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery on Thursday, killing a Nepalese worker and wounding several others, Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry said in a statement, in the latest attack on the kingdom's energy infrastructure as the war in the Gulf widens.

The strike sparked a fire at the refinery in Saudi Arabia's southwestern Jazan region and temporarily suspended some of its operations, the ministry said. The fire was brought under control, and emergency teams and technical crews were sent to assess the damage and verify the safety of the facilities. The injured workers were of various nationalities and were receiving medical care, the ministry said. Aramco, the state-owned oil company, did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The ministry's statement did not identify who carried out the strike. But the Saudi-led military coalition fighting Yemen's Houthis said earlier on Thursday that the Iranian-backed group had attacked the refinery. Later on Thursday, the coalition claimed its forces had destroyed a Houthi ballistic missile launch platform in Yemen's Hodeidah governorate, which it said the group had used to stage the attack on the refinery. That claim, like the attribution itself, comes from the coalition and has not been independently verified.

The Jazan refinery sits on the kingdom's Red Sea coast, near the Yemeni border, and is designed to process up to 400,000 barrels of crude oil a day. It is not the biggest target the Houthis have gone after this week — but it is the one where someone died, and that changes the character of the campaign.

The strike came a day after the Houthis claimed an attack on Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia's largest refinery and a major crude export hub. Satellite imagery taken on Wednesday showed a huge plume of black smoke rising from the coastal peninsula after that attack, and the Houthi-controlled armed forces said the strike had caused a fire at the facility, which is part of one of the world's largest crude oil export terminals. A shipping source told CNN on Thursday that oil loadings at the terminal were suspended after the attack, though it was unclear whether they had resumed.

The Saudi-led coalition said it had intercepted and destroyed four drones over Ras Tanura, with falling debris but no reported injuries. Ras Tanura has a refining capacity of 550,000 barrels a day and is part of a broader complex that housed Saudi Arabia's main crude export terminal before the US-Iran war began. The two refinery strikes in two days — Ras Tanura on Wednesday, Jazan on Thursday — are the sharpest escalation yet in the Houthis' campaign against Saudi energy infrastructure.

The Houthis also claimed a strike on Wednesday on Riyadh's King Abdullah Economic City, which Saudi authorities said had wounded 11 people. The coalition said in a later statement on Wednesday that the strike was carried out by a Houthi drone and vowed a “firm response”. The attacks on the capital are not new: Riyadh's international airport was hit several times last month, killing more than a dozen people, and the financial district has been targeted repeatedly in recent weeks.

Saudi Arabia's response has been to hit back hard across Yemen. On Thursday, the coalition said it had carried out a “precision military operation” against Houthi capabilities, destroying 50 targets it described as legitimate military targets across the Sanaa, Hodeidah and Saada governorates. A day earlier, it said it had attacked “operational capabilities” at the Houthi-controlled Hodeidah port and destroyed targets in Saada and Amran governorates.

The Houthis, for their part, said Saudi warplanes and missiles had carried out 109 strikes over the previous 24 hours across Yemen, including in Sanaa and Hodeidah. The Houthi-controlled health ministry in Sanaa said on Thursday that at least 19 civilians had been killed and 25 others wounded in Saudi strikes over the previous 48 hours across several parts of Yemen. CNN reported that it had not been able to independently verify the claims about the strikes or the casualties, and the figures should be read as the Houthi side's account, not as confirmed fact.

The fighting between the Houthis and the Saudi-led coalition reignited in July, after years of relative calm brought about by a 2022 UN-brokered ceasefire. Clashes intensified in recent months after the Houthis made an advance toward the critical Bab el-Mandeb Strait in September, threatening a new front in the wider Iran war. The strait, at the southern entrance to the Red Sea, is one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints, and a Houthi presence there puts the entire Red Sea trade route at risk.

The Jazan strike does not stand alone. It is the latest in a chain of attacks that has spread across the Gulf in the space of a fortnight. Iran carried out drone attacks on US bases in Bahrain on 15, 17 and 18 November, and missile and drone strikes on US bases in Bahrain again on 21 November. On 23 November, Tehran vowed to confront the US naval blockade. Two days later, on 25 November, the Houthis claimed attacks on Ras Tanura and the Riyadh financial district, and two oil tankers hit mines and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz. On Thursday, the same day as the Jazan strike, Iranian drones killed two Italian technicians at an air-defence surveillance station in Kuwait.

The pattern is now unmistakable: a war that began with American strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz has become a regional campaign fought with drones, mines and missiles against refineries, ports, air bases and air-defence stations across Bahrain, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and the Strait of Hormuz itself. The weapons are cheap and the targets are spread across thousands of kilometres of coastline, which is exactly what makes the campaign so hard to stop — and why each new strike, however small, carries outsized consequences.

The oil market is already pricing in that risk. Brent crude futures hovered near this month's high on Friday, at $96.19 a barrel, after gaining nearly $2 on Thursday to settle at their highest since 6 November. US West Texas Intermediate crude was at $85.41 a barrel. Analysts at ANZ, Brian Martin and Daniel Hynes, wrote that Brent could still push above $100 a barrel without another major escalation in the Middle East, but that doing so increasingly required evidence of constrained deliverable supply — evidence the attacks on Ras Tanura and Jazan are now supplying.

The disruption is not confined to the Gulf. Ukraine has intensified its drone attacks on Russian oil refineries since the end of October, with the latest incident causing a fire at the Slavyansk ECO refinery in Russia's southern Krasnodar region, tightening global diesel supplies. Fires also hit Thailand's Bangchak refinery and Venezuela's Amuay refinery on Thursday, and an incident at the Bacalhau oilfield offshore Brazil has halted production for an estimated five to eight weeks. Taken together, the incidents describe a global energy system under strain from several directions at once.

OPEC+, the group of oil-producing countries that sets global output policy, is likely to maintain its oil output targets for at least the first quarter of 2027 at a meeting on Sunday, according to two people with knowledge of the talks. Holding output steady while supply is being disrupted by war is a choice with consequences: it leaves the market tighter than it would otherwise be, and it keeps prices high at a moment when the world's biggest economies are already worried about inflation.

The diplomacy around the war is moving, too. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is planning his first trip to the United States since the Iran war began, where he is expected to attend next month's G20 summit in Miami, Florida, according to a source familiar with the plans. Saudi officials have not yet publicly confirmed his attendance. Saudi Arabia has sought US support in strengthening its defences as it faces continued Houthi missile and drone attacks, and the planned visit would set up the first in-person meeting between the crown prince and US President Donald Trump since the outbreak of the war.

Why it matters: the Jazan strike is the moment the Houthis' campaign against Saudi Arabia stopped being a story about drones intercepted and fires extinguished and became a story about a dead worker — a Nepalese man who went to work at a refinery and did not come home. It came a day after the claimed strike on Ras Tanura, the kingdom's biggest refinery, and on the same day Iranian drones killed two Italians in Kuwait. The war is no longer being fought on one front, or with one weapon, or against one kind of target. It is being fought across the whole Gulf, against the energy infrastructure the world depends on, and the price of oil — now within reach of $100 a barrel — is telling the rest of the world that this war is no longer someone else's problem.
