Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins Wins Alaska Governor Race, First Democrat Elected Since 1998

politics · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, 37, a former Alaska state lawmaker, won 50.8 percent of first-choice votes to become the first Democrat elected governor of Alaska since 1998.

Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins has been elected governor of Alaska, according to projections by the Associated Press and NBC News reported by Reuters on Saturday, becoming the first Democrat to win the state’s highest office since 1998.

Kreiss-Tomkins, 37, a former Alaska state lawmaker from Sitka, won 50.8 percent of first-choice votes — 139,293 ballots — defeating three Republicans on the general-election ballot. Anchorage businesswoman Bernadette Wilson, who carried President Donald Trump’s endorsement, finished second with 28.1 percent of the vote, or 77,110 ballots. Former Anchorage Mayor Dave Bronson took 13.6 percent and former state Attorney General Treg Taylor took 7.2 percent. The Associated Press called the race on Friday evening local time, more than ten days after voters went to the polls on 3 November, and the results remain unofficial until they are certified by the Alaska Division of Elections.

Kreiss-Tomkins succeeds Republican Governor Mike Dunleavy, who was barred by term limits from seeking re-election after two consecutive terms in office. At 37, he is set to become the youngest governor in Alaska’s history, and he is scheduled to take office on 7 December.

He campaigned on reducing the cost of living, fully funding public schools and regulating data centre construction, Reuters reported — a platform built around household costs and public services rather than the national culture-war issues that have dominated much of American politics in recent years. That focus, and the coalition he assembled behind it, is now being read as one of the main explanations for a result few had predicted in a state that has voted Republican for president in every election for decades.

The most striking feature of the campaign was the support he drew from across the aisle. Republican US Senator Lisa Murkowski endorsed Kreiss-Tomkins a week before Election Day, one of the few members of her party who has been willing to break with Trump on some foreign and domestic issues, Reuters noted. Murkowski said he understood that governing requires listening to people with different views and finding common ground — an endorsement that carried particular weight in a state where she has built her own career on independence from her party’s national leadership.

Alaska’s election was fought under the voting system the state adopted in recent years: a top-four, all-party primary followed by a ranked-choice general election, in which voters rank candidates in order of preference and the lowest-placed candidates are eliminated, with their ballots redistributed, until one candidate holds a majority. In this case the ranked-choice rounds were never needed. Because Kreiss-Tomkins cleared 50 percent of first-choice votes outright, he won a majority on the first ballot and no runoff tabulation was required to decide the race.

The result ends a long drought for Alaska Democrats. The last Democrat elected governor of the state was Tony Knowles, who won in 1998 and left office in 2002. Since then the governor’s office has been held by Republicans and, for one term, by an independent, while the state has reliably backed Republican presidential candidates. Kreiss-Tomkins’s victory is the first time in 28 years that Alaska voters have chosen a Democrat to lead the state.

It is also the latest win in a broader wave for the party in the 3 November midterm elections. Reuters described the Alaska result as part of a string of midterm state-government and congressional victories for Democrats, who also took control of the US Senate and the US House of Representatives in the same elections. In Georgia, Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms won the governor’s race. Kreiss-Tomkins’s victory will give Democrats 30 state governorships next year, out of 50 — a milestone that reshapes the map of who runs America’s states.

The numbers tell the story of how he did it. The three Republicans on the ballot split the conservative vote between them: Wilson’s 28.1 percent, Bronson’s 13.6 percent and Taylor’s 7.2 percent add up to 48.9 percent combined — less than Kreiss-Tomkins’s 50.8 percent on his own. A united Republican field might have made the race far closer, but the split field, the Trump-endorsed candidate’s second-place finish, and a Democratic campaign that picked up Republican and independent votes in roughly equal measure combined to produce a clear first-round majority rather than a narrow ranked-choice win.

What happens next is a transition that now has a firm date. The results will be certified by the Alaska Division of Elections, and Kreiss-Tomkins is scheduled to be sworn in on 7 December. His first test will be turning the platform he campaigned on into a governing programme: measures to ease the cost of living, a budget that fully funds public schools, and rules for the data centre construction he promised to regulate. He will have to do it while working with a state legislature and a political establishment that, until now, has been dominated by the party he defeated.

Why it matters: the Alaska result matters beyond the state’s borders because it shows the limits of national politics in a local race. A candidate endorsed by the president lost, a Republican senator crossed party lines to back a Democrat, and voters in a deeply conservative state chose a 37-year-old former state lawmaker who talked mainly about the cost of living and schools. For Democrats, it is a 30th governorship and a symbolic breakthrough in a state they had not won in nearly three decades. For Republicans, it is a warning that a split field and a divisive endorsement can cost even a seat that has seemed safe for a generation. And for Alaska, it is the start of a new administration under its youngest governor ever — one whose first months will show whether the coalition that elected him can also govern.
