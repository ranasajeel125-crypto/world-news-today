Houthi Attacks Ground Almost Nine in Ten Flights at Riyadh Airport, Data Shows

world · 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

Attacks by Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis have nearly emptied the skies over Riyadh this month, aviation data showed on Monday, 26 October, with cancellations grounding almost nine out of every 10 scheduled flights at the Saudi capital's airport. About 86% of scheduled flights at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport, or 4,071 out of 4,736, were cancelled between October 8 and October 25, according to the latest data from aviation analytics firm Cirium. Cirium counts a flight as disrupted when it is listed as cancelled or as "no-fly" against the schedule airlines filed, and its schedule had about 263 Riyadh departures a day over the period.

The Houthis have stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia this month, targeting airports, ports and oil facilities with ballistic missiles and drones. Riyadh's airport, the kingdom's second-busiest after Jeddah, has been struck repeatedly, including an attack on October 10 that killed 13 people, the deadliest in the kingdom in more than a decade. A cruise missile struck a complex containing Terminals 3 and 4, killing 13 people and injuring more than 300, and the airport was then closed for two weeks. Attacks on 6-7 October on Riyadh's airport and Abha airport had already killed three people and wounded 36, and two attacks on 8 October killed three more, including Saudia captain Hamoud Ali Al Kalthami. The Financial Times and the Wall Street Journal reported that the Houthis struck King Khalid International Airport five times in six days, and at least 20 people have been killed at Saudi airports since 6 October.

Saudi authorities partially reopened the airport on Sunday, 25 October, after a halt that had started on October 10. Domestic flights resumed from Terminal 1, while national carriers were allowed to operate international services from Terminal 2. Domestic flights had already begun again in the past week, but aviation tracker Flightradar24 indicated that most flights to Riyadh remained cancelled even after the reopening, and disruption eased on 24 October before rising again on 25 October, when the kingdom-wide cancellation rate rose to 44.23 per cent, from 16.51 per cent the previous day. Explosions were heard across Riyadh early on Sunday after alerts warned of a potential aerial threat, and the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile and two drones targeting the capital, followed by a cruise missile aimed at Khamis Mushait.

However, the Houthis said they had attacked Riyadh's airport again on Monday, calling it a "military target." Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed on X that the group fired a ballistic missile at King Khalid International and attacked pumping stations on the East-West pipeline with drones. Saudi military spokesman Turki Al-Malki said a ballistic missile bound for Riyadh was destroyed and that Saudi forces then struck missile launchers and storage sites in Yemen. The Houthis have described the airport as a military target and warned airlines against using it, and they have threatened further attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities, warning civilians, workers and international shipping companies to stay away from sites they consider legitimate targets.

The attacks have prompted international airlines to suspend services to the Saudi capital into November, while Delta Air Lines has postponed the launch of its Riyadh route until next year. Airline suspensions include Qatar Airways, which suspended Riyadh and Dammam and reduced Jeddah frequencies, Etihad into November, Air India and Air India Express until 7 November, Emirates and flydubai through 29 October, Oman Air and SalamAir until 31 October, and Lufthansa's Frankfurt-Riyadh route through 18 November. Those end dates, ranging from 29 October to 18 November, give the first concrete markers for when scheduled service might resume.

The disruption has forced several events in Riyadh to be cancelled or postponed, including Saudi Arabia's marquee investment conference, as flight cancellations hamper travel to and across the kingdom. Saudi Arabia postponed the Future Investment Initiative, its flagship annual gathering of global finance and business leaders, nicknamed "Davos in the desert", to 2027 on 19 October, one week before the event was due to open on 26 October. The FII Institute cited a desire to ensure the 10th edition "delivers the full scale, extraordinary quality" its partners expect, and its statement made no mention of the Houthi missile and drone campaign, but five Wall Street chief executives who had been expected to speak, including Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan Chase, David Solomon of Goldman Sachs and Stephen Schwarzman of Blackstone, had already pulled out. At the World Petroleum Congress that opened on 11 October in Riyadh, the chief executives of four Western oil majors, ExxonMobil, Shell, TotalEnergies and BP, one after another notified organisers they would not attend. The Misk Global Forum cancelled next month's gathering altogether, organisers of the Dakar Rally said it would move to a different location, and the United Nations World Data Forum, the Second Saudi Statistical Forum, the Six Kings Slam tennis tournament and the Saudi World Rally Championship were also affected.

The upheaval also spread beyond the Saudi capital. More than 7,800 flights, or 45% of all scheduled departures from Saudi airports, were cancelled between October 8 and October 25, according to Cirium. Of 17,299 scheduled departures across the kingdom during the period, 7,802 were cancelled or recorded as no-fly, representing 45.1 per cent, and Riyadh alone accounts for just over half of them. The peak came on October 12, when cancellations exceeded 60% of scheduled flights nationwide. Several major airports recorded severe disruption, including Riyadh at 86%, Dammam at 42% and Abha at 46.7%, where the Houthis have claimed strikes. A number of smaller regional airports saw the vast majority of flights cancelled, including Najran, near the Yemeni border, where all flights were cancelled.

The Houthis have also attacked Saudi energy facilities, including the Ras Tanura refinery, the Rabigh refinery and a production facility at Abqaiq owned by state oil giant Saudi Aramco. The United States ordered non-emergency staff to leave, oil prices surged to six-month highs before easing, and Qatari mediators travelled to Tehran to try to calm tensions. The campaign is part of the wider war in the Middle East that began in February, in which the Houthis, supplied with arms, intelligence and training by Iran, have sought to draw Saudi Arabia into a costly conflict and to undermine the kingdom's ambitions to become a global tourism and business hub under its Vision 2030 diversification drive. For now, Riyadh's skies remain on edge until the attacks stop, and airlines, businesses and travellers are watching the suspension end dates for the first sign that normal service might return.
