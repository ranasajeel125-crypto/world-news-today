African Insurgents Take the War to the Skies With Off-the-Shelf Drones

world · 25 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The videos are not from Ukraine: they were filmed in Mali, where separatists and al Qaeda's affiliate are flying kamikaze drones against Russian paramilitaries and government troops.

The kamikaze quadcopter banks sharply, locks on to a Russian military vehicle and dives for the kill. Another drone hovers over an airfield. A strike narrowly misses a jet but hits three men identified as Russians. Posts of first-person-view drones targeting Russians have become commonplace as the war in Ukraine drags on — but these videos are not from Ukraine. They are from Mali, 5,000 kilometres away on the edge of the Sahara Desert, and they show how battlecraft honed in Ukraine's kill zone has become a major factor in the resurgence of rebels across the Sahel.

Filmed by separatists and an al Qaeda affiliate during their simultaneous joint assault on Mali's army and its Russian paramilitary backers in April, the videos capture a shift that is now reshaping warfare far beyond Europe. Armed with drones costing a few hundred dollars, insurgents in Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and beyond have robbed government forces of the air superiority they once commanded with fighter jets and attack helicopters. Now their multimillion-dollar military hardware is constantly threatened from above, as is critical infrastructure including gold mines, fuel depots, pipelines and ports — and even distant capital cities.

For decades, the sky belonged to the state. “In the past, the state always had the monopoly of the sky. It was the one thing they could control,” said Niccola Milnes, an independent senior counterterrorism and security adviser. “Now that non-state actors can access these drones cheaply and modify them and attach payloads, it's obviously completely upended that.” That is the heart of the story Reuters documents: a technology that once required an air force can now be bought off the shelf, smuggled into the desert and refitted for war.

The military calculus started to change in 2023, when first-person-view drones armed with basic explosives and flown by remote pilots proliferated in Ukraine. The cheap, accessible technology and the new expertise that came with it soon made its way to West Africa, helped in part by Ukrainians keen to hurt Russian forces wherever possible, according to the separatists in Mali and experts on the conflict. Russia began backing Mali's military government against jihadists and the Tuareg-led separatists in 2021 with the deployment of the Wagner mercenary group, and in 2023 the Russian Defence Ministry created Africa Corps, a paramilitary force that replaced Wagner and absorbed many of its fighters.

The connection is direct, according to the separatists themselves. In 2023, the separatists were trained to use drones by Ukrainians during a 30-day course at a location outside Mali, said Mohamed Elmaouloud Ramadane, a spokesman for the group, now known as the Azawad Liberation Front (FLA). The FLA denied that Ukrainians had been directly involved in its operations, and Ramadane declined to provide details of the types of drones the group used. Reuters was unable to determine who from Ukraine had helped the FLA, and neither Ukraine's military nor its intelligence services responded to requests for comment. In a statement on 2 November, Ukraine's Ministry of Foreign Affairs firmly rejected accusations by Mali and Niger that Kyiv “supports illegal armed groups and interferes in the internal affairs of the Sahel states”. Russia's Ministry of Defence did not respond to requests for comment.

The training was not the only way Kyiv has taken the fight to Moscow far from its own frontline. Ukraine has carried out strikes on Russian assets far from the front, including the sinking of a Russian naval corvette in a Libyan port on Sunday using maritime drones. Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said on Tuesday that the attack was justified because “the Russian Federation systematically uses its military presence and logistical infrastructure outside its own territory, including in Africa, to support military operations and build up capabilities that sustain its aggressive war against Ukraine.” In other words, the shadow war between Kyiv and Moscow now stretches from the Black Sea to the Sahara and the Mediterranean coast.

In Mali, the results of the training arrived quickly. In July 2024, some ten months after they received it, the separatists' dedicated “Unit 6” drone team first unleashed one-way attack, or kamikaze, drones against Russian and Malian forces during the battle for the town of Tinzaouaten on the Algerian border, Ramadane said. By then, al Qaeda's affiliate JNIM — Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin — had already deployed attack drones in Mali. Its debut was humble: in September 2023, a JNIM-operated quadcopter dropped two improvised explosive devices on a rival militia in the central Mopti region, but neither exploded or hurt anyone, according to the conflict monitoring group Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED).

Since then, the frequency and the sophistication of the drone attacks have increased, with JNIM the most prolific user, according to previously unpublished ACLED data. JNIM has carried out 187 drone attacks since 2023 through mid-November. Including the FLA and its predecessors, the number rises to 235 across four West African countries. JNIM has preferred dropping explosives from its drones, but analysts say it ramped up kamikaze strikes in 2026. The region's Islamic State militants are using drones too, for reconnaissance, tactical coordination and crude attacks: Islamic State West Africa Province has used attack drones 15 times since 2024, mostly in clashes with Nigeria's military, according to ACLED, and the group dispatched 13 trainers from the Middle East to share drone skills with Islamic State groups in the region, United Nations experts reported last year. Reuters was unable to determine whether JNIM has benefited from any of the training provided to the FLA, or where the groups procured their drones; experts said they bought commercially available models that were then smuggled into the desert and refitted for war.

The economics of the new warfare explain why it is spreading so fast. “The economics favour the attacker: a munition costing a few hundred dollars can threaten aircraft, fuel depots and bases that are worth millions,” said Justyna Gudzowska, executive director of The Sentry, an investigative and policy group. JNIM, an alliance of four jihadist groups formed in 2017, staged 81 attacks in 2025 and 96 through mid-November this year, according to ACLED, and it has increasingly paired drone attacks with ground assaults on bases in several countries. In February 2025, JNIM used first-person-view drones armed with explosives to attack military positions in the northern Burkina Faso town of Djibo; two months later it staged its only known drone attack in Togo, a kamikaze strike that killed five soldiers.

The April attacks showed how drones have become part of coordinated offensives rather than isolated stunts. After JNIM's ground attacks that month killed Mali's defence minister outside Bamako and hit the capital's main airport, the group released footage showing how it also used drones to target Russian and Malian soldiers at the Sevare-Mopti airport in the centre of the country. The drone footage, set to music proclaiming the glory of jihad, shows Russian paramilitaries — identified by JNIM with superimposed flags — being hit by an explosion on the airport tarmac. Eight more Russians are targeted on a rooftop in the airport grounds, as are seven Malians in and around another building nearby, according to the videos posted by JNIM and verified by Reuters.

Drones also change the risk calculation for the attackers. Heni Nsaibia, a senior West Africa analyst at ACLED, said drones allowed militants to attack while reducing their exposure to direct combat and retaliatory airstrikes. “Drones also allow them to monitor military movements, conduct reconnaissance, and gather information during large-scale coordinated offensives and attacks on military bases and camps,” he said. JNIM has not shown significant technological advances in its drone attacks, mainly using them to drop explosives, including 60mm mortar bombs — a limitation that likely reflects a scarcity of advanced equipment and skilled operators, according to a study published in July by the Policy Center for the New South. “Unlike kamikaze strikes, which by default destroy the drone on impact, a drone dropping IEDs can return and be used again,” the study's authors Milnes and Rida Lyammouri wrote. “This allows JNIM to maintain the region's highest tempo while preserving its inventory.”

The FLA, by contrast, has become one of the most advanced non-state drone operators in the world, according to Milnes and Lyammouri. It achieved a new milestone in March when it used roughly 25 drones in two waves to attack an air defence system at the airport in Gao, the biggest city in northern Mali — the first “drone swarm” attack by a non-state group in the Sahel. Like JNIM, the FLA released videos, also verified by Reuters, of multiple first-person-view strikes, many targeting fighters it identified as Russians during the April attacks, including in Kidal. So far, the region's militaries have not demonstrated decent jamming capabilities, Milnes said: out of 245 strikes documented in the Policy Center for the New South study, there were only 10 instances of interception, jamming or surface-to-air missile fire deployed by governments. The FLA also adopted fibre-optic first-person-view drones last year, a technology used widely in Ukraine to bypass electronic jamming.

Governments are fighting back, but the contest is tilting the wrong way for them. Russia's Africa Corps is trying to regain momentum with ZALA Lancet suicide drones, according to a report by the research body Conflict Insights Group this month, and videos posted in October show Shahed-style drones — used extensively by Russia in Ukraine, as well as during the Iran war — hitting FLA heartlands near Algeria. Governments have been snapping up drones too, most relying on larger, long-range models made by Turkey's Bayraktar or Chinese-made Wing Loong or CH-4 drones; Bayraktar's TB2 and Akinci drones can also carry heavy guided weapons. Burkina Faso recently displayed a sophisticated new air defence system it had acquired from China, though analysts said such technologies were not best suited for fighting insurgents. Nate Allen, associate professor of security studies at the Africa Center for Strategic Studies, said larger drones such as the TB2 played important roles but future wars were likely to be dominated by cheaper systems. “So far, it is non-state armed actors, and not Africa's militaries, that are most effectively taking advantage of this shift,” he said.

Why it matters: the Sahel shows what happens when a battlefield innovation escapes the battlefield. The drone tactics forged in Ukraine now belong to anyone who can buy a quadcopter and watch a tutorial, and the states facing them — Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Nigeria among them — have so far shown little ability to jam, intercept or shoot them down. The same cheap-drone logic is already visible in Ethiopia, where rebels reportedly used drones in an attack on the capital Addis Ababa last month, and Reuters describes the pattern as stretching from South America to the Middle East and Asia. For governments, the bill is no longer just the loss of a jet or a base: gold mines, fuel depots, pipelines and ports are all in range, and a few hundred dollars of explosives can now threaten assets worth millions. Until states learn to defend the low sky as well as the high one, the advantage will stay with the attacker.
