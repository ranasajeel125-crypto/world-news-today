ECB President Lagarde Resigns, Will Step Down on 7 February 2027

European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde has resigned and will step down on 7 February 2027, the ECB said in a statement on Thursday, 29 October. "European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde today informed the Governing Council and the President of the European Council António Costa that she will step down from her position on 7 February 2027," the ECB said.

Lagarde's term was scheduled to run until the end of October 2027, so her departure comes almost nine months early, after months of speculation about an early exit. The ECB gave no reason for the timing of her decision and named no successor, saying only that the process for appointing the next president would be initiated without delay. Her successor will be appointed by the European Council, the body of the European Union's national leaders, after consultation with the European Parliament and the ECB's Governing Council.

The announcement came on the same day the ECB was due to announce its interest rate decision at 1315 GMT on Thursday, followed by Lagarde's regular press conference at 1345 GMT. At that meeting the ECB kept its three key interest rates unchanged, leaving the deposit rate at 2.50 per cent after two increases earlier in the year. The deposit rate, the rate through which the Governing Council steers its policy stance, had been raised by 25 basis points in June to 2.25 per cent and again on 10 September to 2.50 per cent, ending a year in which it had stood at 2.00 per cent. Two more policy meetings remain before Lagarde leaves on 7 February.

The rate decision came against a backdrop of renewed inflation pressure. Euro-area inflation rose to 3.8 per cent in September from 3.2 per cent in August, driven by energy prices, which rose by 18.9 per cent. The ECB's staff projections published with the September decision put headline inflation at 3.0 per cent for 2026, falling to 2.5 per cent in 2027 and 2.1 per cent in 2028, with growth of 0.9 per cent this year, 1.4 per cent next year and 1.5 per cent in 2028. The Governing Council said financing conditions had tightened since September, which it expected to weigh on both inflation and growth, and it committed to no future path for rates.

The former central bank governors of the Netherlands and Spain, Klaas Knot and Pablo Hernández de Cos, are seen as frontrunners to replace her, Reuters reported. Knot led the Dutch central bank until 2025 and built a reputation as a conservative voice during the eurozone debt crisis, when he pushed for strict conditions on ECB help for countries such as Greece and expressed doubts about quantitative easing. Hernández de Cos, a former governor of the Bank of Spain, now heads the Bank for International Settlements, the Basel-based institution often described as the central bank for central banks, and is viewed as more dovish, and likely to be more lenient toward indebted countries. The race is expected to be settled by a package deal among European leaders, with several other senior ECB positions, including the chief economist's post, also due to become vacant in the coming months.

Lagarde, 70, took over the ECB presidency from Mario Draghi in November 2019, becoming the first woman to head the institution and the first ECB chief with no formal economics training or central banking experience. A former French finance minister, she had previously led the International Monetary Fund, where she oversaw a record 57 billion dollar lifeline for Argentina in 2018. She later said she had accepted the ECB job thinking the presidency was a five-year mandate, rather than eight.

Her seven years in Frankfurt were dominated by a succession of crises. Within months of her arrival, the Covid-19 pandemic threatened financial instability across the eurozone, and Lagarde launched what became a 1.85 trillion euro Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme to add liquidity and stabilise Europe's bond markets. Her prompt action helped prevent fragmentation in sovereign bond markets as government bond yields in southern European countries rose relative to those in Germany. In July 2021 she led the ECB's first monetary policy strategy review in almost two decades, replacing the bank's old inflation aim of "close to but below" 2 per cent with a symmetric 2 per cent target, under which deviations above and below the target are treated equally.

The greatest test came after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, when energy prices sent eurozone inflation to a record 10.6 per cent in October 2022. The ECB was slow to respond at first, repeatedly describing the post-Covid price rises as transitory, but it then embarked on the fastest tightening cycle in its history, raising policy rates by 450 basis points between July 2022 and September 2023. The deposit rate rose from minus 0.5 per cent to 4 per cent, the highest level since the euro's creation, and the bank also launched the Transmission Protection Instrument, designed to prevent unwarranted divergences in borrowing costs among member states. Inflation fell back to 2.4 per cent by the end of 2024, without a recession, and the ECB began cutting rates in June 2024, bringing the deposit rate down to 2 per cent by June 2025. Lagarde also advanced work on a digital euro, a project to give the currency a central-bank digital form.

Her early exit is also political. By stepping down before her term ends, Lagarde gives European Union leaders a chance to choose her successor before a French presidential election next spring, in which opinion polls suggest far-right candidate Marine Le Pen could win and replace President Emmanuel Macron, a development that could complicate the appointment process. The departure comes as the eurozone faces renewed inflationary pressures, mounting fiscal strains and growing political uncertainty, and whoever succeeds her will face fresh challenges, including strains in French bond markets and a conflict in the Middle East that threatens a new bout of inflation. Her legacy, analysts say, is that of an outsider who showed political diplomacy could steer a central bank through historic shocks, even as her early departure serves as a final reminder of those same political instincts.
