China Blocks SoftBank's $5.4 Billion Takeover of ABB's Robotics Division

business · 5 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

The original deal, announced in October 2025, would have put ABB's robotics business under Japanese ownership.

business · 5 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

China has blocked SoftBank Group's $5.4 billion takeover of ABB's industrial robotics division, according to a statement released by ABB on November 5, in a decision that has thrown one of the largest industrial deals of the year into doubt and underlined how far Beijing is willing to go to keep sensitive technology inside its borders.

The Swiss engineering group said it had been informed by Japan's SoftBank that China's Office of the Working Mechanism for Foreign Investment Security Review had prohibited the acquisition of the ABB Robotics business in China. The office is part of the machinery run by China's National Development and Reform Commission, the powerful state planner charged with safeguarding supply chain security and stability. ABB said both companies were working constructively on the transaction, and neither company has announced that the deal is terminated.

The original agreement was announced in October 2025, when SoftBank agreed to buy ABB's robotics division for an enterprise value of around $5.4 billion. ABB had expected net proceeds of about $5.3 billion from the sale. The deal was presented at the time as a landmark step in SoftBank's push into what its chief executive, Masayoshi Son, has called physical artificial intelligence: robots and machines that bring AI out of data centres and into factories, warehouses and production lines. Son has described robotics as pivotal to the group's AI investment strategy, and the ABB business, with its industrial robot arms used in car plants, electronics factories and logistics, was meant to be the centrepiece of that ambition.

The block came as a surprise. According to reports in the Financial Times, SoftBank had expected the transaction to be approved until the previous week, when it learned that the National Development and Reform Commission had rejected it. The prohibition was first reported by the Financial Times on November 5, and ABB confirmed it the same day. SoftBank, which announced the deal in October 2025, declined to comment publicly on the decision, and is now trying to determine what went wrong and whether it could seek approval again.

At the heart of the decision are concerns about data and technology. Reporting on the case said Chinese regulators were worried that information generated by ABB's robotics operations in China could be transferred outside the country and potentially used to develop advanced AI systems. SoftBank's ties with Western companies, including OpenAI, in which the Japanese firm is a major investor, were also cited as a concern, along with the risk that sensitive data could be transferred to the United States. The authorities also examined whether intellectual property must remain in China. ABB's robotics unit is heavily focused on the Chinese market, and it operates a large factory in Pudong, Shanghai, serving customers across the country. China accounts for about 30 percent of the robotics unit's business, which is one reason a block on the Chinese part of the deal effectively blocks the whole transaction.

The decision fits a wider pattern of tightening scrutiny in Beijing. It was the second major deal blocked by China this year. In April, Meta Platforms was ordered to unwind its acquisition of the AI startup Manus, a deal worth more than $2 billion. Beijing has spent the year clamping down on transfers of vital data abroad, including in robotics, and it is scrutinising foreign investment involving frontier technologies more closely as the race to dominate AI development with the United States heats up. The ABB case shows that the scrutiny now extends beyond software and chips to the machines themselves, and to the data those machines generate on factory floors.

What happens next is unresolved. ABB and SoftBank are working to restructure the deal to save it, potentially by carving out the Chinese business and selling it to a Chinese company or listing it separately, according to people familiar with the matter. The two sides are hoping to reach a solution in the coming weeks, although talks could drag into next year. If the sale cannot be salvaged, ABB is considering a listing of its robotics unit, or a spin-off, or a sale to another party. SoftBank may be liable for a break fee, although the terms of that fee have not been disclosed. The preference of both companies, according to people close to the discussions, would be to rescue the deal rather than abandon it.

The stakes are considerable for both sides. For SoftBank, the failure of the ABB purchase would be a setback to its ambitions in robotics, which Son has highlighted as central to the group's strategy for the next stage of AI. For ABB, the block cuts its expected firepower roughly in half, to about $5 billion, according to an estimate by Citigroup analyst Martin Wilkie, because the proceeds from the sale were expected to fund other parts of the group's plans. ABB declined to comment on the restructuring discussions, and SoftBank also declined to comment.

The case also matters beyond the two companies. Industrial robots sit at the intersection of manufacturing, data and artificial intelligence, and governments on all sides now treat them as strategic assets rather than ordinary factory equipment. China is the world's largest market for industrial robots, and its factories buy more robots each year than any other country. Whoever controls the data those robots produce, and the software that trains them, has a say in how the next generation of manufacturing works. Beijing's decision signals that it intends to keep that control at home, even when the buyer is a long-standing commercial partner from Japan rather than a rival from the United States.

For dealmakers, the message is equally clear. Transactions that once passed through national security reviews as a formality are now being examined in detail, and approval can no longer be assumed even late in the process. SoftBank had anticipated clearance until the final week, and the sudden reversal will make other buyers and sellers of technology assets think harder about how a deal is structured, which parts of a business sit in China, and whether a carve-out is needed from the start. The ABB case, like the Manus case before it, suggests that China's foreign investment review has become a genuine gatekeeper for deals involving AI, data and advanced manufacturing, and that gate is now closing more often.
