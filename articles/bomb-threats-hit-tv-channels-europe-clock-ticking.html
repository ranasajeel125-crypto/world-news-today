Bomb Threats Warning 'The Clock Is Ticking' Disrupt TV Channels Across Europe

world · 6 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Emails titled "MESSAGE TO THE WEST" warned a device would detonate in 45 minutes; no bombs were found at any affected site, and it remains unclear who is behind the threats.

Bomb threats warning "the clock is ticking" were sent to TV stations across Europe on Friday, prompting evacuations and programming to be suspended. Warnings were sent to Irish broadcaster RTE, Britain's Sky, Bulgarian private station Nova TV and others, the companies said. No bombs were found at any affected site. RTE programmes were replaced by music and a pre-recorded message, some for as long as three hours.

"We want the whole world to know about us, which is why one of our operatives has planted an explosive device in your building," read one email received by Nova and RTE on Friday. "It will detonate in 45 minutes. The clock is ticking." The emails, titled "MESSAGE TO THE WEST", were signed off with the name Mikhail Andropov, with a message saying the author was one of the leaders of a group responsible for this week's plane hoaxes. "But this is only the beginning. What happened this week is just a small part of what awaits Europe in the near future," the message added.

The alerts follow a string of hoax bomb threats that has disrupted passenger flights from Serbia to Finland to Spain and beyond in recent days, forcing diversions, emergency landings and security sweeps, though no explosives have been found so far. They also come amid a growing sense of unease about hacking scams in recent weeks, including cyberattacks on supermarkets. On 5 November, nearly 30 passenger flights were affected by bomb threats, following earlier threats against flights and a cruise ship, and the next day the threats reached broadcasters in Ireland and Bulgaria.

Police came to the Nova newsroom in Sofia, which was evacuated, checked and deemed safe. Broadcasting was not interrupted and employees returned to their desks, broadcasters said. Staff returned to RTE's campus after police completed a more than three-hour sweep of the buildings. RTE briefly evacuated staff, including radio and television presenters, in Dublin after an email was received on Friday morning, and RTE Radio One presenter David McCullagh later told listeners that the threat warned that a bomb would explode within 45 minutes. Irish police, An Garda Siochana, searched the RTE campus in Donnybrook, Dublin, for more than three hours, and RTE replaced its scheduled programming, including Today with David McCullagh, with music and a prerecorded message for the duration of the search.

British broadcaster Sky also said it had received a security threat. In a statement reported by Sky News, the broadcaster said it was working closely with authorities to ensure its people and operations remained secure, and Sky News remained on air. The Irish government said hoax bomb claims were also sent to Virgin Media, Newstalk radio and The Irish Independent newspaper, and that it was unclear who was behind them. Staff at the Dublin-based radio station Newstalk FM also left their offices for a short time after receiving threats by email.

It is unclear who was behind Friday's hoaxes. The purported author shared a surname with Yuri Andropov, a former General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, and the title of the email, "MESSAGE TO THE WEST", echoed the title of a biography of Andropov called "New Challenge to the West". It was unclear if this was a sign of Russian involvement, or an attempt to point the finger at Moscow. Reuters reported on 6 November that it had been unable to establish the identity or origin of the sender of threats to Irish and Bulgarian newsrooms, or whether those messages came from the same source as the threats to flights. As of 11 November, responsibility for the threats had not been publicly established, and it remained unclear whether the incidents shared a common source.

Irish Prime Minister Micheal Martin said security agencies in Europe had not yet determined whether the threats to air travel and media organisations were being made by individuals or as part of a coordinated attack. "It just illustrates how relatively simple it can be to cause disruption and to undermine the normal operation of society through unconventional activities of this kind, which are designed to cause disruption, designed to cause fear and anxiety," Martin told RTE radio shortly after news programming went back on air from an outside broadcasting unit. "We have to remain calm," he added. Martin noted that while the attacks seem to have a connection to Russia, authorities had not definitively identified who was behind the bomb threats.

The threats to media organisations came days before Dublin hosted European Union meetings, and they were part of a wider wave that continued after the broadcasters were targeted. On 11 November, approximately 6,300 schools in Italy received emailed warnings, prompting evacuations and closures, and schools in Scotland, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland also received similar messages. On 12 November, a further wave of emailed threats reached 3,778 schools across Italy, with 77 schools evacuated. The wave has affected passenger flights, media organisations and schools across Europe, in a series of threats that authorities have treated as hoaxes, but that have nevertheless caused flight diversions, security searches and evacuations.

The choice of broadcasters as targets is significant. Television and radio stations are symbols of a free press, and disrupting them sends a message that no institution is safe. The emails were designed to be noticed: a 45-minute deadline, a dramatic subject line, and a claim of responsibility for the week's plane hoaxes, all of it calculated to create fear and to force the authorities to respond. The fact that the threats were sent by email, at almost no cost to the sender, and that they forced evacuations, police searches and the suspension of programming, shows how cheaply disruption can be bought in a modern society.

For the broadcasters, the practical effect was a day of chaos. Presenters were evacuated, programmes were replaced by music, and newsrooms had to continue working from outside broadcasting units while police searched their buildings. For the public, the effect was a reminder that the institutions they rely on for information are themselves vulnerable, and that a single email can take a national broadcaster off the air. For the authorities, the challenge is to find who is behind the threats, and whether they are the work of individuals, a coordinated group, or a state, before the next wave arrives.
