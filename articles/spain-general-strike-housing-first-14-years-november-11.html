Workers Across Spain Stage First General Strike in 14 Years Over Housing Crisis

world · 11 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Average rent of €1,176 now eats up almost an entire young person’s monthly salary, and Spain faces a housing deficit of about 750,000 homes, according to the central bank.

Spanish workers went on a general strike on Wednesday, setting up picket lines despite stormy weather in a mass protest against the country's housing affordability crisis, a few weeks before a snap general election.

It is the first general strike in 14 years to be jointly backed by Spain's two largest unions, UGT and CCOO, as well as other labour organisations and tenants' associations angry about rising rents and evictions. The last joint strike took place on 14 November 2012, during the government of Mariano Rajoy.

Spain has a deficit of about 750,000 homes, which is set to grow to more than a million by 2028, according to the central bank. The average rental price of €1,176 ($1,315) is equivalent to about 98.7% of a young person's average monthly salary, according to the Youth Council of Spain.

The strike follows weeks of demonstrations and encampments provoked by the eviction and subsequent death of an 87-year-old woman, Maricarmen Abascal, in Madrid. Those protests, along with parliament's rejection last month of government decrees aimed at curbing evictions and limiting rent pressures, prompted Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez to call an election for 29 November.

More than 150 pickets, rallies and demonstrations were planned across the country, with a march in Madrid setting off from Atocha in the evening. Two storms, dubbed Charles and Bruna, led weather agency AEMET to issue alerts for 14 regions over strong winds, rough seas and heavy rains. Mr Sanchez has defended the right to strike, and on Thursday the Permanent Deputation of Congress is due to vote on two housing decrees.

The scale of the walkout was large. Four million Spanish workers took part in the general strike on Wednesday, the unions said, enduring stormy weather to set up picket lines across the eurozone's fourth-largest economy. The 24-hour walkout, which began at midnight on Wednesday, disrupted public transport and forced businesses, factories and shops to scale back operations or close entirely.

The two main unions that called the strike reported widespread participation in the early hours, with "significant work stoppages in strategic sectors", including 90 percent in the automotive and port sectors. "Total stoppages" were reported at factories including Volkswagen's plant in Navarre and SEAT's in Barcelona. In Madrid, 93 percent of metro trains were running at 8am compared with a normal Wednesday, but passenger numbers were down by half, and most shops were open. Across much of the country, protesters, some wearing yellow vests, blocked several roads in the early hours and set up picket lines around logistics centres.

The strike was unusual in that it did not target a specific reform or employer, but instead denounced deteriorating living conditions and the authorities' inability to guarantee access to housing. "The housing model in this country must change," said Valeria Racu, spokeswoman for the Tenants' Union, one of the organisations at the heart of the mobilisation. "Homes are for living in, not for speculation." The CCOO union is calling for at least 1.5 million price-controlled homes to be built in the coming years.

The trigger was a single eviction that became a national symbol. On September 23, Maricarmen Abascal, 87, was evicted from the Madrid apartment where she had lived for 70 years. She died several days later. The Tenants' Union then called for a general strike, a call quickly taken up by the major labour unions. Weeks of demonstrations and encampments followed, and the movement grew into a wider protest over housing and the loss of purchasing power — two of the Spanish people's main concerns.

The numbers behind the anger are stark. The average rent in Spain has almost doubled over the past 10 years, eating into household budgets. According to the National Statistics Institute, rents have increased by 22.9 percent over the past decade. Spain's average gross monthly salary is about 2,460 euros, while renting a 50-square-metre apartment in Madrid cost an average of 1,170 euros a month in October, according to property portal Idealista. The average rental price of 1,176 euros is equivalent to about 98.7 percent of a young person's average monthly salary, according to the Youth Council of Spain.

The political backdrop is equally charged. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez called a snap election for November 29 after his minority government failed to secure parliamentary approval for a package of housing measures proposed in response to the protests. Having failed to get the measures through Parliament, Sanchez decided in early October to bring forward elections originally scheduled for 2027. Sanchez hopes to harness voter resentment over housing in the November 29 election to shift the political narrative away from corruption cases involving his inner circle and a deadly train crash last month.

The Socialist government backed the movement, with no ministers scheduling any official events on Wednesday, and Sanchez has defended the right to strike. The strike was sharply criticised by the People's Party, the favourite in the polls for the forthcoming election, and by the far right, whose support could be needed to form a government if the PP fails to win an absolute majority on its own. Millions of workers were striking less than three weeks before a national election that could bring the far right to power.

The paradox of the protest is that it took place against a backdrop of solid economic results. According to International Monetary Fund forecasts, Spain is expected to post 2.5 percent growth in 2026, among the highest in advanced economies. Since Sanchez took office in 2018, the unemployment rate has fallen from 14.55 percent to 10.18 percent, according to Spain's National Institute of Statistics. Yet access to housing stands out as one of the Socialist government's main failures, and the strike revealed the distress of a segment of the population struggling to make ends meet despite those figures.

The day after the strike, the Permanent Deputation of Congress was due to vote on two housing decrees, keeping the issue at the centre of the election campaign. With more than 150 pickets, rallies and demonstrations held across the country and a march in Madrid setting off from Atocha in the evening, the first general strike in 14 years had put housing — and who can afford a home in Spain — at the top of the political agenda.
