Australia Apologises to LGBTQ Veterans for Decades of Discrimination in the Military

world · 25 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The apology was delivered in the House of Representatives on Wednesday, 34 years after the ban on gay, lesbian and bisexual service was lifted. Transgender Australians remained barred from serving until 2010.

The Australian government issued a formal apology on Wednesday to current and former LGBTQ service members for harm caused by decades of discriminatory policies in the armed forces, with Defence Minister Richard Marles telling parliament that the country had failed the very people who had served it. “You and your families offered this nation everything that service asks. In return our nation offered condemnation, derision, and suspicion,” Marles said in a speech to the House of Representatives. “This country did not keep faith with you. But today, it says sorry.”

The apology, delivered by the Deputy Prime Minister and Defence Minister, marked 34 years since the Australian government lifted the ban on lesbian, gay and bisexual Australians serving in the Australian Defence Force. It was, in Marles’s words, an attempt to confront a history the institution had long avoided. “Members of the Australian Defence Force were subjected to appalling treatment based on their sexual orientation,” he told parliament. “It is a shameful chapter in the history of the Australian Defence Force.”

What that chapter contained was spelled out in the government’s own account of the policy. Between 1974 and 1992, a single short paragraph in Defence policy ended careers, shattered futures and forced Australians from military service because of their sexuality. Those words, the Department of Defence said, enabled surveillance, interrogation and the discharge of gay, lesbian and bisexual personnel whose only offence was who they were. Marles described the investigations as “witch hunts”, in which service members were tracked, interrogated and forced either to resign or to be discharged dishonourably.

Until the ban was lifted in 1992, consensual homosexual activity could be prosecuted under military law. Transgender Australians remained barred from military service for longer still, until 2010. And even after the formal ban ended, the culture did not change overnight. “It was a long time before Defence’s culture and attitudes caught up,” Marles said, acknowledging that the lifting of the ban in 1992 could not restore the careers, opportunities and experiences that had already been lost.

The roots of the policy reach back much further than the 1970s. Australia had longstanding bans against LGBT service, but it was not until 1944 — at the behest of the American military — that the Australian Army developed an explicit policy against homosexuality. From 1954, the Royal Australian Navy adopted rules from the British Royal Navy against “Unnatural Offences”, which set out harsh investigation procedures, and the orders were updated in 1969 to prohibit “abnormal sexual behaviour”. From 1974, under direction from the Defence Minister, the three services adopted a consistent tri-service policy: any case of suspected homosexuality was to be referred to the relevant service police to investigate, and those found to be “confirmed homosexuals” were given the option of requesting their own honourable administrative discharge — or else received a dishonourable discharge marked “services no longer required”.

The policy was refreshed and restated through the 1980s. The orders on homosexuality were updated routinely, most prominently in 1985, and in 1986 Defence General Instruction 15-3 explicitly stated that the military did not accept nor condone homosexual behaviour, advising that consideration be given to terminating any member who admitted to, or was proven to have engaged in, same-sex relations. The human cost of that instruction was illustrated in the early 1990s, when Chief Petty Officer Anita van Der Meer was investigated for being in a same-sex relationship and threatened with dismissal — one of the cases that came to symbolise the final years of the ban.

The apology did not come out of nowhere. It follows legislation passed by parliament to extinguish historical convictions for homosexual service offences from service records, clearing the names of people convicted under the old rules. The Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran Suicide also brought the impact of the ban to light, a contribution acknowledged in parliament by the opposition’s defence personnel spokesman, Phillip Thompson. And the apology itself, Marles said, was the product of years of advocacy by veterans who shared their experiences: “Neither that Cabinet decision 34 years ago, nor the apology on Wednesday was a gift from government.”

Marles was careful not to present the apology as a repair of what was lost. “Lifting the ban could not hand back careers ... incomes, sometimes, a home or the company of mates, or even the right to call themselves a veteran at all,” he said. The point, he argued, was honesty about what the institution had done. “We asked these Australians to defend a fair go. Then we refused them one. None of this made our nation safer.”

Defence’s own leadership framed the moment in similar terms. In a joint statement, the department’s LGBTQIA+ Champion, Rear Admiral Tish van Stralen, and its APS Champion, Kathryn McMullan, said the apology was an important acknowledgement of the Australians who served their country with courage and professionalism while facing discrimination because of their sexuality or gender identity — and a reminder of the collective responsibility to keep building the culture inside Defence.

Australia is not the first country to reckon with a military ban of this kind. In Britain, the government commissioned an independent review in 2022 of the impact of the pre-2000 ban on homosexuality in the armed forces, led by Lord Etherton, who made 49 recommendations covering restitution, recognition and just satisfaction. When the review was published in July 2023, then Prime Minister Rishi Sunak apologised on behalf of the country to LGBT veterans. The review had received 1,128 responses from people dismissed or discharged because of their sexual orientation, from those who felt compelled to resign, and from family members and colleagues who witnessed the treatment. Britain’s ban had been lifted in January 2000, and in December 2024 the government announced a financial recognition scheme, with the Ministry of Defence allocating £75 million, including a flat payment of £50,000 for veterans dismissed or administratively discharged and an impact payment of up to £20,000 per individual.

The comparison underlines both what Australia’s apology does and what it does not. The reporting of Wednesday’s apology announced no financial redress scheme, and the government’s account of the day centred on recognition and on the conviction-clearing laws already passed by parliament. Whether further measures follow — for example, restoring ranks or medals, or recognising lost service — is a question the government has not yet answered in the reporting of the apology. For the veterans in the chamber on Wednesday, the immediate significance was simpler: after decades in which their service was treated as a disciplinary problem, the state said, in parliament, that it was wrong.

Why it matters: a formal apology from a government does not undo a dishonourable discharge, a lost career or a conviction on a service record, and Marles did not pretend otherwise. What it does is put the institution on the record. For decades, Australia’s Defence Force investigated, interrogated and expelled its own people for who they were, then kept the policy in place long after the rest of the country had moved on. Wednesday’s apology, 34 years after the ban was lifted, names that history plainly — and, with the conviction-clearing laws, begins the slower work of removing its legal traces. What comes next, and whether recognition is followed by restitution, will determine whether the apology is remembered as a moment of reckoning or merely a speech.
