Democrats Win Control of US Senate in Midterm Elections, Projected to Take Chamber from Republicans

politics · 4 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Decision Desk HQ and NBC projected a Democratic Senate majority; Democrats had already been projected to win the House earlier in the evening.

Democrats have captured control of the US Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections, Decision Desk HQ and NBC projected, completing a Democratic takeover of Congress after the party had already been projected to win a majority in the House of Representatives earlier in the evening.

Democrats flipped Senate seats in South Carolina, North Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Kansas and Iowa, according to USA Today and the Associated Press. Democrats needed to pick up four seats to take control of the chamber, and as of late Tuesday they had flipped at least seven. With Tuesday's results, Democrats secured at least 54 seats, enough to take the majority, with the outcome in a handful of races still to be determined. The final tally later reached 56 seats to 44, after Democrat Mary Peltola defeated two-term Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska, completing a nine-seat Democratic pickup.

The gains were dramatic in their scale and geography. In Texas, Democrat James Talarico, a state legislator and seminary student, delivered an upset against state Attorney General Ken Paxton, who had been endorsed by President Donald Trump. Trump had won Texas by 14 percentage points in 2024, but Talarico won by more than 11 percentage points with most votes counted. He is the first Democrat to win a statewide race in Texas in 32 years, and the first Democrat to win a Texas Senate seat since 1988.

In Kansas, Adam Hamilton, the Democratic pastor of a megachurch, beat sitting Republican Senator Roger Marshall, also endorsed by Trump. Hamilton is projected to be the first Democrat to win a Kansas Senate seat since 1932, snapping a nearly 100-year winning streak for Republicans in the state's Senate races. In Iowa, Josh Turek, a state representative, won a competitive Senate seat by 4 percentage points in a state Trump had carried by 14 points in 2024, becoming the first Iowa Democrat to win a US Senate seat since 2008.

In South Carolina, Democrat Annie Andrews, a paediatrician, took a seat held by Republican Darlene Graham, the sister of longtime Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July. Graham had been appointed to fill the seat until the election and was strongly backed by Trump. Andrews won by at least 4 percentage points. In North Carolina, Democrat Roy Cooper, a former governor, beat Republican Mike Whatley by almost 20 percentage points. In Maine, Democrat Troy Jackson unseated longtime Republican Senator Susan Collins, the longest-serving Republican woman in the Senate, by almost 7 percentage points. In Ohio, Democrat Sherrod Brown defeated Republican Senator Jon Husted by almost 4 percentage points.

In the key swing state of Michigan, progressive Democrat Abdul El-Sayed became the first Muslim to be elected senator, winning a hotly contested race against Republican Mike Rogers. In New Hampshire, Democratic Representative Chris Pappas won a Senate seat by nearly 15 percentage points. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom defeated Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, becoming the state's first Black US senator since Reconstruction. In Georgia, Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election, holding a Senate seat for the Democrats.

The Senate was projected to be harder to flip than the House in Tuesday's midterm elections, given the smaller number of seats on the ballot. Thirty-five seats were up for election, including 22 held by Republicans and 13 by Democrats, one third of the chamber. In the House, where Republicans had held the majority with 219 seats to 214 Democrats and two independents, all 435 seats were up for grabs, and the Associated Press called the House for the Democrats later in the night after the party passed 218 seats.

The result marks the first time since Barack Obama's second term that an incumbent president has had neither chamber of Congress with him. Several Democrats painted the outcome as a defeat for President Trump, who himself conceded that his opponents had bettered his party's campaign. The Democrats capitalised on widespread voter discontent over the president's policies on inflation, tariffs, AI data centres and the Iran war. Forecasters had overwhelmingly favoured Democrats to retake the House in the weeks before the election, while the battle to regain the Senate was expected to be much closer. Few predicted the scale of the Democratic onslaught.

The Democrats' success was built on candidate quality as much as on the national mood. The party managed to pick high-quality candidates able to appeal to independent voters in states Republicans have dominated for decades, including Texas, Kansas and South Carolina. Moderates made both the Senate and House majorities, with the only exception among the flipped Senate seats being Troy Jackson's victory in Maine, while progressives pointed to the wins of Jackson, El-Sayed in Michigan and Peggy Flanagan in Minnesota as signs their candidates can win competitive statewide races. Even so, the Democratic Party brand remained unpopular: a voter poll showed the party viewed unfavourably by 52 percent of midterm voters.

Retaking the Senate will give Democrats legislative leverage in shaping Washington's agenda. They are likely to pursue reversing Medicaid and food stamp cuts, reasserting Congress's role on foreign trade policy, and seeking bipartisan agreement on banning lawmakers from trading stocks. The immediate showdown, however, will likely centre on the war in Iran, and could see Democrats directing President Trump to seek congressional authorisation to continue, or pulling funding to end the conflict. Trump will also have a harder time installing Cabinet and judicial nominees, including for another potential Supreme Court vacancy, should one arise.

The Democrats did not, however, achieve the two-thirds supermajority in the Senate, 67 seats, that would allow them alone to remove the president through an impeachment process. That limit means the new majority can block, investigate and legislate, but it cannot by itself remove a president, and it will still need Republican votes to pass most bills, given the Senate's filibuster rules.

With several races still too close to call on election night, it took several days before the final results were known, but the shape of the outcome was clear within hours of the polls closing. For the final two years of Trump's term, the president will face a divided government, with Democrats controlling both chambers of Congress and a mandate, as they see it, to check his agenda on the economy, on trade, on the war in Iran and on the courts. The scale of the victory, stretching into traditionally conservative states including Texas, Kansas, Mississippi and South Carolina, has potentially altered the map for the 2028 presidential election, and both parties will be studying it closely in the months ahead.
