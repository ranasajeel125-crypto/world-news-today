Mexico Captures 'El Chapo Isidro', One of the FBI's 10 Most Wanted, in Fierce Gunfight in Sinaloa

world · 22 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Mexican soldiers stand guard in Sinaloa after the capture of Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as “El Chapo Isidro”, one of the FBI’s 10 most wanted fugitives, in a military operation on Friday night that left 11 people dead.

Mexican military forces have captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as “El Chapo Isidro”, one of the FBI’s 10 most wanted fugitives, in a special forces operation in the north-western state of Sinaloa that ended in a fierce gunfight, leaving four soldiers and seven suspected cartel gunmen dead.

Mexico’s defence ministry announced on Saturday that army special forces and other elite military units had seized the 44-year-old drug lord inside a house in the city of Guasave on Friday night, in what it described as a three-phase operation carried out using “stealth and surprise”. The operation was conducted with intelligence support from the United States, the ministry said, and Meza Flores was taken to Mexico City afterwards, where he was handed over to the Specialized Prosecutor’s Office for Organized Crime, known as SEIDO.

The raid was not a quiet one. As soldiers moved in on the house where Meza Flores and his aides were sheltering, they were attacked by armed civilians using explosives and firearms, according to the defence ministry and security officials. Four members of the Mexican Army were killed in the fighting, along with seven people who, officials said, had tried to stop the arrest, Mexico’s Security Secretary, Omar García Harfuch, said. Soldiers seized drugs, cash, ten firearms, a grenade launcher, ammunition and other weapons from the scene.

Meza Flores was not captured alone. Arrested with him were his cousin Germán, known as “Serafín”, who officials say served as his second-in-command, and his brother-in-law José, described as the financial operator of his criminal organisation. In total, security forces arrested eight alleged criminals in the operation: the three men who were flown to Mexico City, and five others who were turned over to the Attorney General’s Office in Culiacán, the Sinaloa state capital.

The capture set off a wave of retaliatory violence across the region. Cartel gunmen set up at least 10 roadblocks in towns around Guasave and attacked security personnel, while gunfire forced the evacuation of Kuroda Park during a professional baseball game, sending fans running for safety. The game was part of the 2026 Liga Mexicana, the country’s premier professional baseball league. Because of the violence, Meza Flores was not flown out of the area until around 4 a.m. the following day, when he was taken to Mexico City aboard a Mexican Air Force aircraft.

Meza Flores is alleged to be the leader of the Meza-Flores Transnational Criminal Organization, a Sinaloa-based trafficking group also known as Los Mazatlecos, and an ally of the Sinaloa Cartel and the Beltrán-Leyva Organization. US authorities say the group has spent decades trafficking heroin, cocaine, fentanyl, methamphetamine and marijuana into the United States. The US State Department had offered a reward of $5 million for information leading to his capture, and he had been under indictment in the United States on a drug-smuggling charge since 2012.

His nickname has caused confusion ever since he first appeared on the FBI’s most wanted list. “Chapo” means “Shorty” in Spanish, and the name is the same one carried by Joaquín “El Chapo” Guzmán, the former Sinaloa Cartel leader now serving a life sentence in a US prison. But the two men are not related, and Meza Flores built his own organisation in the shadow of the older cartel boss. Born on 19 June 1982 in Navojoa, in the neighbouring state of Sonora, he rose through the ranks of the Beltrán-Leyva network before establishing the Meza-Flores organisation as a force in its own right.

The arrest is the latest in a string of captures and killings of high-profile suspected drug traffickers by Mexico, which is under heavy pressure from US President Donald Trump to crack down on the cartels and the flow of fentanyl and other drugs across the border. Washington has made the drug trade a central demand in its dealings with Mexico City, and Mexican security forces have responded with a series of high-profile operations against cartel leadership in recent months.

Guasave, the city where the raid took place, sits in the agricultural heart of Sinaloa, a state long described as the cradle of Mexico’s drug-trafficking industry. The region has been ravaged in recent years by turf wars between rival factions that have killed thousands of people, and the state has become one of the most violent in the country as cartels fight over trafficking routes, territory and the loyalty of local communities. The violence that followed Friday night’s arrest — roadblocks, burning vehicles and gunfire at a family baseball game — is a familiar pattern in Sinaloa, where the capture of a cartel leader is often answered with a show of force designed to paralyse a city.

What happens next will be watched closely on both sides of the border. Meza Flores is wanted in Mexico on charges of organised crime and drug trafficking, and he is also wanted for extradition to the United States, where he faces the drug-smuggling indictment that has stood since 2012. Mexican authorities have not yet announced a date for any extradition, and it is not clear where he is being held or whether he has legal representation. Extradition cases for cartel leaders can take months or years to work through the Mexican courts, and previous high-profile suspects have sometimes been held in Mexico for long periods before being sent north.

For the Mexican government, the operation is a significant public success at a moment when it is under intense scrutiny from Washington. Capturing a fugitive on the FBI’s top-ten list, with American intelligence support, is the kind of result that both governments can point to as evidence that their cooperation is working. For the people of Guasave, though, the immediate reality was different: a night of gunfire, roadblocks and a baseball stadium emptied in fear, and a reminder that in Sinaloa, taking a cartel boss off the streets can be the start of the violence, not the end of it.

Why it matters: the capture removes one of the most wanted drug traffickers in the Americas from the streets, and it is the latest sign that Mexico is stepping up its campaign against cartel leadership under pressure from the Trump administration. But the cost of the operation — eleven dead, including four soldiers, and a city thrown into panic — shows how deeply entrenched the cartels remain in Sinaloa, and how much violence a single arrest can still unleash. Whether the blow weakens the Meza-Flores organisation or simply opens a new round of fighting over its territory will become clear in the weeks ahead, as will the question of whether Washington gets its extradition.
