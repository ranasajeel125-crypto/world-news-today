Hackers Breach US Air Force's Flagship Cloud One Platform, Putting Sensitive Military Data at Risk

technology · 27 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Hackers have breached Cloud One, the US Air Force's flagship cloud platform, which powers scores of applications from personnel pay to tracking bombs and missiles at more than 200 locations worldwide. The Air Force has declined to comment on the security of specific systems.

Hackers have breached the US Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, in a development that could jeopardise a vast swathe of sensitive tactical, personnel and logistics data, three people familiar with the matter told Reuters on Friday.

The intrusion at Cloud One was discovered within the past month or so during work to upgrade the system, two of the people said, adding that the discovery has led to disruption across the platform. Reuters could not establish the identity of the hackers, the full scope of the intrusion or whether any data had been stolen — and that uncertainty, as much as the breach itself, is what makes the incident so serious for the US military.

In a statement to Reuters, the Air Force said it “does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems. We continuously take prudent measures to protect Department (of the Air Force) information, systems and mission capabilities.” It offered no further detail on what had happened, what had been affected, or how the breach was found.

Cloud One is not a peripheral system. Initiated in 2017, it was meant to serve as a one-stop shop for the Air Force's IT needs, combining the resources of four operators — Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet — to power scores of applications, including ones that manage personnel data and pay, or logistics and maintenance. It is among the US military's biggest cloud platforms, according to two of the sources. “A breach here is a big deal,” one of the sources told Reuters.

The applications running on Cloud One show just how much is at stake. Among them are the Air Force's Cloud-Based Command and Control system, which collates air defence radar and other data feeds from across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles across more than 200 US Air Force locations around the world. A platform that touches air defence data, munitions inventories, personnel records and pay is, by design, one of the most sensitive networks the US military operates — and one of the most attractive targets for an intruder.

Investigators have called in Cyber Command, the US military's cyber force, to assess the damage, one of the sources said. Cyber Command did not respond to requests for comment. The involvement of the military's own cyber command signals that the Air Force is treating the incident as a serious security event rather than a routine IT problem, even though the public record so far contains no confirmed account of what the intruders did once they were inside.

The discovery of the hack has already led to a range of disruption across the platform. Two of the sources, along with a fourth person familiar with the matter, said that efforts to migrate back-office applications to Cloud One — a longstanding military priority — have been paused. Updates to applications already running on Cloud One were blocked, leaving Air Force personnel unable to apply fixes just as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities has been disclosed across the technology industry. A fifth source said that some users had their administrator rights revoked. All five sources spoke to Reuters on condition of anonymity.

That combination — a frozen migration, blocked updates and revoked administrator rights — is the classic signature of a network in lockdown after a serious intrusion. The Air Force is, in effect, trading operational convenience for containment: stopping new systems from moving onto a platform whose security cannot yet be guaranteed, and preventing changes to the systems already there until investigators understand how the intruders got in and what they could reach.

One source said that the cyber intruders exploited a software vulnerability to reach the control plane. That person likened the control plane to a universal remote, as intruders in theory could leverage their access to check the operational status of military aircraft or the movement of spare parts, though it was unclear whether the hackers did so in this case. Reuters could not identify the specific vulnerability the hackers exploited. The distinction matters: reaching the control plane is not the same as using it, and on the evidence available so far, nobody outside the investigation can say what the intruders actually did with the access they gained.

The contract history adds another layer to the story. SAIC was the prime contractor for Cloud One until 2025, when Leidos took over. SAIC did not respond to a request seeking comment. Leidos referred questions to the Air Force. The handover means the platform changed prime contractors only last year, and the breach was discovered during upgrade work — a reminder that large, complex migrations are exactly the moments when security gaps are most likely to be exposed, and most likely to be found.

The breach comes at a moment of heightened anxiety over AI-powered intrusions, a particularly serious problem for cloud-based networks because of the ability of AI models to swiftly detect weaknesses across wide surface areas. A cloud platform that aggregates scores of applications is, in that sense, the worst kind of target: a single vulnerability can open a door to many systems at once, and automated tools can probe a wide attack surface far faster than a human attacker ever could.

It also comes in the middle of a grim run of disclosures for the US government. The US government already has been dealing with the fallout from at least six other recently disclosed cyber incidents, including two at the FBI, a previous hack of Pentagon data, a recent leak of Department of Homeland Security personnel information, a breach affecting at least two spy agencies and a hack at the Treasury Department. This site reported only last week that the FBI is investigating a hack of the National Crime Information Center database — a second major US government system breach disclosed within days of the Cloud One intrusion.

Cloud One has long been in the crosshairs of hackers. In a 2023 commentary carried by the online publication FedScoop, Cloud One's leadership touted what they called a “100% success rate” in beating back what they said was a “staggering” number of attempts to penetrate the platform. “There is no room for error when it comes to security,” Cloud One's leadership wrote at the time. That record has now been broken — and the irony is that the breach was found not by an alarm going off, but during routine upgrade work, which raises the obvious question of how long the intruders had been inside before they were noticed.

Why it matters: Cloud One was built to be the Air Force's single, secure home for everything from air defence radar feeds to munitions tracking to personnel pay. A breach of that platform does not just threaten one database; it threatens the connective tissue of the service's daily operations. With the hackers unidentified, the scope unknown and no confirmation either way on whether data was stolen, the Air Force now faces the hardest part of any intrusion — proving a negative, that nothing vital was taken, while its migration programme sits frozen and its systems go unpatched. The next disclosure, from Cyber Command's damage assessment, will tell the world how bad this really was.
