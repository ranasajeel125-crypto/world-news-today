Israel's President Tasks Opposition Leader Eisenkot With Forming Government After Deadlocked Election

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Eisenkot, leader of the centrist Yashar party, has 28 days to assemble a coalition commanding 61 of the Knesset's 120 seats; Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister.

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Israel's president on Saturday chose Gadi Eisenkot, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's strongest rival, to try to form a new government, following a deadlocked election that left neither man with a clear path to power, Reuters reported.

President Isaac Herzog told Eisenkot of his decision on Saturday evening and formally handed him the mandate at a ceremony at the president's residence on Sunday, November 8. The former military chief, who leads the centrist Yashar party, will then have 28 days to assemble a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension. That gives him until December 6 to present a government for a confidence vote, or until December 20 if Herzog grants the extension.

Netanyahu's religious-nationalist coalition lost its majority in the 27 October election but still emerged as the largest bloc, with 53 of the Knesset's 120 seats; his Likud party finished first with 27 seats. Eisenkot's four-party alliance won 48 seats, and two Arab parties won a combined 19 seats, leaving no camp with the 61 seats needed to govern. In consultations with Herzog, factions representing 67 of the Knesset's 120 members recommended Eisenkot for the first attempt at forming a government, against 46 for Netanyahu. The ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism party, which holds seven seats, declined to recommend either man.

The arithmetic behind the recommendation is fragile: 19 of the 67 recommendations came from the two Arab parties, whose support Eisenkot's bloc has refused to rely on for governing, while Eisenkot has repeatedly said he will not serve in a government with Netanyahu. During his campaign and after the election, Eisenkot promoted a minority government based on the four Jewish opposition parties — Yashar, Beyachad, the Democrats and Yisrael Beiteinu — which together hold 48 seats, leaving him dependent on support from outside the coalition to reach the required majority. He has also called for a national emergency government, open even to Likud, built around four principles set out by Yashar, though the details of that offer and who would accept it remain unclear.

Eisenkot is a former chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces, and he entered politics as a security figure promising competence and national unity after years of political deadlock and war. His Yashar party campaigned as a centrist alternative to Netanyahu, who is Israel's longest-serving leader and has dominated Israeli politics for most of the past two decades. Netanyahu remains in office as caretaker prime minister while Eisenkot tries to build a coalition, and if Eisenkot fails, the mandate is expected to pass to Netanyahu for a further 28 days.

The process is governed by Israel's Basic Law: The Government, and the timetable is precise. If the candidate who receives the mandate fails to form a government within the allotted time, tells the president the effort has failed, or presents a government that fails to win a confidence vote, the president must decide within three days whether to assign the task to another member of the Knesset or to inform the Knesset speaker that he sees no possibility of forming a government. A second candidate would then have 28 days to establish a coalition, without the possibility of an extension. That would take the process to January 20, 2027, if Netanyahu were given the second mandate.

If the second attempt also fails, or Herzog decides not to appoint another candidate, there are 21 days in which at least 61 members of the Knesset can jointly request that the president assign the task to a particular lawmaker. That candidate would have 14 days to form a government, taking the deadline to February 26, 2027. Should all attempts fail, the Knesset would be dissolved and another election would be held, most likely around June 2027. Israel has been through repeated elections in recent years when no coalition could be formed, and the prospect of another vote is real.

The obstacles facing Eisenkot are formidable. Beyond the numbers, the parties he needs are divided on some of the most sensitive issues in Israeli politics, including the conscription of ultra-Orthodox men into the military, the future of the war and the shape of any post-war arrangement, and the role of Arab parties in supporting a government from outside. The Arab parties hold a record 19 seats, and their support was essential to Eisenkot receiving the mandate, but the parties in his own bloc have been reluctant to depend on them, and some figures on the right, including Avigdor Liberman of Yisrael Beiteinu, have resisted any arrangement that relies on Arab support.

For Netanyahu, the mandate going to Eisenkot is a setback but not a defeat. He remains prime minister, his bloc is the largest in the Knesset, and he is the obvious candidate for the second mandate if Eisenkot cannot assemble 61 seats. His supporters argue that only he can hold together a right-wing government, while his opponents argue that the election showed the country wants a change. The next 28 days will test whether Eisenkot can turn a recommendation from 67 lawmakers into a working majority, or whether Israel is heading, once again, for another election.
