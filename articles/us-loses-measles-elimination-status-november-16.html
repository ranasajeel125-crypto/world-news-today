US Loses Measles-Free Status After 26 Years as Virus Spreads for More Than a Year

world · 16 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Measles cases in the United States reached 5,412 in 2026 as of 12 November, the highest total in 35 years, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

The United States is no longer considered measles-free, reversing a hard-won public health achievement the country had maintained for 26 years, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has told the Trump administration in a letter, according to a senior administration official. PAHO is expected to make the formal announcement on Tuesday.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the letter on Friday. In it, the international health body urged the United States to intensify its vaccination efforts to halt transmission of the highly contagious virus, the official said, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss a decision that is not yet public.

The decision comes as the United States battles the highest number of measles cases it has seen in more than three decades. About 7,700 people in the country have been infected with measles and 13 have died since 2025, Reuters reported. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has recorded 5,412 confirmed cases in 2026 as of 12 November, the highest total in 35 years.

Elimination does not mean a country has no cases at all. It means the virus is no longer spreading on its own: occasional outbreaks imported from abroad fizzle out instead of taking hold. Under the World Health Organization's definition, a country loses elimination status when the same strain of measles passes from person to person for at least 12 months without a break. That is what has now happened in the United States.

As part of its evaluation, PAHO reviewed epidemiological and laboratory evidence submitted by the CDC to determine whether the same strain had been circulating for 12 months or longer. The outbreak in Utah, which began in June 2025, was key to the determination, the senior administration official said. KFF Health News, citing researchers with direct knowledge of the deliberations, reported that PAHO based its assessment on two analyses: one by the CDC and another by an independent group of measles experts. Researchers had anticipated the conclusion after the Utah outbreak surpassed the one-year mark in June.

The scale of the resurgence is now clear in the numbers. In the past two years, more than 830 people have been hospitalised with measles in the United States, and more than 8,000 people have been sickened in outbreaks that researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimate have cost roughly $125 million in medical care, public health response and lost productivity. States have reported 13 measles-associated deaths, including a seven-month-old baby in New York and infants in Pennsylvania and Wisconsin in recent months. Babies are typically not vaccinated before they are a year old.

Pennsylvania is battling its worst outbreak of the disease in decades. The state reported 1,561 cases as of Monday, up 19 from the previous update on Friday, with hospitalisations rising to 310, according to the state health department's dashboard. The CDC last week confirmed six measles-related deaths nationally, though its dashboard counts four fewer deaths than state health departments have reported this year.

The current wave began just after President Donald Trump took office in 2025, when a measles outbreak exploded in West Texas. Within seven months it surpassed the size of any outbreak in the United States since 1992, spread to Mexico and at least three other states, sickened more than 4,500 people in the two countries and killed at least 16. It was the largest American measles outbreak in more than 30 years.

Measles outbreaks are entirely preventable. Two doses of the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine prevent about 97% of infections, so when almost everyone in a community is fully vaccinated, the virus cannot spread. Public health experts say at least 95% of people within a community need to be immunised for outbreaks to peter out and for vulnerable people, such as infants and those with weakened immune systems, to be protected. That shield, known as herd immunity, is what kept imported cases from taking hold for a quarter of a century.

The resurgence coincides with falling immunisation rates. Childhood vaccination rates in the United States have steadily declined since the Covid pandemic began, and the Department of Health and Human Services attributes the measles comeback to vaccination gaps that opened years before the current administration. But many scientists also blame the Trump administration for spreading vaccine misinformation and stifling a swift, strong response to the outbreaks. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who led a prominent anti-vaccine organisation before taking the helm at the department, has for years spread doubts, without scientific evidence, about the safety of measles vaccines.

The administration cut millions of dollars from children's immunisation programmes run by state health departments and terminated funds for research on vaccine hesitancy. After Kennedy sacked CDC director Susan Monarez in August 2025, she told Congress she was fired because she would not preapprove changes to vaccine recommendations without reviewing the evidence, adding that the country had already seen its largest measles outbreak in more than 30 years and that, if vaccine protections were weakened, preventable diseases would return. Spokespeople for the department and the White House defended the administration, saying the president and the secretary have repeatedly encouraged measles vaccination, coordinated with states and facilitated the deployment of vaccine doses across the country. The department said Kennedy and the CDC have provided about $100 million in additional funding to respond to outbreaks.

The loss of elimination status marks the end of a public health story that began with a national effort a generation ago. By 2000, the CDC and PAHO determined that measles transmission had been eliminated in the United States. The achievement followed a surge from 1989 to 1991, when an entire ward of a children's hospital in Minnesota was devoted to children with measles and two children there died. When data suggested some children were not mounting a strong response to a single dose of the vaccine, a CDC advisory committee recommended two doses; when analyses found many children with measles had not been vaccinated because of cost, the government launched the Vaccines for Children programme to make immunisations available free of charge to underinsured families. Cases plummeted.

It would be 14 years before a significant outbreak. In 2015 the virus took off among unvaccinated children at Disney parks in California, and Congress treated some 125 cases as an emergency, summoning the CDC's Anne Schuchat to testify. In 2019, a large outbreak concentrated in Orthodox Jewish communities in New York and New Jersey again brought House and Senate committees together. Each time, the federal government responded with press conferences, clinical guidelines and detailed reports in the CDC's flagship journal, the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report. That is no longer the case, scientists told KFF Health News: the journal has not published a single report on the current outbreaks this year, and CDC scientists say they no longer expect to publish their findings there because the process has become slow, opaque and unpredictable.

To regain measles elimination status, the current outbreaks must end, and then future outbreaks must stop spreading within a year, said Paul Rota, a microbiologist and former CDC official. With outbreaks expanding in several states, he said, that moment is not on the horizon. “Bigger fires create more embers, and any one of those embers can seed another fire,” he said. CDC Director Erica Schwartz told Reuters in an interview last week, before the decision, that if the United States lost elimination status the agency would be doing “the same exact thing”, including working with states to encourage vaccination.

Why it matters: the United States has now joined the ranks of countries where measles spreads on its own again, 26 years after it was declared beaten. The loss is measured not in a label but in hospital wards: more than 830 people hospitalised, 13 dead, and infants too young to be vaccinated among the victims. “It's a very sad moment,” said Anne Schuchat, an epidemiologist and former deputy director of the CDC. “Measles causes so much unnecessary suffering, everything from a high fever and missed work and school to long-term complications like progressive degenerative nerve disease and death.” The formal announcement on Tuesday will make official what the virus has already demonstrated: in the richest country in the world, a disease that was eliminated a quarter of a century ago has come back, and stopping it again will require the one tool that has always worked — vaccinating enough children that the virus has nowhere left to go.
