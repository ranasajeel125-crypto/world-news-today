Krakow Shooting Toll Rises to Nine as Three Bodies Found at Gunman's Home

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Police said the three bodies found in Ostrowiec Swietokrzyski, about 150 km from Krakow, were two women and a man; the defence minister described the attack as an act of terror.

The death toll from Saturday's mass shooting in Krakow rose to nine after police found three more bodies at the gunman's home, hours after he opened fire in the city's Main Market Square, killing six people including a British citizen, Polish officials said.

Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the bodies of two women and a man were found at the suspect's home in Ostrowiec Swietokrzyski, a city about 150 kilometres northeast of Krakow, and were believed to be members of the gunman's close family. Police said on Sunday that the three were the attacker's sister, mother and father, and that they were believed to have been shot. The discovery meant the gunman was suspected of killing three members of his own family before driving to Krakow.

Six people, four women and two men, were killed in the square, among them three Polish citizens, one British citizen and two people who had not yet been identified, police said. Later reports identified the British victim as a 32-year-old man. At least 15 people were injured, including two police officers, one of them critically, and five foreign nationals from Austria, Germany, Portugal, Russia and Britain. Many of the wounded were in life-threatening condition after surgery, hospital officials said. Tusk later named the two wounded officers as Senior Constable Jakub Kulik and Senior Constable Artur Moranski, one of whom was fighting for his life.

The suspect, a 24-year-old Polish citizen from Ostrowiec Swietokrzyski, was shot and wounded by officers and remains in hospital, police said. There was confusion on Saturday about his age, with authorities first saying 24 and later 25, before confirming on Sunday that he is 24. He had no criminal record and held a legal gun permit, police said. On Monday morning, police announced that he had died in hospital from his injuries.

Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski said the man held "very radical" and "ultranationalistic" views, and police said they believed he was inspired by far-right ideology. "Everything indicates that he was fascinated by far-right ideologies," Kierwinski said at a news conference. Defence Minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz described the attack as an "act of terror", and the Krakow branch of Poland's National Prosecutor's Office opened an investigation describing the mass shooting as an act of terrorism. Justice Minister Waldemar Zurek said there was a very strong indication of right-wing extremism behind the gunman's actions, although prosecutors said on Monday that investigations into his motive remained ongoing.

Police said the gunman drove onto the square at around 3 p.m. local time on Saturday and fired at random people from his car, using an automatic weapon and military-style equipment. He was wearing military-style fatigues and a bulletproof vest and carried a military-type long gun, a rifle similar in design to an AR-15, as well as a pistol. A Krakow police spokesperson, Katarzyna Cislo, said the attacker went on a "wild ride" around the Main Square, stopping at multiple locations and opening fire at each one, which is why people were wounded at several places. Witnesses said he also fired at a tram, but no one was injured. More weapons and ammunition were found inside his car, and investigators later seized 11 weapons from the suspect, his vehicle and his residence, including two AR-15 semi-automatic rifles.

When counter-terrorism police searched the family home, they found a Gadsden flag, an American Revolutionary-era flag often linked to the US far right, and an examination of the suspect's computer found evidence suggesting he had visited websites concerning Anders Breivik, the Norwegian neo-Nazi who killed 77 people in July 2011. A senior researcher at the International Centre for Counter-Terrorism in The Hague noted that the suspect's firearms and military-style equipment resembled those used by far-right extremists in Europe and elsewhere. The defence minister denied reports that the man was a soldier, saying he had never had anything to do with the military. The nationalist opposition dismissed suggestions of a far-right connection, with Law and Justice party leader Jaroslaw Kaczynski calling the claims a blatant lie.

A massive armed police presence was deployed across Krakow on Saturday evening, and security measures were ramped up in the capital Warsaw and elsewhere. Tusk said the police had prevented a much larger massacre that could otherwise have occurred. A period of mourning was declared in Krakow until 11 November, and the office of President Karol Nawrocki said the country would observe national mourning on 8 and 9 November. Poland began two days of national mourning on Sunday as police bolstered security in the city's historic main market square.

Police said they were not looking for a second person. Public shootings are rare in Poland, where gun laws are stricter than in the United States, and the country has traditionally considered itself safer than several Western European countries that have experienced similar attacks. The Main Market Square, known as Rynek Glowny, is one of Poland's most visited tourist destinations, in the heart of Krakow's medieval Old Town, and a large number of people were there on a busy Saturday afternoon when the shooting began, which is why the attack has sent shockwaves through the country.
