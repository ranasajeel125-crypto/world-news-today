Hungary Recovers Nearly $20 Million in Cash in Bribery Probe of Former Orbán Minister

world · 14 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The recovered cash — 5.07 billion forints, €2.66 million and $1.26 million — took a dozen Treasury workers eight hours to count, Hungary’s finance minister said.

Hungarian authorities have recovered nearly $20 million in cash — some of it buried in forests, the rest hidden in farm buildings, warehouses and a residential property — in a bribery investigation into Miklós Seszták, a minister who served under former prime minister Viktor Orbán.

“During the searches, investigators found large amounts of cash buried in forests, hidden in farm buildings, warehouses and a residential property, and stored in a high-value safe in a secluded location,” the National Asset Recovery and Protection Office said late on Friday. The haul — 5.07 billion forints, worth about $15.33 million, plus €2.66 million and $1.26 million — took a dozen State Treasury workers eight hours to count, using five banknote counters, Finance Minister András Kármán said.

Seszták was Orbán’s development minister between 2014 and 2018. He has denied wrongdoing. His lawyer, Gábor Papp, did not respond to an emailed request for comment on the recovery office’s statement.

The cash recovery is the most striking find so far in a wider clampdown that has already seen two former ministers from Orbán’s government and the former central bank chief, György Matolcsy, arrested on graft accusations in separate investigations. All three have denied wrongdoing. Authorities have also detained Orbán’s brother in a people-smuggling case — an arrest the former leader dismissed as an act of political revenge.

The crackdown is part of the anti-corruption drive Prime Minister Péter Magyar has dubbed “Operation Purgatory”. A survey by the Median pollster, released on the news website HVG this week, found that 70% of respondents considered the drive “very important” — a sign that the campaign against alleged graft has broad public backing as it moves from the previous government into the courts.

Magyar said the recovered cash signalled the end of what he described as a “mafia” led by Orbán’s right-wing Fidesz party and its Christian Democrat allies during Orbán’s 16-year rule. The prime minister ousted the nationalist leader in an April election, pledging to mend ties with the European Union — strained by years of conflict under Orbán — and to launch a sweeping anti-corruption drive into allegations of graft.

Political scientist Gábor Török said the images of the large piles of cash were so vivid that they likely spelled doom for Fidesz, whose public support has halved to just 16% of voters by November from its levels before the vote, according to a Median survey. The numbers suggest that a party that dominated Hungarian politics for a decade and a half is now struggling to convince voters it was not part of the problem its successor is now excavating — literally, in some cases, from the ground.

The background: Orbán governed Hungary for 16 years, building a nationalist government that repeatedly clashed with Brussels over the rule of law and the handling of EU funds. Those disputes left Hungary’s relationship with the European Union badly strained. In April, voters ended that era, electing Magyar — a centre-right challenger who promised both to repair the relationship with the EU and to open the books on the previous government’s record. The anti-corruption drive, and this week’s cash recovery, are the sharpest expression yet of that promise being carried out.

What happens next: the investigation into Seszták continues, and the question now is whether the recovered cash leads to formal charges — and whether the drive widens to others from the former government. The two arrested former ministers and the former central bank chief are already facing their own cases, and the detention of Orbán’s brother has added a personal edge to a campaign the former prime minister calls political revenge. For Magyar, the political test is whether the spectacle of buried cash hardens public support for the clean-up or is dismissed by Fidesz supporters as a vendetta.

Why it matters: few images capture a political era’s end as vividly as banknotes dug out of a forest floor. The cash — counted for eight hours by a dozen treasury workers — has become the symbol of Magyar’s first months in office and of the reckoning facing Orbán’s Fidesz, now polling at barely a sixth of voters. For Hungary, the stakes go beyond one minister: the drive will test whether the country’s institutions can investigate the powerful without the process itself becoming the next political fight, and whether a government elected on a promise of clean hands can deliver one.
