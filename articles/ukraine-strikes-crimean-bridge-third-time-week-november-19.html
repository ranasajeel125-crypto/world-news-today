Ukraine Strikes Crimean Bridge for Third Time in a Week, Halting Trains Hours Before Putin Meets Trump and Xi

world · 19 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The 19-kilometre bridge across the Kerch Strait, opened by Vladimir Putin in 2018, has now been attacked three times in a week, with its road section collapsed and its railway section now also hit.

Ukraine has struck the bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea for the third time in a week, forcing a suspension of train services across the crossing, Russian authorities said on Thursday, hours before President Vladimir Putin was due to meet the leaders of China and the United States.

Russia's transport ministry said nobody had been hurt in the overnight attack and that specialists were assessing the damage to the bridge. "As a result of a nighttime drone attack, train services on the railway section of the Crimean Bridge have been temporarily suspended," the ministry said in a statement on Telegram, Reuters reported.

Sergei Aksyonov, the Moscow-installed governor of Crimea, confirmed the attack earlier on Thursday, saying traffic had been temporarily halted and that the relevant services were on site. The BBC also reported that Aksyonov had confirmed the strike in the early hours of Thursday and that the damage was being assessed.

Thursday's attack is the third on the crossing this week, and the first to hit its railway section. Ukrainian monitoring channels suggested the overnight strike may have targeted the rail span, which had survived Tuesday's far more destructive attack largely intact. There was no independent confirmation of the number of hits or the type of weapons used, and no reports that the railway section had been completely destroyed.

The timing is awkward for the Kremlin. The strike came hours before Putin was due to take part in what Moscow has cast as a historic trilateral summit with US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation leaders' meeting. The Kremlin said one hour had been allocated for the three-way meeting, and that a separate Putin-Trump encounter would take place after the APEC plenary session, with no full-scale bilateral negotiations planned.

Moscow hopes the Shenzhen meeting will send a signal to allies and rivals alike that Russia — and not the European Union and Britain — is part of an emerging new multilateral world order, Reuters reported. A fresh attack on one of Putin's signature projects, on the very morning of that meeting, undercuts that message and hands Ukraine a visible demonstration of its reach at the moment the world's attention is on the summit.

The scale of the damage from this week's earlier strikes was already severe. On Tuesday, Ukrainian drones and missiles destroyed the central road section of the bridge, forcing its main road arch to crumble into the sea and leaving a yawning gap, according to videos and images verified by Reuters. The Guardian reported that a 227-metre road arch span had fallen into the Kerch Strait, and BBC Verify confirmed from daylight imagery that the arch, weighing thousands of tonnes, now lies on the seabed with about 200 metres of roadway below the water.

The first attack of the week, on Monday, caused more minor damage, but gave Ukrainian forces a chance to see how Russian air defences would react, Reuters reported. The Kremlin said on Monday that the first attack had caused only minor damage and limited traffic resumed, before the second strike in the early hours of Tuesday did far more damage: the road arch over the navigable span and the roadway beside it are now in the water, according to photos and video from several angles.

President Volodymyr Zelenskiy has said the strikes on the bridge were part of an operation that had been under way for several weeks, and the BBC reported that he described Tuesday's attack as the seventh stage of that operation. Ukraine has repeatedly targeted the Kerch Bridge since Russia's full-scale invasion began in 2022, including a truck bomb in October 2022 that killed four people and limited the bridge's capacity for months.

The 19-kilometre (12-mile) bridge, opened by Putin in 2018 after Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014, is one of his signature infrastructure projects and the longest crossing in Europe. The $3.7 billion crossing was built to handle 40,000 cars a day and move 14 million passengers and 13 million tons of cargo a year, according to Russian state media, and it has been a vital supply line for Moscow's troops on the peninsula. Ukraine has declared the bridge illegal.

The debris from Tuesday's collapse also appears to have blocked the main navigable route beneath the bridge, which connects the Sea of Azov with the Black Sea and is used by shipping from the ports of Berdiansk and Mariupol, the Guardian reported. With the road section down and the railway now also hit, traffic from Crimea can leave the peninsula only via a land corridor through occupied southern Ukraine, which is vulnerable to Ukrainian drones.

Moscow has moved quickly to respond to the earlier damage. The Kremlin has convened a government commission on the consequences of the attack, led by Deputy Prime Minister Marat Khusnullin, and Russian officials said funds, designers and builders had already been gathered for repairs. Open-source analysts estimated that the damaged section could take years to rebuild, and Russia's Investigative Committee has opened a terrorism investigation, calling the attack a "terrorist act by Ukrainian armed groups".

The exchange of strikes has run in both directions this week. Russian missiles hit two major bridges over the Dnipro River in Kyiv late on Monday, the city's mayor said, damaging critical infrastructure and cutting heating and water supplies in parts of the capital. Two people were killed in Kyiv in the overnight assault, officials said, and Aksyonov said two people had been killed in the wider attack on Crimea.

The bridge strikes are also shaping the diplomacy around them. On Thursday, Ukraine's main European allies are meeting in Berlin — the leaders of Germany, France, Britain, Poland and Italy, with Zelenskiy and NATO chief Mark Rutte — in a gathering deliberately timed so Europe could give a quick, joint response to any announcements coming out of Shenzhen. Britain used the occasion to announce a new £260 million support package for Ukraine, including more than 1,000 long-range strike drones, and the European Commission has proposed a €45 billion loan to fill Ukraine's funding gap for next year.

What happens next: the full extent of Thursday's damage is still being assessed, and it is not yet clear how long the railway section will stay closed or whether the road section can be repaired on any timescale that matters to the war. The more immediate test comes in Shenzhen, where the trilateral meeting will show whether Putin can turn the summit into the diplomatic signal Moscow wants — or whether a bridge in ruins, on the morning of the meeting, becomes the image that defines it.

Why it matters: the Crimean Bridge was built to bind Crimea to Russia permanently, and each strike on it challenges that claim in the most visible way possible. Three attacks in a week, culminating in a hit on the railway that survived the first two, show that Ukraine can keep reaching a target Russia has spent years defending — and that it chose to do so on the day Putin wanted the world to see him as an equal partner in a new world order. For the people of Crimea, the practical effect is simpler: their only direct link to Russia is now cut, and the land route that remains runs through a war zone.
