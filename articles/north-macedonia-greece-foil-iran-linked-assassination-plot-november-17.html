North Macedonia and Greece Say They Foiled Iran-Linked Assassination Plot

world · 17 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

North Macedonia's National Security Agency said the network acted on the instructions of an Iranian citizen linked to Iran's security services, and that the intended target was not identified.

North Macedonia and Greece said on Tuesday that they had arrested five people and broken up a suspected Iran-linked network planning an assassination in Greece, in a joint counter-intelligence operation that officials in both countries said followed months of surveillance.

North Macedonia's National Security Agency said in a statement that it had uncovered a network which, acting on the instructions of an Iranian citizen, had planned to carry out the assassination of a person in Greece whose identity it did not disclose. Its investigation, carried out in cooperation with Greece's intelligence service, concluded that the Iranian citizen was linked to Iran's security services and to an Iranian crime boss who helped hire the operators, Reuters reported.

The agency said the group had undertaken preparatory logistical activities for the killing. Greek security officials said the suspects, who have criminal records, had travelled to Iran, where they met their handler. One of the people arrested in North Macedonia had likely revealed details of the meeting with the Iranian and of the plans for the assassination, a Greek intelligence official said.

The arrests unfolded in two stages. A North Macedonian national was arrested in Greece on 12 November and extradited to North Macedonia, whose security services then moved to arrest the other four members of the group. North Macedonia's Interior Ministry confirmed that five people had been arrested in total. Iran did not immediately respond to a request for comment, Reuters reported.

North Macedonia's agency identified the alleged handler as Iranian national Edris Jahangiri Mavaneh, whom it described as a collaborator of Iran's security and intelligence services and the main instigator of the planned attack. Greek media reports citing the agency said Mavaneh maintained a wide network of contacts and was linked to the Iranian drug lord Naji Sharifi Zindashti, the crime boss Reuters reported had helped recruit the operators. The authorities have not said who the intended victim was, and have not disclosed the charges in detail.

Greek media reported that the investigation had been running since late February or early March, conducted by Greece's National Intelligence Service in cooperation with North Macedonia's agency and other Western services. North Macedonia's agency said that since the beginning of 2026 it had been working to uncover “the intentions and plans of individuals linked to foreign/Iranian actors, to commit terrorist acts”. It said its investigations would continue in an effort to identify other people involved.

The case is a reminder of how much of Europe's counter-terrorism work now happens across borders. Greece and North Macedonia are both NATO members and neighbours with a shared interest in the security of the Balkans, and the operation was run jointly by their intelligence services, with the first arrest made on Greek soil and the rest in North Macedonia. That kind of cooperation is the difference between a plot discovered in time and one discovered too late: the network was, on the agencies' account, still in its preparatory phase when the arrests were made, and no attack took place.

The account the agencies gave also fits a pattern that European security services have described before: a handler directing the operation from abroad, and local criminals recruited to do the work. The suspects, according to Greek officials, were not ideologues but people with criminal records who had travelled to meet their handler in Iran. Using organised-crime figures as cut-outs gives the sponsor distance from the act and the operators a motive that has nothing to do with politics. It also, as this case shows, leaves a trail — travel, meetings and money — that intelligence services can follow.

The announcement came at a moment of acute tension between Iran and the United States. In recent days Iran has fired drones at a US military radar at Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain, in the second such attack in three days. None of that proves a connection to the alleged plot in Greece, and the agencies have not suggested one. But it is the backdrop against which European governments are reading any sign of Iranian activity on their soil, and it helps explain why the announcement was made jointly and in public — a warning, as much as a report of an arrest.

What happens next will play out in North Macedonia's courts. The five suspects are in custody there, and the investigation is continuing, with the agencies saying they are still working to identify others involved. The public record so far leaves the most important questions unanswered: who the target was, how far the preparations had advanced, and whether the network reached beyond the five people arrested. Iran has yet to respond to the allegation, and any formal denial — or any diplomatic protest from Athens or Skopje — is still to come.

Why it matters: an assassination on European soil, carried out by hired criminals on the instructions of a foreign intelligence service, would be an attack on the sovereignty of the country where it happened and a test of the continent's ability to protect people living within its borders. That it was stopped in the preparatory phase is the good news in this story, and a credit to the two services that ran the operation together. The harder question it leaves behind is how many such networks exist, and how many are caught only because someone, somewhere, talks.
