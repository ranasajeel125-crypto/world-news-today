German Military Bans Chinese-Made Drones Over Security Concerns

world · 10 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The drones were bought by individual units in recent years under a 2024 strategy that included a “white list” of approved systems — including several made by DJI, the world's largest drone maker — and are now being withdrawn as quickly as possible.

Germany's military has banned commercially available Chinese-made drones from its barracks and training grounds with immediate effect, over security concerns, Der Spiegel reported on Tuesday. The defence ministry confirmed the decision the following day.

The drones, bought by individual military units in recent years with limited bureaucratic oversight, are being withdrawn from service as quickly as possible, the magazine reported. Germany's Defence Ministry was not immediately available for comment outside regular business hours when the report first appeared, but a spokesperson later confirmed that Chinese-made models would no longer be used, and that existing systems would be replaced by products from domestic or, where applicable, European sources.

Local commanders had been allowed to buy the drones under a 2024 strategy that included a "white list" of approved systems, including several made by China-based DJI, the world's largest drone maker, Der Spiegel reported. The Bundeswehr, Germany's armed forces, had used inexpensive, off-the-shelf drones for training purposes, such as target practice, buying them with minimal bureaucratic effort. According to Spiegel's sources, the army may have purchased several thousand Chinese drones, although the ministry is not disclosing the exact number.

Western military forces have grown increasingly concerned that Chinese-made technology could pose a security risk and may be used to transmit sensitive security information to Beijing. The fear is that the drones could transmit photos of barracks and training grounds, as well as data on exercises and German weapons systems, to Chinese intelligence services. Modern commercial drones carry high-resolution cameras, GPS receivers and wireless links, and many rely on software and cloud services provided by the manufacturer, which creates a channel through which data could, in principle, leave the device.

The ministry framed the decision in terms of data and IT security, and of technological sovereignty. "To strengthen the Bundeswehr's self-sufficiency and technological sovereignty, Chinese-made models will no longer be used for target or training purposes," its statement said. A defence ministry spokesperson added that there was no blanket ban on the use of systems from specific countries, and that the decision was based on individual risk assessments aimed at safeguarding military security, IT and data security.

As there is no central record of the purchases made by individual units, the military has had to impose a blanket ban, Der Spiegel added. The decentralised buying is itself part of the problem: because commanders bought drones locally and without a central register, the ministry cannot say with certainty how many Chinese drones are in use, where they are, or what data they may have collected. A blanket ban is the only way to be sure they are all withdrawn.

The ban is not limited to drones. According to Der Spiegel, the Bundeswehr has also restricted vehicles from Chinese companies such as BYD, Geely and NIO from entering barracks and other facilities, due to serious security concerns, with soldiers who own them required to park outside. The ministry said it had no general ban on vehicles from specific countries or manufacturers, but said sensors installed in modern vehicles are seen as potential security risks, and therefore access to sensitive military sites can be restricted where necessary. Modern cars carry cameras, microphones, GPS and mobile data connections, and an electric vehicle in particular gathers detailed information about its surroundings as it drives.

The decision fits a wider pattern across Western militaries. The United States last month prohibited vehicles made by China's BYD on its air base on Japan's Okinawa island, and Poland in February banned Chinese-made vehicles from military facilities over concerns their sensors could be used to collect sensitive data. The United States has also moved against DJI itself, restricting the company's drones for government use and raising the prospect of a wider ban, while several European governments have reviewed Chinese equipment in critical infrastructure, from telecoms networks to port cranes.

DJI, based in Shenzhen, is by far the world's largest maker of civilian drones, and its products are used by police forces, fire services, farmers, film-makers and hobbyists around the world, as well as by the military. The company has consistently denied that its drones send data to the Chinese government, and it has said its products are designed for civilian use. But Western governments have increasingly taken the view that the risk is not about what a company says it does, but about what the law in its home country could compel it to do, and about the difficulty of verifying that a complex piece of hardware and software is free of hidden data channels.

For the Bundeswehr, the practical challenge now is replacement. The Chinese drones were cheap, widely available and easy to fly, which is why commanders bought them in the first place. European alternatives exist, but they are generally more expensive and in some cases less capable, and the military will need to buy them in quantity and train soldiers to use them. The ministry's promise to replace the systems with German or European products will take time and money, and in the meantime some training exercises may have to do without the drones that units had come to rely on.

The episode also illustrates a broader tension in Germany's defence policy. The country is in the middle of a large military build-up, with a special fund of 100 billion euros and a commitment to spend more on defence, but it is also trying to reduce its dependence on foreign suppliers, particularly China, in sensitive technologies. The drone ban is a small part of that effort, but it is a visible one, and it sends a signal to allies that Berlin is taking the security of its own forces seriously.

Whether other European militaries follow suit remains to be seen. Several have also bought Chinese drones for training, and they face the same questions about cost, capability and security. For now, Germany's decision stands as one of the clearest examples of a Western military deciding that the convenience of cheap commercial technology is not worth the risk that it might be watching, and reporting back.
