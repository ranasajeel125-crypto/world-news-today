Spain Approves One of the Housing Decrees That Triggered Snap Election

world · 12 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Spain’s caretaker deputies approved the housing decree by a single vote, a day after a general strike over housing costs brought the country to a standstill.

world · 12 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Spanish lawmakers on Thursday approved one of two housing decrees whose rejection by parliament last month triggered a November 29 early general election, a day after Wednesday's general strike in protest over housing costs.

By a one-vote margin, the standing body of 69 caretaker deputies who remain in place after parliament's dissolution passed the decree, which includes rent caps, a temporary ban on evictions and limits on corporate purchases of residential property. The vote in the 69-member Permanent Deputation was 35 to 34, and the measure approved was Royal Decree-Law 29/2026. The body rejected, also by one vote, another measure, sought by tenants' associations, calling for the automatic renewal of rental contracts.

The approved decree extends protections for vulnerable tenants against eviction until December 31, 2030. It caps annual rent increases at 2 percent through the end of 2027, allows up to two-year extensions on expiring leases, and imposes a 10 percent value-added tax on tourist rentals of 30 nights or less, starting December 1, 2026. The rejected companion measure, Royal Decree-Law 28/2026, would have mandated automatic five- and seven-year renewals of residential rental contracts, a provision tenants' groups had pressed for and landlords' groups had opposed.

The vote came a day after a general strike over housing costs that brought large parts of the country to a standstill. The strike on Wednesday, November 11, was Spain's first general strike in 14 years, and it spread across more than 90 cities. Organisers claimed roughly 4 million people took part, and police reported more than 50 arrests. Workers and tenant groups shut down transport and clashed with police during the 24-hour walkout over soaring rents and stagnant wages, putting immediate pressure on Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez's minority government.

The housing decrees have been at the centre of Spanish politics for weeks. The leftist government's measures, aimed at tackling rising housing costs, came into effect temporarily when the decrees were published in the official state gazette in early October. But when they came before parliament last month, they were rejected, and that rejection triggered the decision to call an early general election for November 29. Because parliament had been dissolved, it fell to the Permanent Deputation, the standing body of 69 deputies who remain in place after dissolution, to decide the fate of the decrees.

Housing has become one of the most urgent issues in Spain. Rents have risen sharply in recent years, particularly in Madrid, Barcelona and other large cities, and in tourist areas where short-term holiday rentals have taken homes out of the long-term market. Many young Spaniards struggle to leave the parental home, and tenants complain of steep increases when contracts come up for renewal. The government's measures were designed to respond to that pressure: capping rent rises, protecting vulnerable households from eviction, and discouraging large companies and investment funds from buying up residential property in bulk.

The opposition has argued the opposite. Opposition leader Alberto Nunez Feijoo, whose conservative People's Party leads in opinion polls, said on Wednesday he would repeal the decrees if he becomes prime minister. The opposition has argued that the measures only reduce housing supply and push up prices, by discouraging landlords from renting out their properties and discouraging investment in new housing. Landlords' groups have made similar arguments, saying that rent caps and limits on evictions make owners reluctant to let their homes, which in the end leaves fewer homes available and higher prices for those that remain.

The political stakes could hardly be higher. The long-term survival of the housing package depends on the outcome of the November 29 snap election and on the new parliament, which is due to convene on December 23. If the People's Party wins, Feijoo has promised repeal, and the measures approved by a single vote on Thursday could be swept away within weeks. If Sanchez's Socialists and their allies hold on, the decrees could become the foundation of a longer-term housing policy. Spain launched its 15-day general election campaign on November 16, setting up a high-stakes ballot in a country gripped by cost-of-living protests.

The strike and the vote also showed how housing has moved from a policy debate to a street issue. Tenant associations, trade unions and housing campaigners organised the November 11 walkout, and the scale of the protest, the first general strike in 14 years, underlined how far the issue has risen up the political agenda. For many of those who marched, the decrees were a test of whether the government was willing to act against rising rents and evictions, and the narrow vote in the Permanent Deputation was a partial victory: one decree saved, the other lost, both by a single vote.

What happens next will be decided by voters on November 29. Until then, the approved decree stands, with its eviction protections running to 2030, its rent cap in place through 2027, and its new tax on short tourist stays taking effect on December 1. But its future, like that of the government that introduced it, is uncertain, and the election that the housing crisis helped to trigger will now decide whether Spain's experiment with rent caps and eviction bans survives or is repealed.
