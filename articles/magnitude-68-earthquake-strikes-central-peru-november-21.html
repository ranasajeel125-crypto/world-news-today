Magnitude 6.8 Earthquake Strikes Central Peru, Sending Lima Residents Into the Streets

world · 21 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

A deep 6.8-magnitude quake shook the Andes and the capital, but early reports showed no casualties or major damage.

A magnitude 6.8 earthquake struck central Peru late on Friday, sending residents of the capital, Lima, rushing into the streets and interrupting a session of Congress, Reuters reported. The tremor was felt across the Andean region and in the capital, but there were no immediate reports of casualties or significant damage.

The German Research Centre for Geosciences put the magnitude at 6.8. Peru's Geophysical Institute located the quake in the Andean region of Junín, at a depth of 129 kilometres, while the United States Geological Survey also recorded the magnitude at 6.8, estimating the depth at about 108 kilometres. The tremor struck at about 7:09pm local time on Friday, 20 November — 00:09 GMT on Saturday, 21 November.

In Lima, people left their homes and buildings as the shaking spread across the city, and a television broadcast showed lawmakers walking out of Congress as the tremor hit. The sight of the legislature emptying underlined how widely the quake was felt, even though the epicentre lay in the mountains well inland from the Pacific coast.

Local authorities in Junín said preliminary checks found no immediate reports of structural damage or casualties around the epicentre. Junín council member Vicente Quispe said the first checks revealed no immediate reports of structural damage or casualties in the area. The US Tsunami Warning System confirmed that the earthquake posed no tsunami threat, and no tsunami watch or advisory was issued.

The depth of the quake is likely to have limited the damage. Earthquakes that strike deep underground spread their energy over a wide area, so they can be felt hundreds of kilometres away, yet they usually shake the ground less violently at the surface than shallow quakes of the same magnitude. That pattern fits the early reports from Peru: a tremor felt across a wide area, including the capital, with no immediate reports of serious damage.

On the scale used by seismologists, a magnitude 6.8 earthquake counts as strong. Quakes in the 6.0 to 6.9 range can cause serious damage in populated areas, particularly when they strike close to the surface — but depth, distance from towns and the strength of local buildings all shape the outcome. Friday's quake was deep, and its epicentre was in the sparsely settled highlands of Junín rather than beneath a city.

Peru is one of the most earthquake-prone countries in the world. It sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, the horseshoe-shaped belt around the Pacific Ocean where tectonic plates meet and where most of the world's earthquakes occur. The country is shaken regularly as the Nazca plate slides beneath the South American plate off its Pacific coast, a slow collision that has built the Andes and keeps the region in almost constant seismic motion.

The deadliest recent reminder of that risk came in August 2007, when a magnitude 8.0 earthquake struck off the coast near Pisco, south of Lima, killing nearly 600 people and destroying tens of thousands of homes. Friday's quake, by contrast, was both smaller and far deeper — and, on the first reports at least, far less destructive.

Junín is a largely rural region in the central Andes, and its capital, Huancayo, sits more than 3,000 metres above sea level. Seismic stations around the world recorded the tremor within minutes, and monitoring agencies in Europe and the United States published their first magnitude estimates within the hour, as they do for every major quake. The speed of those readings is what lets governments and aid agencies judge within minutes whether a disaster is unfolding — or, as in this case, whether the first news is reassuring.

Damage assessments were under way in the hours after the quake, and the picture could change as reports arrive from more remote parts of Junín, where mountain roads and scattered settlements can slow the flow of information. Aftershocks are possible after an earthquake of this size, and seismologists will be watching for further tremors in the region in the coming days.

Why it matters: a magnitude 6.8 earthquake under a densely populated Andean region, felt in a capital city of around 10 million people, is the kind of event that tests a country's readiness in minutes. The early signs are reassuring — no casualties, no major damage, no tsunami — but the depth that spared Peru the worst this time is also a reminder of the force building beneath it. Friday's tremor is a warning that the Ring of Fire is never quiet for long.
