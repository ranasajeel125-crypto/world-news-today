Egypt Authorises Military Support to Defend Saudi Arabia as Houthi Attacks Escalate

world · 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

Egypt, the Arab world's most populous state, has not undertaken a large-scale foreign ground deployment since the 1991 Gulf War.

Parliament authorised Egypt's military on Monday to provide support to help defend Saudi Arabia, parliamentarians said and local media reported, a move that could push Cairo directly into the regional conflict that has expanded with intensified attacks by Yemen's Iran-allied Houthis on the Gulf kingdom.

President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi had asked parliament last week to approve defending its Gulf Arab ally and the world's biggest oil exporter if Riyadh requests help against the Houthis, whose advances also threaten the strategic Bab el-Mandeb Strait, a gateway to Egypt's Suez Canal. The House of Representatives, chaired by Speaker Hisham Badawi, voted with a two-thirds majority in favour of authorising the president to take the necessary measures to support the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, according to a parliament statement.

The parliament, stacked with supporters of Sisi, had held three rare secret meetings on the matter and postponed the vote twice before approving the mandate. The Social Democratic Party, which holds 11 seats in parliament, abstained from the vote after requesting specific objectives, clear information on the mission's scope and duration, and an accurate assessment of the potential risks to the lives of Egyptian soldiers. One MP, Medhat Rekaby al-Mansourawy, said discussions of the potential operation were "surface-level without details", and lawmakers had received assurances it would be defensive in nature.

Sisi has said Egypt's support would be limited to defending Saudi territory and borders rather than attacking Yemen, and Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty has said Cairo advocates exploring every opportunity to resolve the Yemen conflict diplomatically. A regional official, briefed on the matter, said that Egypt will deploy air defence systems and aircraft to help repel missiles and drones fired at Saudi Arabia, and that the Egyptian support is defensive in nature and will not include any ground troops in Yemen.

Egypt has frequently condemned Iranian and Iranian-backed attacks on Gulf countries but has not publicly engaged in direct combat or joined the Mecca mutual defence pact between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey that was signed in August. Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty said this month that Egyptian membership in the pact remains under serious consideration. Abdelatty and Egyptian intelligence chief Hassan Rashad travelled to Riyadh on 21 October to deliver a letter to Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman from Sisi, who said Egypt's support would be limited to defending Saudi territory and borders rather than attacking Yemen.

Egypt's highest security body pledged in a meeting earlier this month to provide "all necessary and possible forms of support" to Saudi Arabia to defend "its security and stability, safeguard its resources and territorial integrity, and protect Islamic holy sites." Sisi himself said earlier in the month that Egypt "will carry out any measure to protect Saudi Arabia."

Egypt, the Arab world's most populous state, has not undertaken a large-scale foreign ground deployment since the 1991 Gulf War, when it sent troops to help liberate Kuwait from Iraqi occupation. It joined the Saudi-led coalition in an earlier phase of Yemen's war in March 2015 mainly as a naval partner, sending warships to secure Red Sea shipping lanes while pledging air support. It also deployed around 800 ground troops but played only a limited combat role. The authorisation on Monday does not itself send troops, but it gives the president the legal authority to do so if Riyadh asks, and it is a significant step for a country that has been careful to avoid being drawn into Yemen's war.

The reason for Egypt's caution is history. Egypt's intervention in Yemen in the 1960s, when President Gamal Abdel Nasser sent tens of thousands of troops to support republican forces in the Yemeni civil war, became a costly quagmire that drained the Egyptian military and is still remembered as a warning against foreign entanglements. The current authorisation is deliberately framed as defensive, and the government has stressed that it will not involve ground troops in Yemen, precisely to avoid a repeat of that experience.

Saudi Arabia is a major political and financial partner of Cairo, providing billions of dollars in aid, deposits at the central bank and energy supplies. Egypt's economy depends heavily on Gulf support, and Saudi Arabia has repeatedly provided financial assistance at moments of crisis, including deposits to shore up Egypt's foreign currency reserves. The relationship is one of mutual dependence: Saudi Arabia values Egypt's military weight and its political support in the Arab world, while Egypt values Saudi money and energy.

Fighting between the Saudi-led coalition and the Houthi militia, which controls the capital Sanaa, has escalated in recent weeks, becoming a volatile front in the US-Israeli war on Iran, with Houthi attacks on Riyadh's international airport and the state oil giant Saudi Aramco disrupting flights and oil exports. The Houthis, who are aligned with Iran, have launched missiles and drones at Saudi Arabia for years, but the intensity of the attacks has increased as the regional war has widened, and the threat to the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the narrow waterway at the southern end of the Red Sea through which ships pass to reach the Suez Canal, is a direct concern for Egypt, which earns billions of dollars a year in canal revenues.

Egypt has the Arab world's largest military, boasting hundreds of thousands of active personnel, fighter jets and complex air defences sourced from countries including the United States, China, France and Russia. What it could offer Saudi Arabia in practice is air defence, fighter aircraft and naval support, rather than ground troops. The deployment of air defence systems to intercept Houthi missiles and drones would be a defensive mission, consistent with the government's assurances, but it would still place Egyptian forces in the line of fire and would make Egypt a party to a conflict it has so far managed to avoid.

The decision also has a wider significance. It shows how the US-Israeli war on Iran has drawn in countries across the region, and how the conflict in Yemen, which was once seen as a separate war, has become part of a larger regional confrontation. Egypt's authorisation does not mean Egyptian troops will fight in Yemen tomorrow, but it does mean that Cairo has taken a public step towards military involvement, and that the next Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia could bring Egyptian forces into action.
