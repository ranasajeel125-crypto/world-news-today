Yemen's Houthis Say They Attacked Saudi Oil Refinery and Riyadh Financial District

world · 25 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Ras Tanura is Saudi Arabia's largest refinery and a major crude export terminal on the Gulf coast. The Saudi-led coalition said air defences intercepted and destroyed four drones there on Wednesday; the Houthis say the refinery attack caused fires.

Yemen's Houthis said on Wednesday they had attacked Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura oil refinery and Riyadh's financial district, escalating a campaign that has now struck the kingdom's most symbolic economic targets twice in less than a week. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the refinery attack caused fires and that the group had attacked the financial district in three phases, in a claim that puts the war in Yemen back at the centre of Gulf security — and of global oil markets.

The Saudi-led coalition gave a different account of what happened over Ras Tanura. It said earlier on Wednesday that air defences had intercepted and destroyed four drones in Ras Tanura, in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, without saying whether any of them had reached their target. The two versions of events could not immediately be reconciled: the Houthis say their attack caused fires at the refinery, while the coalition reported only the interceptions, and no independent account of damage at the site was available.

Ras Tanura is no ordinary target. It is Saudi Arabia's largest refinery and part of a complex on the kingdom's Gulf coast that also serves as a major export terminal for Saudi crude, the artery through which much of the world's largest oil exporter's output reaches the sea. An attack that genuinely disrupted it would matter far beyond Saudi Arabia's borders, which is precisely why the claim — and the question of whether it succeeded — drew immediate attention from oil traders on Wednesday.

The financial district attack, by contrast, is already a matter of record. Saudi civil defence said 11 people were wounded on Tuesday in a strike on a tower in Riyadh's financial district, and the Houthis said the attack on the district took place on Tuesday, in three phases. It was the second strike on the same tower in less than a week, and the wounded included four Egyptians, three Nepalis, two Indians and two Saudis, according to Saudi civil defence figures reported by the Wall Street Journal.

The tower is the Public Investment Fund Tower, the tallest building in the Saudi capital, which stands 385 metres high over the King Abdullah Financial District and houses the headquarters of the kingdom's sovereign wealth fund. Saudi civil defence posted a photograph of the landmark showing damage to the building about halfway up its facade, similar to the impact of the first strike on the same tower on 19 November, which caused damage but no injuries. Reuters said it could not immediately determine when the image was taken or the extent of the further damage from the latest strike.

The Saudi-led coalition said later on Tuesday that the attack had been carried out with a drone, and warned that it would respond “decisively to the deliberate targeting of civilians and civilian property” by the Houthis. The language was a measure of how the campaign is being read in Riyadh: not as a distant border war, but as a direct assault on civilians and on the physical symbols of the kingdom's economic ambitions.

Those symbols have been hit repeatedly in the past three weeks. On Thursday 19 November, the PIF Tower was struck in a Houthi attack that, according to Saudi civil defence, caused material damage to several floors of the building and nearby buildings. The strike blew out windows across seven to eight floors and bent parts of the tower's metal facade. Three people familiar with the matter told Reuters the building had been struck by an incoming Houthi missile, while the coalition said it had dealt with a missile threat launched toward Riyadh and that an initial assessment pointed to falling debris.

The Houthis said on that day that they had targeted Riyadh's financial centre twice, first at dawn and again in the evening, and that they had also attacked King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, the Khamis Mushait airbase in southern Saudi Arabia and the SADAF petrochemical company in Jubail, in the kingdom's Eastern Province. It was the second time in less than two weeks that a Houthi attack had damaged a landmark in Riyadh's financial district: a building owned by state oil company Aramco in the same district had been hit in an attack on 8 November.

The district itself is the physical centre of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's drive to move the Saudi economy beyond oil. The Public Investment Fund, which the crown prince chairs, holds more than $900 billion in assets and is the main instrument of that diversification effort. The King Abdullah Financial District also houses the Saudi stock exchange and multinational firms including Goldman Sachs, which is why the repeated targeting of its towers carries a message that goes beyond the damage to any single building.

The campaign behind the strikes is a war that had been frozen and then reignited. Fighting in Yemen had been largely paused under a 2022 truce, but it flared again in July, as part of a wider regional conflict that began in February when the United States and Israel attacked Iran. The Houthis, who are backed by Iran, have used the renewed fighting to press attacks deep inside Saudi Arabia, reaching the capital's financial heart and the Gulf coast's oil infrastructure in the same week. Reuters has reported that Saudi officials now expect their campaign against the Houthis to run into 2027 before any talks.

The oil market's reaction on Wednesday was muted but watchful. Brent crude futures fell 43 cents, or 0.5%, to $90.97 a barrel, paring some of the previous day's gains, as concerns over supply disruptions in the Middle East were offset by rising supply and the prospect of a market surplus. But traders were also weighing fresh attacks on ships near the Strait of Hormuz and the exchanges of attacks between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis, which Reuters said had raised uncertainty over the sustainability of that trend. Attention is now turning to the OPEC+ meeting on 29 November, where ministers will decide next year's production quotas.

What remains unresolved is the central question of Wednesday's claim: what actually happened at Ras Tanura. The Houthis have not given a date for the refinery attack, and the coalition has not confirmed any fire or damage there, only the destruction of four drones. Until Saudi Aramco or the coalition says more, the refinery attack stands as a claim rather than a confirmed event — but even as a claim it signals that the Houthis are now openly targeting the kingdom's oil export infrastructure, not only its capital.

Why it matters: the strikes of the past week have brought the Yemen war to the two places Saudi Arabia can least afford to see hit — its financial district and its largest refinery. The PIF Tower has now been struck twice in six days, the second time wounding 11 people, and the Houthis are claiming to have set fires at the heart of the kingdom's oil exports. With Saudi officials expecting the campaign to run into 2027 and no talks in sight, the pattern of the past three weeks — a tower, an Aramco building, an airport, a petrochemical plant, and now a refinery — suggests the Gulf's biggest economy will remain within the Houthis' range for some time to come.
