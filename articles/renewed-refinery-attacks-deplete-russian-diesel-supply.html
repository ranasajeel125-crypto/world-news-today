Renewed Refinery Attacks Deplete Russian Diesel Supply as US Deal in Focus

world · 5 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Ryazan refinery, one of Russia's largest, appeared to have come under attack in an overnight drone strike, in what would be the second refinery hit this week.

Ukraine's renewed attacks on Russian oil refineries are adding to Moscow's difficulty in addressing domestic fuel shortages, even as it ships diesel to international markets under an export deal with US President Donald Trump, Reuters reported.

The Ryazan oil refinery, one of Russia's largest, appeared to have come under attack as part of a large drone strike overnight on industrial sites in the city, southeast of Moscow, according to a Reuters video analysis. Ryazan Governor Pavel Malkov said more than 200 Ukrainian drones had attacked the region overnight, and that falling debris caused fires at several industrial sites and damaged power lines, cutting electricity to parts of the city, with one person injured. Footage verified by the independent Russian outlet Astra showed a fire at the Ryazan refinery, which is owned by state energy company Rosneft and is among Russia's largest. If confirmed, it would be the second refinery to be hit this week, following an attack on the Yaroslavl plant, northeast of Moscow, overnight on Monday, marking the breakdown of a pause in strikes.

The Yaroslavl attack was the larger of the two. Air defences downed 124 drones over the Yaroslavl region overnight from Monday to Tuesday, according to Governor Mikhail Yevrayev, and refinery infrastructure was damaged, with two workers suffering shrapnel wounds and five or more big blazes reported at the site. The plant is run by Slavneft, which Rosneft and Gazprom control together, and ranks among the five biggest refineries in Russia, with capacity of 314,000 barrels per day. It completely halted production after the drone attack on 3 November. The Ryazan refinery, with annual capacity of 17 million metric tons, was targeted on 5 November and is now operating at just 10 per cent of capacity, processing 34,000 barrels per day out of a possible 342,000.

Ukraine's strikes on oil refineries this year have forced large amounts of processing capacity in western Russia offline, causing fuel shortages that meant Moscow had to enforce fuel export bans from April onwards. The pause in strikes was brief: Ukraine stopped hitting refineries for a few days in mid-October after President Donald Trump publicly asked it to, and after an earlier strike on Ryazan on 15 October knocked out a primary crude unit, reported attacks went quiet for about two weeks. They picked up again on 30 October, with hits since then on Rosneft's Novokuybyshevsk refinery, Bashneft's Novoil plant in Ufa, Lukoil's NORSI refinery and a Transneft pipeline station in Samara. By mid-November, fifteen major refineries had been disrupted or shut down, and industry sources cited by Reuters said combined crude oil refining volumes had fallen by an estimated 45 per cent since late October.

A ban on diesel exports has been lifted in part, and the US struck the deal in October with the Kremlin to resume diesel exports to international markets, when Trump was seeking to calm diesel prices ahead of the midterm elections on November 3, which his Republican Party lost. Trump announced the agreement on 9 October, after a call with President Vladimir Putin, saying Russia would make more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel available immediately to the US and global markets, with another 500,000 tonnes in November and one million tonnes immediately thereafter. The deal included suspending sanctions on Russian diesel exports for about six months, with a temporary US Treasury licence allowing Russian diesel to be sold, delivered, unloaded and imported until 7 April. Trump also waived parts of a new US sanctions law against Russia and threatened Europe with a US diesel export ban unless countries released more from their emergency reserves.

At least six Russian diesel cargoes have broadcast that they plan transatlantic voyages, shipping data from Kpler and LSEG show, while two cargoes appeared to have changed course after being previously bound for the US Atlantic coast. No Russian diesel cargo has arrived in the US since the deal, the data show. The first observed US-bound Russian shipment is expected to arrive in New York this weekend, but regulatory, legislative and sanctions concerns mean that its discharge is not guaranteed, Kpler analyst Matt Smith said. The Malta-flagged tanker Agioi Fanendes loaded about 40,000 metric tons of ultra-low-sulphur diesel, equivalent to roughly 300,000 barrels, at Russia's Primorsk port on 22 October, and updated its destination to New York before changing it to "for orders", a designation typically indicating a vessel has no confirmed final discharge port. The cargo would be the first imported by the US since former President Joe Biden announced a ban on US imports of Russian oil and petroleum products in 2022, but analysts note it is equivalent to roughly two hours of US distillate consumption.

Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev said Russian diesel cargoes destined for the US are continuing on course, in a post on X. There is no connection between diesel tankers changing course and the result of the US midterm elections, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov added. But the ships kept turning away: the Russian tanker Talisman and the Sierra Leone-flagged Leruo left Primorsk with the US East Coast as their declared destination, and one was later heading towards Brazil and the other into the Mediterranean. Russia's diesel exports doubled in October, to an average of 271,000 barrels a day from 135,000 in September, according to Kpler, but the fuel went to Brazil, Turkey and other markets, not the United States.
