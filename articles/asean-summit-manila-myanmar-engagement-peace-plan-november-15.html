ASEAN Summit Opens in Manila as Leaders Stick With Myanmar Peace Plan

world · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr, chairing the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila, said the bloc would pursue “constructive and principled engagement” with Myanmar.

Southeast Asian leaders opened their annual summit in Manila on Sunday and agreed to keep engaging with Myanmar while holding to the bloc’s five-point peace plan, Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr said, putting the crisis in the war-torn country at the top of the agenda on the first day of talks.

Marcos, chairing the 49th summit of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, said the 11-member bloc had agreed to pursue “constructive and principled engagement” with Myanmar while adhering to the five-point plan, the framework ASEAN adopted after the 2021 military coup. He said he hoped the bloc could advance the implementation of the plan, and urged leaders to ask “what more can we do for Myanmar” as the crisis continued to displace families, disrupt communities and affect the country’s youth.

The decision came as the summit faced growing pressure to ease a five-year ban on Myanmar’s military-backed leaders attending high-level meetings. Thailand and several other members have stepped up engagement with the army-backed government despite the continuing civil war, and Myanmar’s rulers want to normalise ties after being barred from summits in 2021 for failing to de-escalate the conflict that followed the coup.

But there was no softening of the bloc’s formal position, at least not yet. ASEAN’s special envoy on Myanmar, Philippine Foreign Affairs Secretary Theresa Lazaro, told reporters on Saturday there would be “no easing” of the approach, although a review of the five-point plan and proposals about Myanmar’s participation remained under discussion and would ultimately be decided by the leaders. Thailand’s call to allow Myanmar’s foreign minister to take part in meetings of ASEAN foreign ministers also remained on the table, with no decision made.

Myanmar has been in turmoil since the military ousted the elected government of Nobel Peace Prize winner Aung San Suu Kyi in February 2021, triggering a civil war that the United Nations estimates has killed around 100,000 people and displaced more than 3.5 million. The five-point consensus, agreed by ASEAN leaders in April 2021, calls for an immediate end to violence, constructive dialogue among all parties, mediation by an ASEAN chair-appointed special envoy, humanitarian assistance, and access for the envoy to meet the parties involved. More than five years on, its implementation has remained limited as the fighting and displacement continue, and Myanmar’s military leadership has stayed barred from the leaders’ table, represented instead by a senior foreign ministry official.

The junta’s position has nevertheless been strengthening. Coup leader-turned-president Min Aung Hlaing’s military has received support from China, India and Russia, Reuters reported, and his administration has gained increasing international recognition in recent months, including secret talks with US officials as the Trump administration reverses years of isolation. That drift is part of why the question of readmission has become so difficult for ASEAN: the bloc’s exclusion of Myanmar’s leaders was meant to pressure them toward the peace plan, but engagement by individual members — and now by outside powers — has steadily eroded its leverage.

The summit itself is the Philippines’ turn in the rotating chair, held under the theme “Navigating Our Future, Together”. The 49th ASEAN Summit runs from Sunday to Tuesday at the Philippine International Convention Center in Manila, with a plenary session and a leaders’ retreat followed by meetings with external partners. Philippine officials said at least 19 world and institutional leaders would take part across the summit and its related meetings, with dialogue partners including Australia, China, India, Japan, South Korea, New Zealand, Russia, the United Nations and the United States.

Beyond Myanmar, the agenda is crowded. Leaders are grappling with an energy crisis triggered by the Middle East war and the disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, tensions in the South China Sea, and cross-border haze pollution from forest and land fires. Drafts of the post-summit communiques seen by the Associated Press call for peaceful resolutions to the region’s conflicts, including the South China Sea disputes and the wars in the Middle East and Ukraine, though no breakthroughs are expected. Diplomats say progress has been made toward a code of conduct for the South China Sea, but the agreement is not expected to be finalised before the end of the year.

The week’s biggest diplomatic moment comes on Tuesday, the summit’s final day, when US President Donald Trump is due to arrive in Manila for talks with ASEAN leaders and a meeting of the Quad security grouping — the United States, Japan, Australia and India. It would be the first Quad leaders’ meeting in two years, and the first since Trump returned to office, according to two people familiar with the planning cited by BusinessWorld. The supply of critical minerals is expected to be on the Quad agenda, one of them said. Chinese Premier Li Qiang is due to meet ASEAN leaders on Monday and leave before Trump arrives, while the Philippines hands the rotating chairmanship to Singapore on Tuesday.

What happens next will be decided in the remaining two days. Leaders are expected to continue their discussions through Tuesday, when a leaders’ document on Myanmar is due to be published and the review of the five-point plan presented. For now, the bloc has chosen continuity over change: engagement without readmission, and a peace plan it has not yet managed to make work.

Why it matters: ASEAN’s handling of Myanmar has become the sharpest test of the bloc’s credibility. Five years after it agreed a plan to end the violence, the war grinds on, the human cost keeps rising, and the bloc itself is divided between members pressing for engagement and those insisting the military leadership stay excluded until the fighting stops. Meanwhile the junta is gaining recognition abroad, including from Washington, which weakens the one lever ASEAN had. How Manila ends on Tuesday will signal whether the bloc can still act together — or whether its most difficult member is drifting beyond its reach. And with Trump and Li Qiang passing through the same city days apart, a Quad summit on Tuesday night and the South China Sea code still unfinished, the gathering is also a measure of whether Southeast Asia can keep its own agenda at the centre of a region where the great powers are competing harder than ever.
