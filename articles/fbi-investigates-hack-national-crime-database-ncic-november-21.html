Hackers Breach FBI's National Crime Database in Second Cyberattack in Months

world · 22 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The FBI is investigating an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center, the national database that feeds warrant and criminal-history records to police patrol cars across the United States; the scope of the intrusion is still unclear.

The FBI is investigating an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center, the national database that American police rely on for warrants, fugitives and background checks, a person familiar with the matter told the Associated Press on Saturday. The bureau declined to comment on the incident, which was first reported by CNN on Friday and confirmed to the AP by a person familiar with the matter who was not authorized to speak by name. The scope of the problem was not immediately clear.

The National Crime Information Center, known as the NCIC, is a computerized database of criminal justice information available to local, state and federal law enforcement agencies. It holds records on fugitives, missing persons, criminal history data, stolen property and other topics. It is, in the words of Fox News, a critical information-sharing tool for federal, state and local authorities, helping them track fugitives, monitor registered sex offenders and check whether someone has failed a gun background check, among many other things.

Three sources familiar with the matter told Fox News that hackers had breached the NCIC, marking the second cyberattack against the bureau in months. The FBI is investigating the hack to determine the unauthorized entry point, but for now it is unclear what information was compromised, the sources said. The intrusion is believed to have been discovered in recent days, and investigators have not publicly identified the attackers or said whether any records were stolen, altered or deleted.

President Donald Trump was asked about the incident on Saturday. In an interview with Fox News Channel on Saturday morning, he said he had only just been told about it. “We just heard about it, and I will say that it’s not good,” Trump said, adding: “I don’t like that.” The FBI did not immediately respond to requests for comment from either news organization.

The danger in this breach is not only what may have been taken, but what may have been changed. Jason Pack, a former FBI supervisory special agent, warned on Saturday that the bureau’s incident response team must quickly determine whether data was manipulated inside the system, which feeds criminal records to patrol cars nationwide. A corrupted warrant record or an altered criminal history could endanger police officers during traffic stops, he said, if officers act on information that is no longer true. A former FBI official put the point bluntly: corrupted records could be as dangerous as exposed ones.

There is, for now, no indication that any records were altered or that the system is experiencing an outage, Pack said. But the warning underlines why the NCIC is different from an ordinary government database. It is the connective tissue of American policing: the check an officer runs in seconds before approaching a car, the lookup that tells a department whether a person standing in front of them is wanted, missing, or barred from owning a gun. If officers cannot trust the answers it gives, the system itself becomes a risk.

What the database holds makes the breach sensitive in a different way. While it does not contain classified intelligence or sensitive FBI investigative case files, Pack said, it stores personally identifiable information, including names, dates of birth and Social Security numbers. The NCIC has more than 100,000 users across the country, and investigators will examine IP logs and metadata to determine whether the attackers exploited a compromised password, breached a state terminal, or infiltrated the system through a third-party vendor.

The breach is the second known security incident involving an FBI system in roughly two months. In September, a criminal hacking group known as ShinyHunters revealed that it had obtained a massive trove of data on FBI employees and applicants and had compromised the bureau’s jobs portal. The group claimed it stole sensitive information belonging to almost every FBI agent, including names, home addresses, Social Security numbers, assignments and, in some cases, the names of family members.

The bureau struck back at that network in October. Canadian cybersecurity executive Edward Dubrovsky was arrested on Oct. 8 and faces federal extortion conspiracy charges in connection with the FBI’s investigation into ShinyHunters, according to court records and law enforcement sources that spoke to Reuters. FBI Director Kash Patel announced the arrest of the ShinyHunters “suspected co-conspirator” on Oct. 9, a day after Dubrovsky was taken into custody. The details of Dubrovsky’s alleged crimes are unknown, as the court records in the case remain sealed.

The bureau has been a frequent target for hackers, and the pattern stretches back further than the last two months. In March, the FBI disclosed that it was investigating “suspicious activities” on an internal system that contains sensitive information related to surveillance operations and investigations. And in 2023, hackers infiltrated a computer network at the FBI’s New York field office used in child sexual exploitation cases — a system that also stored images tied to the Jeffrey Epstein probe, CNN previously reported.

The NCIC incident also arrives in the middle of a strange run of digital failures across the United States. The country has encountered a series of outages in government and the private sector in recent weeks, affecting Microsoft, several major US banks, air traffic control systems and the National Security Agency. Trump administration officials have said those outages were caused by routine efforts to upgrade computer systems and fix vulnerabilities, rather than a cyberattack, and there was no immediate indication that the FBI incident was related to them.

The bureau itself has been warning others about the speed of modern attacks. On Nov. 13 it issued an advisory about ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing framework that malicious actors have repurposed with large language model-powered agents to automate reconnaissance, exploitation and data extraction. The advisory said some attacks moved from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours — a measure of how fast an intruder can move once inside a system like the NCIC.

FBI Director Kash Patel used a Fox News appearance on Thursday to tout the bureau’s offensive posture, pointing to an initiative called Operation Winter Shield, without addressing the breach. The timing was awkward: the director was promoting the FBI’s ability to go on the attack in cyberspace on the same day his own agency’s most widely used database was found to have been breached.

What happens next depends on what the investigation finds, and on how much of it the FBI is willing to say. The bureau must establish how the intruders got in, whether they merely viewed data or gained deeper control of the system, and whether any records were copied, altered or deleted. It must also decide whether the more than 100,000 officers and agencies that use the NCIC every day need to treat its answers with caution until the system is verified clean. None of those questions had an answer by Saturday, and the bureau’s silence — it declined to comment — left the most basic facts of the incident to anonymous sources and the president’s brief televised reaction.

Why it matters: the NCIC is not a back-office system. It is the database that sits behind almost every police encounter in the United States, from a routine traffic stop to a fugitive arrest to a gun background check. A breach that only exposed personal data would be serious enough; a breach that altered records would be worse, because it would turn the tool officers trust into one they cannot. The FBI has now been hit twice in two months, and each time the public has learned about it from reporters rather than from the bureau itself. Until the FBI says what happened, how far it went, and who did it, every agency that queries the NCIC is working with a database whose integrity is, for the moment, an open question.
