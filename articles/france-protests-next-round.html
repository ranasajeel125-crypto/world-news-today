French Protest Groups Call for More Rallies as Macron Faces Tight Choices

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

Protesters march in France during Saturday's nationwide rallies, which drew 410,000 people according to the Interior Ministry.

French protest groups and unions called on Sunday for further demonstrations as they sought to maintain pressure on the government, after a huge security operation helped to contain unrest during nationwide rallies on Saturday.

The Interior Ministry said 410,000 people took part in Saturday's marches as authorities deployed 70,000 police, helping to avert a repeat of the looting and vandalism that swept many cities on 17 October. Some 1,314 people were arrested on Saturday, half of last week's numbers, and about 50 police were injured, compared with more than 1,000 last week.

Labour leaders urged further protests next Saturday and a nationwide strike on 5 November, saying frustration over wages, living costs and public services was spreading far beyond the student movement that first mobilised last month. “We see an aggregation of anger,” Murielle Guilbert, co-leader of the left-wing Solidaires trade union federation, told BFM TV, adding: “We call on people to take to the streets next Saturday.” The SUD Education teaching union said it would join the 31 October demonstration, while the large CGT union said it would decide during the week. The Yellow Vests, the grassroots movement that emerged in 2018 as a revolt against fuel taxes, also called for mass rallies next Saturday, including sit-ins outside town halls and the National Assembly.

President Emmanuel Macron's scope to respond is minimal. In 2019 he was able to find the resources to reduce fuel costs in response to the Yellow Vests; seven years on, strained public finances leave him with little room to meet protesters' demands. His mandate also expires next April and his centrist party no longer has the majority needed to pass major legislation. The government has begun talks with unions and student representatives, but protest leaders say the measures proposed so far are inadequate. “We have received no response at all from this government,” said Guilbert. Police unions said on Sunday they did not have enough staff to maintain for any length of time the high level of deployment that on Saturday helped to limit disorder to sporadic clashes in a number of cities, including Paris and Marseille.

The protests have resonated beyond France's borders. On Saturday, Belgian police used tear gas and pepper spray as they clashed in Brussels with anti-government demonstrators. In Albania, peaceful protests that began at the end of May turned into violent unrest, and skirmishes have taken place in several Italian cities for a second week running.

Saturday, October 24, was the second weekend of protests, and the contrast with the first was the story of the day. All those who had predicted, hoped for or feared a repeat of the violence that broke out on the fringes of the October 17 protests, accompanied by the looting that shocked the country, found themselves facing a different scenario. In the end, none of that happened on the same scale: the marches drew large crowds where they had been authorised, and the turnout was only slightly lower than the numbers reported the previous week.

The scale of participation remained contested. According to the Interior Ministry, 410,000 people took part in Saturday's demonstrations across France, including 110,000 in Paris, in 298 demonstrations across the country. Trade unions estimated turnout at approximately 800,000, with the CGT union putting the figure at 800,000 people, including 250,000 in the capital. In Paris, an agreement between the unions and the police prefecture led to the creation of a demonstration route running from Place de la Bastille to Place de la Nation.

The security operation was the largest of its kind in years. Authorities deployed 70,000 police and other security personnel, and Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the increased security presence had helped contain the unrest. French authorities arrested 1,314 people on Saturday, while 53 security personnel were injured. The figures marked a significant decrease compared with the first day of mobilisation on October 17, when 2,628 people were arrested and 1,144 members of the security forces were injured. Police responded to 75 fires during Saturday's demonstrations, while 24 shops were looted. In Paris, riot police charged into a group of protesters at Sciences Po, pushing demonstrators against a railing and knocking a man carrying a megaphone to the ground.

The movement began last month when students demanded improvements to overcrowded classrooms, deteriorating school buildings and teacher shortages. It has since expanded into broader demonstrations against economic inequality, rising prices and President Emmanuel Macron's government, with frustration over wages, living costs and public services spreading far beyond the student movement that first mobilised. Hard-left leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon warned that protests could continue unless Macron addressed public grievances. Protesters carried signs competing in sharp-tongued humour aimed at the president, and one union leader said: "We are protesting under restricted conditions. Yesterday, Emmanuel Macron said that he would allow the union marches to take place and then, at the last minute, the route was changed, in total contradiction with what he said."

The political pressure on Macron is acute. In 2019 he was able to find the resources to reduce fuel costs in response to the Yellow Vests; seven years on, strained public finances leave him with little room to meet protesters' demands. His mandate expires in April 2027, and his centrist party no longer has the majority needed to pass major legislation. The unrest comes as Macron approaches the end of his presidency, with France preparing for elections next year. The government has begun talks with unions and student representatives, but protest leaders say the measures proposed so far are inadequate. Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin asked judges to systematically sentence arrested protesters to the loss of their civil rights and therefore their right to vote — a proposal one protester called outrageous, saying: "The right to protest is just as important as the right to vote."

Labour leaders urged further protests next Saturday, October 31, and a nationwide strike on November 5. The SUD Education teaching union said it would join the October 31 demonstration, while the large CGT union said it would decide during the week. The Yellow Vests also called for mass rallies next Saturday, including sit-ins outside town halls and the National Assembly. Police unions warned they did not have enough staff to maintain for any length of time the high level of deployment that on Saturday had helped to limit disorder to sporadic clashes in Paris, Marseille and other cities. Whether the movement can sustain its momentum into a third weekend — and whether the government can find an answer — will decide whether the October protests become a lasting crisis for a president with six months left in office.
