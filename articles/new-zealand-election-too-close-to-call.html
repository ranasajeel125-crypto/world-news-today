New Zealand Election Ends in Cliffhanger as Luxon Says Result Is 'Too Close to Call'

world · 7 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Prime Minister Christopher Luxon said National had a pathway to forming the next government but the result was too close to call; Labour's Chris Hipkins said it may take longer to know what voters have said, with the official result due on 27 November.

New Zealand’s general election ended in a cliffhanger on Saturday, with more than 95% of ballots counted and no clear winner between the governing centre-right National Party and the opposition Labour Party, the country’s Electoral Commission said.

Both major parties were polling just short of 30% of the popular vote, separated by only a few thousand votes out of nearly 2.4 million counted, Reuters reported. Labour was narrowly ahead in the party vote with 710,330 votes, or 29.36 per cent, against National’s 706,680 votes, or 29.21 per cent — a gap of fewer than 4,000 votes. National remained the largest party in parliament on seats, but the overall balance was too tight for either side to claim victory.

A projection from New Zealand’s Electoral Commission showed the current three-party ruling coalition — National and its partners, libertarian ACT and populist New Zealand First — would hold 67 seats, one more than the 66 needed for a majority in a 131-seat parliament, though other tallies put the coalition at 66. National was on course for 42 seats, Labour 36, the Greens 16, ACT 13, New Zealand First 12, the centrist Opportunity Party 7 and Te Pāti Māori 5. The Greens recorded their best result, on about 13 per cent of the vote, and ACT was on a record high of about 11 per cent. The left bloc of Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori was projected to hold 57 seats.

The 131-seat parliament is unusually large: New Zealand’s parliament normally has 120 seats, but National won seven more electorate seats than its party vote entitled it to, and Te Pāti Māori won four extra, creating the largest overhang in the history of the country’s mixed-member proportional (MMP) system, introduced in 1996.

“It is heartening to see that on the numbers so far, National is once again the largest party in parliament, and we have a pathway to forming the next government,” Prime Minister Christopher Luxon told supporters in Auckland. “But I have to tell you right now, it is too close to call.” Labour leader Chris Hipkins delivered a similar message in Upper Hutt, northeast of the capital Wellington. “Tonight, New Zealand has spoken. It may take us a little bit longer to figure out exactly what it is that you’ve said,” he told supporters, without conceding defeat. “Every vote matters and every vote must now be counted.”

The centrist Opportunity Party, led by political newcomer Qiulae Wong, crossed the 5% threshold needed to enter parliament and was projected to win about seven seats, potentially making it a kingmaker. The party won 5.96 per cent of the party vote, or 144,298 votes, becoming the first new party to be elected to parliament since 2011. Wong, a 38-year-old business consultant, has said she could work with either side to form a government, and her pledges of evidence-based centrism and an end to partisan bickering won support from voters weary of both major parties. Luxon had ruled out working with Opportunity before the vote, leaving the path to a new government uncertain, though he struck a more conciliatory tone afterwards, saying he wanted to build a relationship with Wong and find common ground.

The result may not be known for weeks. Between 300,000 and 700,000 special votes — including ballots cast overseas, outside a voter’s home electorate, or by people who enrolled late — will not be counted until the official result is released on 27 November. The Electoral Commission estimates about 399,365 special votes remain to be processed, around 14 per cent of all votes cast, including 96,947 from overseas. Special votes have historically skewed to the left: across the 2017 to 2023 elections they went more to Te Pāti Māori, Opportunity and the Greens, and less to New Zealand First, National and ACT, than their election-night share. Detailed results, including list seat allocation and special vote statistics, are expected on 14 December.

The night was a twin-edged sword for National. The party won more electorate seats than expected, including Tamaki, Nelson and Hamilton East, which increased its overall numbers, but no members from its party list made it into parliament, and several of its highest-profile MPs ended their careers. Deputy leader Nicola Willis and other top ministers — Chris Bishop, Paul Goldsmith and Gerry Brownlee — are all out of parliament.

The six-week campaign was dominated by the cost of living and a struggling economy, with voters showing little enthusiasm for either major party. Voting began on 26 October, with nearly 3.8 million people eligible to vote, and early voting surged to a record, with more than 900,000 ballots cast in the first week alone. The Treaty of Waitangi, New Zealand’s founding document, was another major campaign issue, particularly among Māori voters. So unclear was the result that on Sunday, “who won the election 2026” was the top trending Google search in New Zealand.

New Zealand’s electoral system makes coalitions the norm, and the tightness of the 2026 race means one or more of the country’s smaller parties will almost certainly participate in a governing coalition. The result raises the prospect of a four-party government for the first time since MMP was introduced in 1996. Until the official result is declared, Luxon’s government will continue in caretaker mode, and the parties will not begin negotiations to form a government until the final tallies are released on 27 November.
