New Zealand Election Ends in Cliffhanger as Luxon Says Result Is 'Too Close to Call'

world · 7 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Prime Minister Christopher Luxon said National had a pathway to forming the next government but the result was too close to call; Labour's Chris Hipkins said it may take longer to know what voters have said, with the official result due on 27 November.

New Zealand's general election ended in a cliffhanger on Saturday, with more than 95% of ballots counted and no clear winner between the governing centre-right National Party and the opposition Labour Party, the country's Electoral Commission said.

Both major parties were polling just short of 30% of the popular vote, separated by only a few thousand votes out of nearly 2.4 million counted, Reuters reported. National remained the largest party in parliament on seats, but the overall balance was too tight for either side to claim victory.

"It is heartening to see that on the numbers so far, National is once again the largest party in parliament, and we have a pathway to forming the next government," Prime Minister Christopher Luxon told supporters in Auckland. "But I have to tell you right now, it is too close to call."

Labour leader Chris Hipkins delivered a similar message in Upper Hutt, northeast of the capital Wellington. "Tonight, New Zealand has spoken. It may take us a little bit longer to figure out exactly what it is that you've said," he told supporters, without conceding defeat.

A projection from New Zealand's Electoral Commission showed the current three-party ruling coalition — National and its partners, libertarian ACT and populist New Zealand First — would hold 66 seats, one more than the 65 needed for a majority in parliament, though other tallies put the coalition at 67. ACT was on a record high of about 11% of the vote, and the Greens recorded their best result, on about 13%.

The centrist Opportunity Party, led by political newcomer Qiulae Wong, crossed the 5% threshold needed to enter parliament and was projected to win about seven seats, potentially making it a kingmaker. Luxon had ruled out working with Opportunity before the vote, leaving the path to a new government uncertain.

The result may not be known for weeks. Between 300,000 and 700,000 special votes — including ballots cast overseas, outside a voter's home electorate, or by people who enrolled late — will not be counted until the official result is released on 27 November. Special votes have historically skewed to the left.

The six-week campaign was dominated by the cost of living and a struggling economy, with voters showing little enthusiasm for either major party. Voting began on 26 October, with nearly 3.8 million people eligible to vote, and early voting surged to a record, with more than 900,000 ballots cast in the first week alone.
