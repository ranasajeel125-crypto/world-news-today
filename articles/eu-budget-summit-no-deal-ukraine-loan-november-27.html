EU Summit Ends Without a Budget Deal as Ukraine's €45 Billion Loan Gets Caught in the Crossfire

politics · 27 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, left, and European Council President António Costa at the EU summit in Brussels, which ended early on Friday without agreement on the budget or the Ukraine loan.

European Union leaders have ended a special summit in Brussels without agreeing on the bloc's next seven-year budget or on a new €45 billion loan for Ukraine, leaving both decisions — and Kyiv's funding for 2027 — unresolved until a further summit in December.

The gathering, convened by European Council President António Costa on Thursday, had been scheduled to run for two days. Instead it ended in acrimony in the early hours of Friday, with the 27 leaders no closer to a deal on how much the Union should spend between 2028 and 2034, or on who should pay for Ukraine's next loan.

The budget fight has been running for months. The European Commission proposed a budget of nearly €2 trillion in July, and Costa went into the summit with a compromise that cut €204 billion from that figure, bringing the total to about €1.78 trillion. It was not enough. The so-called frugal nations — including the Nordic countries, Germany, the Netherlands, Austria and Belgium — argued that the cuts did not go far enough and failed to address spending discipline and the burden of financing the budget. On the other side, the “friends of cohesion”, led by countries such as Poland, want a bigger budget to bolster the bloc's collective security and help the EU compete economically with the United States and China.

“A cheap Europe could turn out to be a very expensive mistake,” Poland's Prime Minister Donald Tusk wrote in a social media post on Thursday. “Security comes at a price. As simple as that.”

Caught in the middle of that argument is the €45 billion loan for Ukraine. The European Commission proposed it on 18 November to help cover Kyiv's financing needs in 2027: up to €25 billion for military support and up to €20 billion to keep the Ukrainian state running. It would come on top of the €90 billion EU loan already agreed for 2026 and 2027, and would cover roughly two-thirds of Ukraine's estimated €70 billion funding gap for next year. The money would be raised by the EU on the capital markets through bond issuance, backed by the headroom in the EU budget.

Twenty-four of the 27 member states backed the loan at the summit. But three — Czechia, Hungary and Slovakia — stayed outside the consensus, and Bulgaria said it would take part only in financing the non-military part of the package. Because the loan has not been formally approved, the leaders agreed only to return to the issue in December.

Germany has now tied the two questions together. Chancellor Friedrich Merz has said he will not back the seven-year budget unless every member state signs up to the Ukraine loan, a position that has deepened the divisions. In practice, that makes the loan a hostage of the budget fight: a measure that could in principle be agreed on its own is now part of one large package, and neither part can move until both do.

Speaking to reporters after the summit, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said progress had been made on some issues, including a digital services tax, but urged greater action to reach a deal by the end of the year. “Today's conversations were useful. But there is still a lot of work ahead of us,” she said. “Now, all the pieces of the puzzle must be put together. This will be a challenge. But we all know we need an agreement.”

The next attempt comes at the regular European Council summit on 17 and 18 December, which leaders described as the last chance to settle both questions before the end of the year. But even that deadline is now contested. French President Emmanuel Macron has said he will not relaunch budget talks after 31 December if no agreement is reached, warning that the timing would then run into France's presidential election in April 2027. Marine Le Pen, the far-right candidate and current frontrunner in that race, has accused the Commission of trying to get the budget agreed before French voters go to the polls. Macron rejected that firmly: “It is a strange kind of arrogance to think that she is already in charge of France's destiny because, until proven otherwise, she has not been elected, and yours truly is elected until May 13, 2027.”

The budget also needs the consent of the European Parliament, where at least 361 of the 720 members must back it, as well as the unanimous agreement of all 27 governments — a high bar that makes the December summit a genuine deadline rather than a formality. A run of big elections across Europe in 2027, starting with Spain's general election on Sunday, adds to the pressure: if no deal is reached by the end of this year, the next realistic window for serious talks may not open until late 2027.

Ukraine, meanwhile, is left waiting. The debate in Brussels unfolded as President Volodymyr Zelensky gave an interview to the BBC in Kyiv on Friday, in which he criticised the recent interaction between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin as “strange”. The two leaders met at a summit in China last week, where Trump was heard calling Putin a “friend”. Zelensky was blunt: Putin is “an aggressor and a killer”. “He can't be a friend. Just cannot be.”

Trump said on social media that during that conversation he told the Russian leader to “pull back all military aggression against territories throughout Europe, in the hopes that a deal can be made between Russia and Ukraine”. But Zelensky also accused Washington of not paying enough attention to the conflict and of delaying the delivery of the Patriot air-defence missiles it promised. “The deficit of these missiles, that is people's lives,” he said.

For Ukraine, the stakes of the Brussels deadlock are immediate and practical. Russia's attacks on energy and civilian sites, and the disruption of Black Sea trade, are straining Kyiv's finances, and the €45 billion loan was meant to cover 2027. Every month of delay narrows the options for a country already fighting a war while its economy runs on foreign support. The loan is not the only money at issue — the draft seven-year budget also carries further support for Ukraine — but it is the most urgent, because it is the money Kyiv needs next year.

Some analysts believe the urgency will eventually force a deal. Jacob Funk Kirkegaard, a senior fellow at the Brussels-based think tank Bruegel, pointed out that the frugal nations are also among Ukraine's biggest supporters, and that supporting Kyiv on their own would cost them far more than sharing the burden through the EU budget. “In general, as the frugals know, it will be far more expensive for them to support Ukraine on their own. In the end, they will agree to a budget compromise,” he said. “As such, the needs of Ukraine, which due to the war and upcoming European elections, cannot be postponed, will help seal a deal in mid-December.”

Why it matters: the European Union's most important spending decisions are now hostage to a single disagreement, and the country that pays the price for the delay is not in the room. Twenty-four governments backed the €45 billion loan for Ukraine, yet it remains unapproved because three would not join and the budget that carries it is itself unsettled. Leaders have three weeks to find a compromise before the December summit — and if they fail, Ukraine's 2027 funding and the EU's seven-year budget both slip into an election year in which agreement will only get harder.
