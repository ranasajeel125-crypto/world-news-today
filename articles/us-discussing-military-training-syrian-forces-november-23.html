US Discussing First Military Training Mission With Syrian Forces Since Assad's Fall

world · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Syrian Defence Minister Murhaf Abu Qasra met the US operation's commander in Damascus on 12 November, as Washington and Damascus discuss a multinational training mission focused on demining.

The United States is discussing plans to train Syrian soldiers in demining, in what would be the first American training mission with the Syrian military since former rebel fighters took power in Damascus in 2024. Five people familiar with the proposal described it to Reuters, and four of them said the initial programme would emphasise training in demining and the safe disposal of ordnance — but could serve as a model for wider cooperation if it succeeds.

The proposal still faces legal hurdles in the United States and scepticism among some in the Syrian military, the sources said. But if it moves ahead, it could see the US return a small number of troops to Syria as soon as next year, just a year after withdrawing a decade-old mission to fight Islamic State.

The framework under discussion would build on a body known as the Damascus Coordination Center, set up last year in Jordan to facilitate cooperation between Syrian authorities, American forces and regional allies. Captain Tim Hawkins, a spokesperson for the US military's Central Command, said the centre was continuing its work in support of a stable, unified and peaceful Syria, and that full decisions had not yet been taken on its future structure and mission focus. A spokesperson for the US State Department said Washington welcomed having a partner in Damascus committed to combating Islamic State. The Syrian Defence Ministry did not respond to a Reuters request for comment.

The framework would also involve personnel from about 15 US allies, including Britain, France, Germany, Australia, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, according to a person involved in the discussions. One option under consideration would involve training Syrian soldiers in both Jordan and Syria, potentially beginning in March, with limited US military participation. The arrangement could also involve the Syrian National Mine Action Centre, a government body responsible for coordinating mine clearance, as a way of facilitating cooperation. The centre did not respond to a request for comment.

The clearest sign of how far the discussions have come was a meeting in Damascus on 12 November. Syrian Defence Minister Murhaf Abu Qasra met the commander of the US-led operation against Islamic State, Rear Admiral Liam Hulin, according to Syrian state news agency SANA. A Syrian military source said the meeting included discussions about the proposed multinational coordination mechanism, plans to train Syrian forces in demining and explosive ordnance disposal, as well as unspecified assistance with border security.

The proposed mission would be a further step in a rapprochement between Washington and Damascus since the overthrow of President Bashar al-Assad in December 2024. Washington lifted broad economic sanctions on Syria in December 2025, removed its designation of the country as a state sponsor of terrorism in August 2026, and eased arms-export and defence services restrictions in October, allowing licences to be considered case by case. Each step has moved the relationship from open hostility toward something closer to normal state-to-state dealings — and direct military cooperation would be the most sensitive step yet.

But there are still hurdles on both sides. In the United States, legislation known as the Leahy Laws prohibits US assistance to foreign security forces' units when there is credible information that they have committed gross violations of human rights. American officials would need to vet prospective Syrian recipients before providing funding, two of the sources said. The human rights concerns stem in part from the backgrounds of some commanders in Syria's new security forces, including former members of Islamist armed groups. United Nations investigators have also documented abuses by forces affiliated with the new authorities.

On the Syrian side, some in Syria's defence ministry have been reluctant to grant American military personnel access to its institutions and facilities, complicating efforts to establish training programmes, the sources said. That reluctance is understandable from Damascus's point of view: the new government is barely two years old, its armed forces are still being stitched together from the rebel factions that overthrew Assad, and opening its barracks and bases to the military of a country that was, until very recently, its adversary is a political risk as much as a practical one.

The history matters here, because the Americans and the Syrians have never actually worked together. During Syria's 2011–2024 civil war, hundreds of US troops were deployed in the country without cooperating either with Assad's government or with the main Sunni Muslim rebel forces that ultimately toppled him. The Americans mainly worked alongside Kurdish forces under Operation Inherent Resolve, the mission to battle Islamic State in both Syria and Iraq. The last US troops were withdrawn from Syria in April this year and from Iraq in September, although the wider operation has not been fully wound down.

That history is also why demining is the chosen starting point. It is the least politically charged form of military cooperation available: clearing mines and unexploded ordnance is humanitarian work as much as it is military work, and Syria's soil is littered with the remnants of thirteen years of war. A training mission that begins with demining lets both sides test whether they can work together at all, without either side having to commit to anything larger. If it works, the sources said, it could become a model for wider cooperation; if it does not, the damage is limited.

The money question is still open. US officials are exploring whether remaining funding from Operation Inherent Resolve could support the programme, two of the sources said. One estimated the available funds at approximately $200 million, though Reuters could not independently verify the amount. That the funding would come from the anti-Islamic State mission's own budget is itself a signal: Washington is framing the training as part of the same fight, not as a new and separate entanglement.

What happens next will turn first on the vetting. The Leahy Laws are not a formality: they require a genuine check of each unit that would receive American help, and the backgrounds of some Syrian commanders are precisely the kind of record the law was written to catch. Whether the administration can satisfy its own lawyers — and Congress, which watches these decisions closely — will decide whether the proposal survives in Washington. On the Syrian side, the question is whether the defence ministry's sceptics can be brought along, and whether Damascus is willing to accept the access and oversight that come with American training.

Why it matters: if the proposal goes ahead, it would be the first time the United States has trained the Syrian military, and the first time American troops have returned to Syria since the last ones left in April. That would mark a decisive turn in a relationship that has moved, in less than two years, from regime change to sanctions relief to a possible military partnership — a pace that would have been unthinkable when Assad fell. But the proposal is still only a discussion, and the obstacles on both sides are real. The demining programme is deliberately small, deliberately practical, and deliberately reversible — which is exactly why it may be the one form of cooperation both sides can agree to try.
