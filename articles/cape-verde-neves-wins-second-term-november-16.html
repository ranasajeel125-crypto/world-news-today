Cape Verde's Neves Wins Second Term in a Landslide, but Low Turnout Tells Its Own Story

politics · 16 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Cape Verde President José Maria Neves won a second five-year term on 15 November 2026, with 63.8% of the vote in provisional results — though turnout was below one-third of eligible voters.

Cape Verde President José Maria Neves has won a second five-year term, according to provisional results from Sunday's election, besting five challengers including the archipelago's first female presidential candidate after a campaign focused on the need to create more jobs for young people. The results from 97% of polling stations, announced on state-owned television, showed Neves earning 63.8% of the vote, more than the 50% needed to avoid a second round.

The same results showed his main challenger, former justice minister Joana Rosa, receiving 31.6% of the vote, with the four other candidates receiving less than 1% each. In a speech, Rosa conceded defeat and said she had already congratulated Neves. “When I embarked on this journey, I knew I had to be prepared for any outcome. I also said throughout the campaign that, as the first woman in Cape Verde's history to run for president, my candidacy would help pave the way for greater equality in our society,” she said.

Neves, meanwhile, pledged to work for all Cape Verdeans, both at home and abroad, to deliver higher wages and fight poverty. “You can count on me to promote freedom, democracy and the rule of law, and to inspire the Cape Verdean nation towards inclusive and sustainable growth,” he said. The national electoral commission is expected to announce official results this week.

Turnout appeared to be low on Sunday, with less than one-third of eligible voters participating, according to the results announced on state-owned television. “The high abstention rate may have been partly driven by the significant mobility of Cape Verdeans in recent months. We therefore need to update the electoral registers,” Neves said. “At the same time, we should modernise the electoral system through the introduction of electronic voting.”

Cape Verde, a former Portuguese colony situated more than 600 km (373 miles) west of mainland Africa, is home to around 500,000 people, with a larger number of Cape Verdeans living overseas, mainly in the US and Portugal. It is one of Africa's most stable democracies but has lost many young people to jobs abroad.

Neves, 66, who was prime minister for 15 years and became president in 2021, is from Cape Verde's oldest political party, the centre-left African Party for the Independence of Cape Verde (PAICV). Rosa is from the centre-right Movement for Democracy party, which had governed for 10 years prior to a May legislative vote. Cape Verde operates under a hybrid presidential-parliamentary model. The prime minister heads the government while the president retains significant powers including veto authority over legislation.

The result cements full control of government by the PAICV, the island nation's oldest political party, after its victory in legislative elections earlier this year. In the parliamentary election held on 17 May 2026, the PAICV won 37 of the 72 seats in the National Assembly, against 33 for the MpD and two for the Democratic and Independent Cape Verdean Union (UCID), ending a decade of MpD-led government. Francisco Carvalho subsequently became prime minister on 19 June. Neves, historically associated with the PAICV, was widely expected to win a second term, and his victory now leaves the same party holding both the presidency and the government for the first time in a decade.

That sweep matters because it removes the divided-government friction that has shaped Cape Verdean politics in recent years. Under the hybrid system, a president from one party and a prime minister from another can check each other through the veto and through competing mandates; with the PAICV now in charge of both offices, Neves and Carvalho will be judged together on delivery, with no rival camp to blame if the jobs pledges stall. The test, as ever in Cape Verde, will be whether unified control produces results that voters can feel, or whether it simply concentrates responsibility for the same stubborn problems.

Those problems were the real subject of the campaign. The economy, on the headline numbers, looks strong: the World Bank reported in July 2026 that Cape Verde's economy expanded by 6.3% in real terms in 2025, driven by record tourism arrivals, stronger private consumption and improved fiscal performance, and the country was reclassified as upper-middle-income in July 2025 after real growth of 7.3% in 2024, buoyed by a record 1.18 million tourist arrivals. Unemployment fell to 6.2%, and the country recorded its first fiscal surplus since 2007. But the growth has not translated into enough work for the young: youth unemployment remains above 15%, and the World Bank put youth unemployment among 15- to 24-year-olds at 20.1% in 2024, with nearly one in four young people aged 15 to 35 not in employment, education or training.

The gap between the national figures and young people's daily experience helps explain both the campaign's focus and the empty polling stations. A survey by Cape Verde's National Institute of Statistics, published in July 2026, found that 64.6% of young people aged 15 to 35 said they intended to emigrate, mainly in search of employment and better pay. The intention was highest among the youngest: 78.1% of those aged 15 to 20 said they wanted to leave, and more than half of those surveyed (54.7%) described the employment opportunities available in the archipelago as “limited” or “very limited”. When asked to name the main problems facing young people, 61.9% cited the need for more job opportunities. It is a striking picture for a country whose economy is growing at more than 6% a year: the jobs exist in tourism and services, but too many young Cape Verdeans do not see a future in them, or cannot reach them.

That is the context for Neves's two concrete promises in his victory remarks: higher wages and a fight against poverty, alongside the defence of freedom, democracy and the rule of law. It is also the context for his remarks on turnout. The president himself linked the low participation to the “significant mobility of Cape Verdeans in recent months” — in other words, to emigration — and proposed updating the electoral registers and introducing electronic voting. The May parliamentary election told the same story: turnout there was just 46.5%, with more than half of voters staying away. Sunday's figure, below one-third, is lower still, and it raises a question that goes beyond this election: in a country where more citizens live abroad than at home, and where most young people say they want to leave, how does the democratic system keep its connection to the people it governs?

Why it matters: Cape Verde is one of Africa's most stable democracies, and a peaceful, conceded election with a clear result is the kind of outcome the region's democrats point to with pride. But the scale of the win — 63.8% against a divided field, with the first woman to run for president taking nearly a third of the vote — sits alongside a turnout figure that weakens the mandate. Neves now has five more years and a government from his own party, which means the promises on wages, poverty and jobs will be tested without excuses. Rosa's candidacy, meanwhile, has already changed something: even in defeat, the first woman to contest the presidency, taking 31.6% of the vote, has shown that the office is no longer out of reach for women in a country where the presidency has always been held by men.

What to watch next: the national electoral commission's official results, expected this week, which will confirm the provisional figures and the final turnout. Then watch whether the new government acts on Neves's electoral-reform ideas — updated registers and electronic voting — and, more importantly, whether the PAICV's unified control of the presidency and the government produces the jobs and higher wages that dominated the campaign. With nearly two-thirds of young Cape Verdeans saying they want to emigrate, the clock on that promise is already running.
