ULA Vulcan Rocket Fails Minutes After Liftoff, Destroying 40 Amazon Leo Satellites in Company's First Mission Failure

technology · 15 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket, carrying 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites, failed about 10 minutes after liftoff from Cape Canaveral on Sunday.

A Vulcan rocket owned by United Launch Alliance, the joint venture of Boeing and Lockheed Martin, failed on Sunday shortly after liftoff from Florida, destroying its payload of 40 satellites from Amazon’s Leo internet network in the launch company’s first mission failure. The rocket failed roughly 10 minutes after lifting off from the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, and the cause was not immediately clear. It was a major setback for the two-decade-old company’s new flagship rocket, just as ULA was seeking to begin a regular cadence of launches for Amazon’s burgeoning satellite internet constellation.

“Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit,” ULA said in a statement. “Every flight generates data, and every flight teaches us something. Since the anomaly just occurred, we don’t have any specific data yet to understand what occurred today,” the company added.

What little is known so far points to a failure late in the climb. It appeared to occur after the separation of Vulcan’s second stage, the portion carrying the Amazon satellites, from the first-stage booster powered by the BE-4 engines. It was unclear whether the BE-4 engines played a role in Sunday’s failure. The failure is expected to lead to a lengthy technical investigation that would keep the rocket off the market for months, a grounding with consequences well beyond a single lost payload.

Sunday’s flight was Vulcan’s fifth since its 2024 debut and its first in nine months, following a launch hiatus drawn out by separate technical investigations into its solid rocket side boosters and its core BE-4 engines, which are supplied by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos’ Blue Origin space company. The boosters were being scrutinised for issues encountered on Vulcan’s last two flights, including a mission in February for the US Space Force. Both missions successfully delivered their payloads to orbit, but the Pentagon had not yet approved the rocket to resume flying national security payloads, pending its performance with the Amazon mission on Sunday.

Blue Origin is working with ULA in the investigation into Sunday’s failure, according to a person familiar with the matter who spoke on condition of anonymity. The engine maker has its own recent failure to explain: Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket suffered a launchpad explosion in May, and its investigation determined the engine was the cause. That led the company to modify the engines delivered to ULA for Vulcan, ULA spokesperson Jessica Rye told Reuters in August. Whether those modifications, or something else entirely, are connected to Sunday’s anomaly is not yet known.

Amazon, which lost all 40 satellites on the flight, struck a supportive note. “There is risk with any launch, and that’s especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01,” Amazon said in a statement, referring to the name of Sunday’s mission. “ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they’ll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan.” The company has bet heavily on Vulcan as one of the workhorses for building its constellation, and a months-long grounding now threatens the pace of that build-out.

The failure is another setback for Amazon as it races to deploy satellites using third-party launch providers, many of which have had groundings and failures. The New Glenn explosion in May happened days before it was due to launch Amazon Leo satellites. The rocket failures have persistently held up Amazon’s bid to rival SpaceX’s vast Starlink internet network, which has more than 11,000 satellites and millions of customers worldwide. SpaceX’s wide lead over its satellite rivals is due in part to the advantage of having its own rocket to launch its satellites, while other companies spend billions of dollars on external launches.

The scale of the task Amazon still faces is stark. Since 2025, 398 Leo satellites have been deployed across 14 launches, about 12% of the 3,236 satellites Amazon needs for global service coverage. The company has hoped to start initial internet service this year. Every lost launch now pushes that timetable further out, and with Vulcan grounded for months, Amazon will have to lean even harder on its other launch providers — the same providers whose rockets have repeatedly been grounded or failed.

The damage extends beyond Amazon. The failure could deepen a US launch bottleneck brought on by other rocket mishaps and challenges across the industry as well as the looming retirement of SpaceX’s Falcon 9, the world’s most active rocket. SpaceX is no longer accepting bookings for the Falcon 9, meaning customers who once had a reliable fallback are running out of options just as Vulcan, the rocket meant to pick up the slack, is grounded. For the Pentagon, the timing is awkward: it had been waiting on Sunday’s flight before clearing Vulcan to resume carrying national security payloads, and that decision will now wait for the outcome of the investigation.

For ULA itself, Sunday ended one of the proudest records in the launch business. ULA was formed in 2006 in a merger of Boeing’s Delta rocket programme and Lockheed Martin’s Atlas rocket programme. The company had embraced what was a 100% mission success record ever since. Its Delta and Atlas rockets had only suffered a handful of failures in the decades before the merger. Sunday’s failure was the launch company’s first mission failure in its two-decade history — a record that had been central to its pitch to the Pentagon and to commercial customers alike.

Why it matters: Sunday was the day the American launch industry’s reliability story cracked. Vulcan was supposed to be the dependable American workhorse — the rocket that would carry the Pentagon’s most sensitive satellites, end reliance on foreign engines and haul Amazon’s constellation into orbit on schedule. Instead, its fifth flight ended in the loss of 40 satellites, a months-long grounding and a fresh round of questions about the engines at its heart. Amazon’s race to catch Starlink, already years behind and barely an eighth of the way to a full constellation, has lost more time it cannot easily recover. And with the Falcon 9 winding down and Vulcan grounded, the United States has less launch capacity than it planned for at the very moment demand for it is rising.

What to watch next: the investigation itself. ULA and Blue Origin will need to establish whether the failure lay in the BE-4 engines, the second stage, or somewhere else entirely — and how long the fix takes. The Pentagon’s decision on national security certification will follow that finding, not precede it. Amazon, meanwhile, will have to say how it plans to keep its deployment schedule moving while one of its main rockets is out of service, and whether its hope of starting initial Leo service this year survives. The answers will shape not just one company’s fortunes, but the balance of the satellite internet race and the capacity of the American launch industry for the rest of the decade.
