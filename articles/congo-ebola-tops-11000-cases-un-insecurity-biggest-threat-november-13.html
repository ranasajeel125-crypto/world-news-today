Congo’s Ebola Outbreak Tops 11,000 Cases as UN Warns Insecurity Is Now the Biggest Threat

world · 14 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Health workers in Bunia, eastern Congo, where cases are falling — but the virus is now reaching more rural and harder-to-reach areas, the WHO says.

The Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ebola outbreak has topped 11,000 cases, the government said this week, a stark figure that makes it the country’s deadliest outbreak on record — and the second-worst Ebola epidemic ever recorded anywhere in the world.

According to the latest health ministry data reported by the Associated Press on Wednesday, 11,042 people have been infected with the virus and 5,387 have died — nearly half of everyone infected. The outbreak was declared in mid-May and is driven by the rare Bundibugyo species of Ebola, for which there are no approved vaccines or treatments, so patients receive only supportive care.

Six months after the outbreak first prompted global concern, UN health officials said on Friday that the response is making progress in the places where the epidemic first took hold. “We do see encouraging signs,” said Chikwe Ihekweazu, head of the World Health Organization’s Health Emergencies Programme, citing falling cases in former hotspots like Bunia in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.

But the same officials warned that the picture is changing rather than ending. “But what we have seen now is the emergence of cases in more rural and more difficult-to-reach areas ... The biggest challenge the response faces right now is the rising insecurity,” Ihekweazu said, citing WHO data showing 80 attacks on health sites and workers so far.

The virus has now spread to eight of the country’s 26 provinces, with the two eastern provinces of Ituri and North Kivu accounting for the bulk of the toll — 9,782 confirmed cases and 4,792 deaths between them, according to health ministry figures cited by the Associated Press. Sixty-seven of the country’s 178 health zones have reported cases, and 2,788 people have recovered.

The French medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières has been more downbeat than the UN about the state of the response, saying that supplies are missing and that the virus is still spreading out of control. Julien Harneis, the UN’s special Ebola coordinator, pushed back against that assessment, pointing to the sheer scale of the operation. “The area of operation is vast, just unimaginable vast... But 80% of the time, and across this vast area, we’re able to operate,” he said.

The disagreement between the UN and MSF captures the central tension of this outbreak: the medical tools exist to slow Ebola, but reaching the sick depends on security, roads and trust — and all three are in short supply in eastern Congo. Congolese authorities have struggled to contain the disease because of armed conflicts, a health system in crisis, poor roads, and violence from residents fuelled by mistrust and misinformation, the Associated Press reported.

The resurgence of violence in the two major hotspots, Ituri and North Kivu, has alarmed health authorities, who are calling for a halt to the fighting so health workers can reach the sick without hindrance. Health facilities and medical professionals have also been targeted by people in communities sceptical of the virus. The Associated Press reported that a nurse was killed last week while responding to a call about a suspected Ebola case, and that a politician from the ruling party was killed in September after advocating health measures on the radio.

The scale of the outbreak puts it second only to the West Africa epidemic of 2014 to 2016, the largest and deadliest Ebola outbreak on record, which saw 28,000 infections and more than 11,000 deaths. The current outbreak has already recorded at least three times as many cases and deaths in the time since it was declared as the West Africa epidemic had at a comparable stage, according to the Associated Press — a measure of how fast this one has grown.

The figures themselves come with a caution: different agencies report different totals because they use different cut-off dates, and the numbers are revised as health officials investigate and reconcile cases. The WHO’s November 6 bulletin recorded 10,668 confirmed cases and 5,198 deaths as of November 3, while the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control reported 10,990 confirmed cases and 5,354 deaths using data through November 8. The health ministry figures reported by the Associated Press on November 11 — 11,042 infections and 5,387 deaths — are the most recent available.

The outbreak has also spread beyond Congo’s borders. The Associated Press reported that the virus has reached multiple African countries, including Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria, adding to the pressure on health systems across the region and to the case for a coordinated international response rather than one confined to Congo alone.

What happens next will be decided largely in the places the response can barely reach. The WHO has recommended additional experimental vaccine candidates for inclusion in an ongoing clinical trial against Bundibugyo virus disease, it announced on 4 November, offering a longer-term hope — but no approved vaccine or treatment exists today. In the meantime, the immediate test is whether the fighting in Ituri and North Kivu can be paused long enough for contact tracers and treatment teams to follow the virus into the rural areas where cases are now emerging.

Why it matters: this is already Congo’s deadliest Ebola outbreak, and the second-largest the world has ever seen, yet it has drawn far less attention than its scale warrants. The UN’s cautious optimism about falling cases in Bunia is real, but so is the warning underneath it — that the virus is moving into places where health workers are attacked, roads barely exist and trust is thin. If the response cannot follow it there, the falling curves in the old hotspots will be little comfort, and an outbreak that has already killed more than 5,300 people will keep growing in the places least able to stop it.
