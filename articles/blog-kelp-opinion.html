The Ocean's Forests Are Falling, and Hardly Anyone Is Watching

blog · 24 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

Kelp forests: the rainforests nobody can see.

blog · 24 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

If 60% of the Amazon were degraded, it would lead every bulletin for a month. This week a report at the UN biodiversity talks found that over 60% of the world's assessed kelp forests are already degraded — and the story struggled to make the front page anywhere. The difference is not importance. It is visibility. Kelp forests fall in the dark, underwater, where no cameras roll.

That is a tragedy of measurement as much as of ecology. Kelp forests shelter roughly a quarter of marine species, nurse the fish that coastal communities eat, blunt storm waves, and pull carbon out of circulation. They do all of this while growing up to half a metre a day — among the fastest growth of any ecosystem on Earth. A kelp forest is a forest in every sense that matters: it has a canopy, it has layers, it provides shelter and food, and it shapes the environment around it. The only difference is that it grows in cold, nutrient-rich water along rocky coasts, out of sight of almost everyone.

The scale of the loss is hard to grasp precisely because it is hidden. Kelp forests fringe the coasts of every continent except Antarctica, from the cold waters of the North Pacific and North Atlantic to the southern oceans around Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and South America. They are among the most productive ecosystems on the planet, and they support fisheries worth billions of dollars a year. When a kelp forest disappears, the fish that depended on it go too, and the coastal communities that depended on the fish lose their livelihoods. The loss is felt in fishing villages and in seafood markets long before it is noticed in the news.

Their collapse is a cascade we set in motion: take away the sea otters and big fish, warm the water, and sea urchins multiply unchecked, grazing forests down to white rock barrens. Every link in that chain is a human decision, which means every link can, in principle, be decided differently. The chain works like this. Sea otters and large predatory fish eat sea urchins. When otters are hunted out and big fish are overfished, the urchins have no predators left. At the same time, warming water stresses the kelp itself, making it grow more slowly and recover less well from grazing. The urchins, freed from predation and facing weakened kelp, multiply and eat everything in sight, until the forest is reduced to bare rock covered in urchins, a state scientists call an urchin barren. Once a barren forms, it can persist for years or decades, because the urchins remain and the kelp cannot re-establish itself.

Warming water is the other great pressure. Kelp thrives in cold water, and as the oceans warm, the conditions it needs are shifting towards the poles. In some places, such as the coast of Tasmania and parts of northern California, whole kelp forests have vanished as the water has warmed beyond what the kelp can tolerate. Marine heatwaves, periods of unusually warm water lasting weeks or months, can kill kelp outright, and they are becoming more frequent and more intense as the climate changes. Pollution from the land, sediment washing off deforested hillsides, and nutrient runoff from farms add further stress, clouding the water and blocking the sunlight kelp needs to grow.

The hopeful part of the report is that kelp responds fast when given the chance. Protect an area, manage the urchins, plant where forests were lost, and recovery can begin within seasons rather than generations. Few climate stories offer that kind of return on effort. Kelp grows so quickly that a restored forest can be functioning within a year or two, and the fish and other species return with it. In places where urchin numbers have been controlled, whether by restoring otter populations, by culling urchins, or by creating marine protected areas where fishing is limited, kelp has come back. Restoration projects that plant young kelp on ropes or reefs have shown that lost forests can be re-established, and the speed of the recovery is one of the most encouraging findings in marine conservation.

There are practical reasons to care about kelp beyond its beauty. Kelp forests protect coastlines by absorbing the energy of waves and storms, reducing erosion and flooding in coastal communities. They are nurseries for fish, including commercially important species, and they support the shellfish, crabs and other animals that coastal economies depend on. They also store carbon, pulling carbon dioxide out of the water as they grow, and when kelp sinks to the deep ocean it can lock that carbon away for long periods. Losing kelp forests means losing all of those services at once, and paying for the consequences in storm damage, lost fisheries and higher emissions.

The problem, in the end, is attention. We protect what we can see, and kelp forests are invisible to most people. There are no satellite images of a kelp forest burning, no dramatic photographs of a dying reef that can be put on a front page. The loss happens underwater, out of sight, and so it is easy to ignore. But the report at the UN biodiversity talks was a reminder that the loss is real, that it is already extensive, and that it is reversible if we choose to act. Protecting kelp means protecting the predators that keep urchins in check, limiting the warming that stresses the forests, and giving restoration the funding and the political support it needs.

We protect what we can see. So the first task is simply this: look. The ocean's forests are falling. It is long past time they got the attention of the ones on land.
