The Ocean's Forests Are Falling, and Hardly Anyone Is Watching

blog · 24 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

Kelp forests: the rainforests nobody can see.

If 60% of the Amazon were degraded, it would lead every bulletin for a month. This week a report at the UN biodiversity talks found that over 60% of the world's assessed kelp forests are already degraded — and the story struggled to make the front page anywhere. The difference is not importance. It is visibility. Kelp forests fall in the dark, underwater, where no cameras roll.

That is a tragedy of measurement as much as of ecology. Kelp forests shelter roughly a quarter of marine species, nurse the fish that coastal communities eat, blunt storm waves, and pull carbon out of circulation. They do all of this while growing up to half a metre a day — among the fastest growth of any ecosystem on Earth.

Their collapse is a cascade we set in motion: take away the sea otters and big fish, warm the water, and sea urchins multiply unchecked, grazing forests down to white rock barrens. Every link in that chain is a human decision, which means every link can, in principle, be decided differently.

The hopeful part of the report is that kelp responds fast when given the chance. Protect an area, manage the urchins, plant where forests were lost, and recovery can begin within seasons rather than generations. Few climate stories offer that kind of return on effort.

We protect what we can see. So the first task is simply this: look. The ocean's forests are falling. It is long past time they got the attention of the ones on land.
