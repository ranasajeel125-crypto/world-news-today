Norway's Premier Defends Putin Call as Effort to Present European Views

world · 28 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Europe Desk)

Tuesday's 90-minute call between Putin, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian premier Jonas Gahr Stoere broke a longstanding practice among Western European leaders of shunning direct contact with the Kremlin.

Norway's prime minister defended on Wednesday a three-way call with Vladimir Putin and Finland's president, saying it was intended to ensure the Russian leader heard European as well as US views.

Tuesday's 90-minute call between Putin, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian premier Jonas Gahr Stoere broke a longstanding practice among Western European leaders of shunning direct contact with the Kremlin, and was criticised by allies including Denmark's Mette Frederiksen. It was the first direct contact between Putin and Western European leaders since French President Emmanuel Macron spoke to him in July 2025.

The call, held on Tuesday, 27 October, was originally scheduled for less than 30 minutes but lasted about 90 minutes. Stubb said in a post on X that it was a follow-up to a meeting in Oslo on 20 October, referring to talks between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, held amid deteriorating relations between Washington and Kyiv. Stubb said the conversation with Putin was prepared in consultation with Ukraine and the United States, and that before the call they informed key allies, including all Nordic states, and afterwards informed them of its content. They also briefed Zelenskyy for about 30 minutes after the call, going through the key issues and messages discussed. Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said the call had been coordinated with Kyiv and was "the right approach".

"We (Stubb and I) were focused on looking forward, what must happen for this war to not continue endlessly for months, with people dying ... and it is even more difficult to find a solution," Stoere told public broadcaster NRK. "I don't think (Putin) will change his mind, but he gets to hear our position very clearly. I am worried that some of these messages do not reach him when the Americans talk to him," he said. "Talking to the other side does in no way mean that we take off the pressure. So I believe that that is something Europe also should do with voices that can be understood on that side."

Stubb described the discussion with Putin as "frank and substantive". "We are neighbouring countries to Russia. The outcome of this war will have a direct impact on our and the broader European security. Ending the war requires direct discussions," he said. Stoere said he and Stubb had stressed the importance of de-escalation, and both voiced strong support for Ukraine. The two leaders said they spoke with Putin in their capacities as national leaders, not on behalf of all of Europe, and had consulted thoroughly with all their partners.

Norway, said Stoere, was keen to introduce de-escalation steps such as stopping attacks on energy infrastructure in Russia and Ukraine, halting strikes on ships exporting Ukrainian grain and conducting prisoner-of-war exchanges. Those three measures were chosen because each is, in principle, verifiable and reversible: a pause in strikes on power plants and substations would protect civilians on both sides as winter approaches; a halt to attacks on grain ships would protect Ukraine's exports and global food supplies; and prisoner exchanges have been one of the few areas where Russia and Ukraine have cooperated throughout the war, with thousands of prisoners swapped in deals brokered by intermediaries.

Asked whether there could be peace without Ukraine ceding territory, Stoere said: "Today Russia controls nearly 20% (of Ukrainian territory). Ukraine will never sign a deal that recognises that they have lost territory, but whether they can take the current frontline as a reality and as a starting point, that is a different question." "And the big question is what kind of security guarantees can there be for the country (Ukraine) after a peace deal? Putin recognises that this is the big question."

The Kremlin's readout of the call did not suggest any softening of Putin's position. It said achieving a just and long-term peace was possible only if the legitimate interests of the Russian Federation were fully and unconditionally taken into account, and made reference to the confrontational policies of EU and NATO countries, which it said were contributing to the prolongation of the hostilities. According to a source cited by Reuters, Putin told Stubb and Stoere that the European involvement was welcome and that the war needed to end "taking into account the realities on the ground". The Europeans responded by saying a "just" peace was needed. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the call was "constructive and substantive", adding that Putin's position was well known and consistent, and that he had spoken about the need to address the root causes. According to the Financial Times, Putin spent much of the call lecturing the Nordic leaders on medieval Slavic history and the "initial reasons for the conflict", leaving Ukraine's main demand for an immediate ceasefire barely discussed.

Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov has said Tuesday's call offered nothing new on the Ukraine conflict, and that European countries were simply seeking a role in the negotiation process. Lavrov said the conflict was likely to escalate.

The 90-minute call came ahead of what are expected to be the first face-to-face talks since February between Ukrainian and Russian officials, in Abu Dhabi next month. The talks, involving the United States, Russia and Ukraine, are set to resume under US brokerage. The call by Stubb and Stoere was an attempt to influence the diplomatic process after US President Donald Trump shifted his position in favour of Russia and US ties with Ukraine hit their lowest point in 18 months. With concern growing that Europe is being sidelined in Ukraine ceasefire talks led by the United States, the Nordic leaders' call was seen as an attempt to reflect Europe's voice in the discussions.

Even so, the mood within Europe around the call was not entirely friendly. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen told Denmark's public broadcaster DR that "Europe must unite rather than act individually", expressing scepticism about Finland and Norway's independent outreach. She added that Russia shows no sign it wants peace, and said: "I am very sceptical about this Norwegian and Finnish initiative. It was not coordinated with any of us. Honestly, I don't know what these two countries think that they can achieve this way." Swedish Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson questioned the effectiveness of the call. He told Swedish daily DN that "it seems everyone except Putin wants peace", and said other European leaders were not informed of the talks. "My impression is that everyone wants peace, except for Putin himself. I have quite low expectations for this type of conversation," Kristersson said. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk likewise said extra caution and vigilance are needed when engaging with Russia, telling Stubb and Stoere that "absolute caution and vigilance are necessary in such contacts".

The Kremlin said Putin, Stoere and Stubb would keep in touch by phone. Whether the conversation helps the Abu Dhabi talks or simply deepens divisions among allies will become clearer when negotiators meet next month — but the row has already turned a diplomatic initiative into a test of European unity, and of whether talking to Putin is a way to end the war or a concession to the man who started it.
