Typhoon Champi Disrupts Flights Across East Asia as It Nears Taiwan

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Kenji Watanabe (Asia Correspondent)

Typhoon Champi brought Taiwan to a near standstill on Friday, 23 October 2026, as the powerful storm swept past the island's eastern coast, forcing the closure of schools, offices and financial markets and grounding almost 1,000 flights. Only the Taiwan-controlled Kinmen islands, which sit close to the Chinese coast, opened schools and offices as normal. Taiwan's transport ministry said all 262 domestic flights were cancelled for Friday, along with 717 international flights, in one of the largest single-day aviation shutdowns the island has seen from a storm.

Champi had been one of the strongest tropical cyclones in the Pacific this year. The Japan Meteorological Agency named the storm on 15 October, and it brushed past Guam on Sunday, 18 October, its eye passing about 10 miles south of the island's southern tip, before strengthening into a Category 5 super typhoon with sustained winds of about 145 knots, or 270 kilometres per hour. By the time it approached Taiwan it had weakened somewhat, but Taiwan's Central Weather Administration said it was still packing gusts of around 191 kilometres per hour near its centre, with a storm radius of about 320 kilometres, large enough to cover most of the island. Forecasters reported a central pressure of around 946 hectopascals and waves of up to 13 metres in the open sea.

Taiwan's weather authorities issued a land warning on Thursday, 22 October, and warned of destructive winds, torrential rain and dangerous coastal waves, urging residents in exposed areas to secure property and avoid the shoreline. The forecast track showed the storm passing just off Taiwan's northeastern coast, turning north toward the Chinese coast and eventually moving into southern Japan, without making a direct landfall on the island. Its closest approach came on Friday evening, when the eye passed roughly 137 kilometres east of Taipei, and weather experts said the storm's effects could last nearly two days.

One person was killed after being swept away by powerful waves generated by the storm, and 163 people were injured, a figure revised down from an earlier count of 171. The government said 2,639 people had been evacuated from mountainous areas. Taiwan Power Company said more than 77,000 homes had lost power, though electricity had already been restored to most of them. In the northeastern county of Yilan, where strong winds and heavy showers were intensifying and the typhoon was forecast to pass close by, authorities distributed sandbags and put amphibious vehicles and inflatable rescue boats on standby.

The disruption to transport was severe. At Taiwan Taoyuan International Airport, 414 flights had been cancelled by Thursday evening, including 353 passenger flights and 61 cargo flights, and a further 596 flights were cancelled between midnight and 9 a.m. on Friday, including 521 passenger flights and 75 cargo flights. EVA Air cancelled all of its long-haul flights between Taiwan and Europe or the United States for Friday, along with some flights at Taoyuan and Taipei Songshan airports. Starlux Airlines said it would operate only long-haul flights to Europe, the United States and other destinations. China Airlines cancelled afternoon flights from Songshan, Taoyuan and Kaohsiung, along with every Taichung flight that day. Tigerair Taiwan cancelled 42 Friday flights between Taiwan and Japan or South Korea, including services to Naha, Fukuoka, Osaka Kansai, Jeju and Busan. The north-south high-speed rail service continued to operate as usual.

Ferries were hit just as hard. The Maritime and Port Bureau said 10 ferry routes were disrupted on Friday, affecting 96 sailings. All sailings were suspended on nine routes: Keelung to Matsu, Nangan's Fuao Port to Fuzhou's Langqi Port in China, Beigan's Baisha Port to Fuzhou's Huangqi Port, Kinmen to Shijing, Kaohsiung to Magong, Donggang to Xiaoliuqiu, Yanpu to Xiaoliuqiu, Fugang to Green Island and Fugang to Lanyu. Airlines across the region said they would rebook affected passengers once conditions allowed, and several carriers offered fee-free changes. China Airlines said passengers whose flights were cancelled or delayed by two hours or more could rebook within 14 days or take a refund without a fee, while Cathay Pacific waived rebooking and rerouting charges on Taipei tickets issued on or before 21 October for travel on 23 or 24 October.

The storm also disrupted travel in neighbouring Japan, where more than 100 domestic flights were cancelled ahead of its expected approach. Forecasters said the typhoon would first pass over Yonaguni, Japan's westernmost inhabited island in Okinawa Prefecture, before turning northeast into the East China Sea and heading toward southern Japan, where it was expected to lose energy and become a post-tropical cyclone.

Typhoons form over the warm tropical waters of the western Pacific, drawing energy from the warm sea surface and the moisture rising above it. Taiwan sits in one of the most typhoon-prone regions in the world, and its Central Weather Administration issues sea warnings followed by land warnings as a storm approaches, giving local governments time to close schools and offices, move people out of mountain areas at risk of landslides, and prepare emergency services. The island has invested heavily in flood defences, early-warning systems and evacuation planning, and warmer ocean waters are giving typhoons more energy, making the strongest storms more destructive when they arrive.

By Saturday afternoon, weather officials expected Champi to begin moving away from Taiwan, with heavy rain and strong winds easing as the storm weakened and accelerated northeast. Airports advised travellers to keep checking flight status before leaving home, as departures and arrivals could still be moved earlier or delayed while schedules recovered. The shutdown of Taiwan's financial markets, offices and transport for a day underlined how much a single storm can interrupt daily life and business on the island, which is a major hub for electronics manufacturing and regional air travel, and how heavily the region depends on accurate forecasting and early preparation when a storm of this strength approaches.
