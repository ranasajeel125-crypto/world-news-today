Peltola Wins Alaska Senate Seat, Giving Democrats a 56–44 Majority

politics · 17 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

With 99% of the vote counted, Peltola led Sullivan 50.1% to 45.8%, clearing the 50% threshold under Alaska's ranked-choice voting system and avoiding further rounds of counting.

Democrat Mary Peltola won Alaska's U.S. Senate race on Tuesday, defeating two-term Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan, the Associated Press said, in the last Senate contest of the 2026 midterm elections to be called — two weeks after Election Day. Her victory gives Democrats a ninth seat flipped from Republicans and a commanding 56–44 majority in the chamber, Reuters reported.

With 99% of the vote counted, Peltola had 50.1% to Sullivan's 45.8%, the Associated Press said as it called the race. That was enough to clear the 50% threshold under Alaska's ranked-choice voting system, so no further rounds of counting were needed; Reuters reported the split as 50% to 46%. Retired teacher Dan J. Sullivan, an independent candidate who shares the incumbent's name and was dubbed “Decoy Dan”, took about 1.7% of the vote, according to the New York Post.

Peltola becomes the first Alaska Native elected to the U.S. Senate, the Wall Street Journal reported. She previously held Alaska's lone House seat, making her the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress, and she will be just the fifth Democrat since statehood to represent Alaska in the Senate, the Associated Press reported. She is the first Democrat to win one of Alaska's Senate seats since Mark Begich in 2008.

The scale of the Democratic sweep is historic. Democrats flipped nine Senate seats in the midterms, the most since 1958, the Associated Press reported, after entering the elections with Republicans holding a 53–47 majority. They now emerge with a 56–44 margin — their largest Senate majority since 2009, according to Reuters — and the Alaska result, the last to be called, settles the full 100-seat count.

The result also completes a remarkable night for Alaska Democrats. The state elected a Democrat as governor for the first time since 1998 in the same midterm elections, the Associated Press reported, after former state lawmaker Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins defeated three Republicans, including one endorsed by President Donald Trump. Trump won Alaska by 13 percentage points in 2024, Reuters noted — a measure of how far the state swung in a single cycle.

For Sullivan, the defeat ends a Senate career that began in 2015. He was seeking a third term, The Times reported, having held the seat through two previous elections in a state Trump has repeatedly won by wide margins. Peltola, by contrast, served in the House of Representatives until 2024, when she was unseated by a Republican as Trump handily won the state, The Times reported — making Tuesday's result a political comeback in one of the country's most Republican-leaning states, the Associated Press said.

The race was roiled in its final days by the independent candidate who shares the incumbent's name. The Times reported that Dan J. Sullivan's similar name caused controversy and a lawsuit from the Department of Justice, and the Associated Press reported that Peltola overcame allegations that members of her campaign helped recruit a candidate who shares the incumbent's name. Peltola has denied being involved in an alleged scheme to siphon votes away from Senator Sullivan, the New York Post reported. She finished first in the primary with 49.5% of the vote, followed by the incumbent with 41.4%, according to the Associated Press.

What the majority means in practice is control of the Senate's machinery. When Democrats take control of Congress next year, the Wall Street Journal reported, Peltola's victory will bring their majority to 56 seats — enough to set the chamber's agenda, chair its committees and move the party's nominees through confirmation votes without Republican support. Combined with the Democrats' securing of the House of Representatives, Reuters reported, the result sets up two years of possible deadlock between Congress and President Donald Trump.

That deadlock will shape the second half of Trump's term. With Democrats holding both chambers, the White House faces a Congress able to block its legislative agenda, open investigations and use the power of the purse — while the president retains the veto and executive action. The 56–44 margin, wider than the one-seat majorities that have decided recent Congresses, gives the Democratic leadership room to lose a handful of votes and still pass its priorities.

Why it matters: the Alaska call closes the book on the 2026 midterms, and it closes it with the biggest Democratic Senate gain in nearly seven decades — nine flips, the most since 1958, and a 56–44 majority, the party's largest since 2009. It puts the first Alaska Native in the Senate, returns a Democrat to one of Alaska's Senate seats for the first time since 2008, and does it in a state the president carried by 13 points a year earlier. With the House also in Democratic hands, the result locks in two years of divided government in Washington, where the fate of the Trump administration's agenda will be decided not by one party's majority but by the space between two.
