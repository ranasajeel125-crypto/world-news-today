Ukraine Says Starlink Disruption Lifted as Europe Weighs a Satellite Back-Up

technology · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Tom Becker (Technology Editor)

Ukraine's battlefield communications depend heavily on commercial satellite networks such as Starlink.

Ukraine's military said on Saturday that restrictions on its Starlink and Starshield satellite connections had been lifted, after more than a week of disruption that hampered drone operations against Russian forces. Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara said the services were stable on equipment travelling above 400 kilometres per hour, and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said speeds had returned to the level they were at before the restrictions began.

The disruption began suddenly. On 13 October, Ukrainian officials announced that sudden restrictions on Starlink services had left the country's military scrambling, and reports the following morning said SpaceX had throttled the service. The restrictions capped connectivity for equipment travelling faster than roughly 100 miles per hour, or about 160 kilometres per hour, disrupting some drone missions and complicating efforts to counter Russia's increasingly fast attack drones. Zelenskyy appealed directly to SpaceX chief executive Elon Musk, calling the slowdown a big mistake.

Ukraine relies heavily on Musk's SpaceX satellite services for communications and drone missions. Starlink terminals are woven through the country's command structure, guiding drones, coordinating artillery and keeping units connected where terrestrial networks have been destroyed. Starlink is the commercial satellite internet service, while Starshield is the military-grade version of the system, which Musk says is owned and controlled by the United States government.

Who ordered the restrictions was never clearly established, and the question became a public blame game. Musk said on 14 October that any limits on speed or usage for Starshield were decided by the US military and ultimately by the president of the United States, and that commercial Starlink was limited to civilian or defensive activities and could not be used for offensive military action. The Wall Street Journal reported on 21 October that Musk himself was responsible for the squeeze, citing anonymous US officials, while a conflicting report from Reuters on 14 October said the White House had ordered the slowdown.

President Donald Trump denied ordering the restrictions, but the Journal reported that he had called Musk and instructed him to end them. Asked about the report, Trump declined to discuss whether he had made the call, telling reporters that the bottom line was that Musk had put the service back. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said during the same week that decisions about Starlink's speed and access belonged to Musk. Zelenskyy later told NBC's Meet the Press that Ukrainian officials believed the US government was behind the disruptions.

The restoration was announced in stages and was not immediately accepted by everyone on the ground. Trump said on 22 October that Musk had lifted all speed restrictions on Starshield, and on 23 October he said the service had been restored. But a presidential communications adviser, Dmytro Lytvyn, wrote on 22 October that the restrictions remained in place and that nothing had changed, and activist and former defence ministry adviser Serhiy Sternenko reported on 23 October that some units had resumed drone missions above the previous limits while others still found the restrictions in effect.

By Saturday, the picture from Kyiv was clearer. Zelenskyy said on X and Telegram that the restoration had begun overnight and that by morning speeds were already sufficient, adding that the most important thing was that Starlink and Starshield operations had been restored to the speeds they had before the restrictions. The Defence Ministry said its monitoring systems were officially recording the restoration of operating speed, with connectivity stable for projectiles and interceptors travelling at more than 250 miles per hour.

Hours after Ukraine announced the connection was back to normal, Musk admitted that SpaceX had throttled the service. In a post on X, he said Ukraine's military had violated Starlink's terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people, and that the loophole had been closed the previous week. He also repeated his warning that Ukraine's front line would collapse immediately if Starlink were turned off, a claim that underlines how completely the country's defence depends on a single commercial provider.

The episode revived a debate European governments have been having for years: can Europe plug the gap with a sovereign satellite network of its own? The problem is scale. As of mid-October 2026, Starlink had approximately 11,150 satellites in orbit. Its closest European competitor, the British-French OneWeb network operated by Eutelsat, had around 650, roughly seventeen times fewer, and plans to grow its fleet to about 1,300 by 2030 after a one-billion-euro contract signed with Airbus. OneWeb's older technology is also less suited to the front line, because its terminals are bulky, heavy and power-hungry, and switching from Starlink or Starshield would require replacing thousands of terminals because SpaceX uses proprietary technology.

The European Union's flagship answer is IRIS², short for Infrastructure for Resilience, Interconnectivity and Security by Satellite, a multi-orbit network intended to provide secure communications for governments, defence institutions and critical infrastructure, alongside connectivity for businesses and citizens. The project is worth about 10.6 billion euros, and under current plans the constellation will include 330 satellites in low Earth orbit and 18 in medium Earth orbit, with dozens more for defence and emergency response. In August 2026 the European Commission signed an implementation agreement with the SpaceRISE consortium, but the first launches are not planned until 2029 and operational services are expected to start in 2030.
