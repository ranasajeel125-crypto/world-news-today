Fed Holds Rates Steady, Calling the Economy Strong — but Leaves Bond Investors Guessing

business · 29 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

The Federal Reserve kept rates on hold on Wednesday and upgraded its assessment of the US economy to "strong", but the lack of a clear signal on future moves left longer-dated Treasury yields under pressure.

The Federal Reserve kept rates on hold on Wednesday and upgraded its assessment of the US economy to "strong", but the lack of a clear signal on future moves left longer-dated Treasury yields under pressure.

The US Federal Reserve kept interest rates steady on Wednesday, 28 October, as expected, but it was not able to comfort bond investors concerned about what a deliberate pace of policy tightening will mean for the outlook for longer-term government bonds. The Federal Open Market Committee voted 10-2 to keep the benchmark federal funds rate in a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, after raising rates by a quarter percentage point in September, its first rate hike in more than three years. Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented, preferring an immediate quarter-point increase.

The central bank described an economy strong enough to keep inflation elevated, yet refrained from raising rates immediately. That deepened uncertainty over how much further policy may need to tighten, analysts said, and over how much compensation investors could demand to hold longer-dated Treasuries, which have soared this year to their highest yields in two decades. The 10- and 30-year Treasury yields surged to 24-year highs earlier in October, and a combination of resilient economic growth and persistent inflation could complicate the US fiscal outlook, limiting the Fed's ability to lower interest rates when the economy turns and keeping Treasury borrowing costs elevated.

The Fed upgraded its assessment of the economy to "strong" from the "solid" expansion seen as of the September meeting, while describing investment as robust and job gains as keeping pace with the workforce. Real US GDP expanded at a 3.4% annualised rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2% in the second quarter and ahead of expectations for 3.2%, and growth in the first half of 2026 was revised up from an earlier estimate of 1.8% to 2.4%. But how that leads to price pressures coming down — the main priority for bond investors — was less clear. Inflation remains above the Fed's 2% target, with consumer prices up 3.7% from a year earlier in September and core inflation at 2.7%, and Americans have been dealing with inflation running above the committee's objective for close to six years.

"We will deliver price stability, no ifs ands or buts," Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at his press conference on 28 October. He said that while "inflation is too high", the central bank is "encouraged by progress" and that it is "our job to get monetary policy right". Warsh has repeatedly emphasised that price stability remains the Fed's priority and that the 2% inflation target is not flexible, telling reporters there was "no soft inflation target" and "no implicit inflation target". He said energy and related prices had moved higher again in recent weeks, and that the Fed's job was to ensure relative price changes in some sectors did not broaden out across the economy and that inflation expectations remained anchored. He also said productivity had been moving higher, and that stronger growth did not necessarily mean the economy was overheating: "Economic growth is higher. But that doesn't tell me that economic growth is somehow causing inflation, making our job harder," he said.

"Ultimately, if we see strong growth, which (Fed Chair Kevin) Warsh is expecting, we're going to see more inflation, which means those fiscal deficit concerns and how to address them longer term become more relevant," said Anders Persson, chief investment officer for global fixed income at investment manager Nuveen in Charlotte, North Carolina. Market participants also questioned the Fed's assessment of the economy, with some analysts arguing Warsh was "overstating the breadth of strength in the US economy, meaning he will likely have a relatively low bar for a downside surprise". That uncertainty over the economic outlook can weigh on Treasuries at the front end of the curve, while the long end faces pressure from inflation uncertainty and fiscal concerns.

The yield curve steepened following Warsh's press conference, with the long end selling off. The spread between 2- and 10-year yields widened to as much as 34.1 basis points after flattening over the last few weeks. The 10-year yield increased to 5.036% from 5.020% before the rate decision was announced, while the two-year fell to 4.702% from 4.770%, according to Tradeweb. Treasury yields climbed across most maturities from the belly of the curve, between five and seven years, onwards, with longer-dated yields coming under particular pressure. "The bond market selling off is because the Fed is going to have a hard time raising rates much higher without causing issues somewhere," one analyst said. The dollar weakened during Warsh's remarks, and stocks closed slightly higher on the day.

Warsh did not give a definitive commitment on future policy action, and suggested the Fed's last rate increase is starting to work and that financial conditions have started to tighten. Financial markets are already pricing in the possibility of another increase in December, with expectations for further hikes next year adding to uncertainty over the policy outlook. Following the decision, odds of an interest rate increase in December reached 83%, up from 78% the day before, according to CME FedWatch, and markets were pricing in two more hikes through the end of 2027. J.P. Morgan Wealth Management strategists believe there could be 50 basis points in total additional hikes before the cycle is over, and that conditions will be met for a rate hike in December given the underlying strength of the economy. Warsh said "the period right in front of us is information-rich", with GDP data and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures index, due on Thursday, and he said politics played "no role" in the decision.

Warsh also announced changes to the FOMC calendar that will maintain eight meetings per year but reduce the number of rate decisions to seven in 2027 and six in 2028, with the remaining meetings dedicated to broader monetary policy strategy, with no rate vote or policy statement expected. Investors are also looking ahead to the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement next week, and given the recent bond selloff, any signal that the Treasury would adjust long-end issuance to reflect lower demand will be watched closely. Deutsche Bank strategists said their analysis of Treasury holdings data suggested "there has been a retrenchment in demand for long-dated Treasurys", and that there was "a compelling case for more proactively shortening the maturity profile of future issuance". Attention now shifts to the other central banks meeting this week: the European Central Bank announces its decision later on Thursday and the Bank of Japan concludes a two-day meeting on Friday, with both expected to leave interest rates unchanged.
