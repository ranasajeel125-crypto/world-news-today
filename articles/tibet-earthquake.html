Powerful Earthquake Strikes China's Tibet Region

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Kenji Watanabe (Asia Correspondent)

A powerful earthquake has struck China's Tibet region, prompting emergency response efforts and raising concerns about communities in one of the most remote and mountainous areas on Earth.

The earthquake measured magnitude 7.0 and struck at 10:33 p.m. local time on Thursday, 22 October 2026, according to the United States Geological Survey and the China Earthquake Networks Center. Its epicentre was in Baqen County, in the city of Nagqu in northern Tibet, in a sparsely populated, high-altitude stretch of the Tibetan Plateau about 190 kilometres east-northeast of Nagqu and roughly 390 kilometres northeast of the regional capital, Lhasa. The United States Geological Survey put the depth at about 18 kilometres, while the Chinese network reported a depth of about 10 kilometres.

At least 16 people were killed and 76 others were injured, according to Chinese authorities, including 11 people who were seriously hurt. Officials reported the collapse of at least 339 homes and 20 livestock sheds. More than 12,000 residents were evacuated from the affected area as rescue teams moved in. The earthquake was the strongest to hit China since January 2024, and Chinese state broadcaster CCTV described it as the biggest earthquake in the region in the last five years.

China activated a level-II earthquake disaster emergency response, and Beijing deployed about 1,600 rescue personnel to the area. Electricity and communication lines were reported to be down in parts of the affected region, and rescue workers faced a difficult journey to reach villages sitting at an average altitude of about 4,800 metres, where the thin air and cold make heavy physical work far harder. Many roads were blocked or damaged by rockfalls, and a section of National Highway 317 was closed to all but emergency, rescue and official vehicles while crews worked to clear debris.

The danger did not end with the main shock. A magnitude 5.2 aftershock was felt about 45 minutes after the earthquake, and by the morning of Monday, 26 October, the China Earthquake Networks Center had recorded a total of 2,054 aftershocks, including two of at least magnitude 5.0. On Sunday evening, 25 October, a magnitude 5.9 aftershock struck the same area, followed a minute later by a magnitude 5.6 tremor in neighbouring Qinghai province. Chinese authorities said on 26 October that there had been no secondary geological disasters and no further casualties from the earthquake.

The epicentre lay in Maru township in Baqen County, a remote area where homes are widely scattered across high grassland used by herding families. The sparse population limited the human toll, but it also meant that help had further to travel and that damage to individual homes and sheds could cut families off from shelter and livestock at the start of winter. Intermittent snow was forecast for the earthquake region in the days after the quake, adding urgency to the delivery of tents, warm clothing and food.

Tibet sits in one of the most earthquake-prone regions on Earth. The Tibetan Plateau was formed by the slow collision of the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates, a process that continues today and that builds the Himalayas while storing up enormous strain in the crust. When that strain is released along faults beneath the plateau, the result can be a powerful shallow earthquake, and shallow quakes tend to produce stronger shaking at the surface. The 22 October earthquake was caused by movement on a normal fault, a type of fracture in which one block of rock drops relative to another as the crust is stretched.

The region has suffered major earthquakes before. In January 2025, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck Dingri County in southern Tibet, near the border with Nepal, killing at least 126 people and damaging thousands of homes. A decade earlier, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake centred in Nepal in April 2015 killed nearly 9,000 people across Nepal and caused damage in Tibet as well. Those disasters shaped the way China now responds to quakes on the plateau, with standardised emergency levels, pre-positioned rescue teams and rapid deployment of military and civilian personnel.

The response in Nagqu followed that pattern. Firefighters and soldiers inspected damaged villages, set up emergency shelters and moved supplies into the area, while officials worked to restore power and communications and to assess which buildings were safe to re-enter. The authorities warned that aftershocks would continue and that people should stay away from damaged structures. With winter approaching and snow expected, the immediate priority was keeping evacuated families warm and fed while engineers checked roads, bridges and public buildings for hidden damage.

The earthquake also raised wider questions about building safety in remote high-altitude communities, where many homes are built from local materials and where retrofitting against strong shaking is difficult and expensive. The collapse of hundreds of homes in a sparsely populated county showed how vulnerable such settlements remain, even when the population is small. Recovery in Baqen County was expected to take months, with temporary shelter giving way to repair and rebuilding once the full scale of the damage had been surveyed and the aftershock sequence had eased.
