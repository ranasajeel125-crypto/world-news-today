Syria Shuts Its Only Border Crossing With Jordan After Airstrikes Kill Seven

world · 27 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Layla Haddad (Middle East Correspondent)

Nassib is the only passenger and commercial border crossing between Jordan and Syria, and its closure cuts the main overland link between the two countries.

Syria closed the Nassib border crossing with Jordan on Tuesday, suspending passenger and commercial traffic in both directions, after Jordanian airstrikes on southern Syria killed seven people, including three children and a woman, according to Syrian officials.

Syria’s General Authority for Border Crossings and Customs said the closure was ordered because of developments on the ground in Daraa province, and was intended to ensure the safety of travellers and workers at the crossing. Nassib is Jordan’s only passenger and commercial border crossing with Syria.

Jordan said on Monday that its air force had struck what it described as militant hideouts in southern Syria, after determining that the suspects behind last week’s deadly church attack in the kingdom were linked to terrorist groups operating there. Jordan’s armed forces said the operation was conducted in coordination with Damascus, but Syria’s government gave a contradictory account, saying it had launched an investigation into the attacks and informing Jordan of its complete reservation over strikes carried out without prior coordination.

Syria’s Civil Defence said in a preliminary report that seven people were killed and three others wounded, with emergency teams responding to four sites hit in the countryside of Daraa and Sweida provinces. It said five of the dead — a man, a woman and three children from one family — were killed in a strike on a tent in the village of Al Tai in Daraa, while an 80-year-old man was killed in Al Shayyah and a 60-year-old man in Shahba in Sweida province. Jordan said its strikes killed and wounded several militants and destroyed vehicles and sites, but gave no specific casualty figures.

The strikes were retaliation for Friday’s attack during a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in the village of Hamoud in Jordan’s Karak governorate, in which gunmen opened fire for several minutes, killing nine people and wounding at least 34. Earlier on Monday, Jordanian security forces arrested two brothers and their brother-in-law after a shootout in the village of Al-Jadeeda, near Karak. The three suspects were wounded and no security personnel were injured, officials said, and there has been no claim of responsibility for the church attack.

Such violence is rare in Jordan, a relatively stable US ally in the Middle East, and King Abdullah ordered security forces to respond with an iron fist after the church attack. With investigations under way on both sides and diplomatic contacts continuing, the border closure leaves the two neighbours to manage a dispute over an operation one side calls coordinated and the other says it was never told about.

The airstrikes came on Monday evening, when Jordanian aircraft hit four sites in rural Daraa and Sweida provinces. Syria's Civil Defence service said its crews had recovered the bodies of seven people. Five of the dead — a man, a woman and three children — were members of one family killed in a strike on a tent in the village of al-Taf, in the Lajat area of northeastern Daraa province. In the al-Shayah farms near Nassib, in Daraa countryside, a strike on a farm killed Riyad Karrad, 80. In the town of Shuhayb, in northern Suwayda countryside, a strike on a home killed Mutlaq Abu al-Khail, 60. The head of the Health Ministry in Daraa district told the Syrian state news agency SANA that the dead included three children, three men and a woman, and that two men and one child were also injured. Rescue teams were still searching for more victims, and a final toll had not been determined.

Jordan's account of the operation differed sharply from Syria's. The Jordanian Armed Forces said in a statement that it targeted "hideouts" of armed groups in southeastern Syria in response to the gun attack at a church in Jordan's Karak governorate on Friday. Jordanian government spokesman Mohammad al-Momani later said the air strikes killed 20 fighters from the Islamic State armed group, but acknowledged that some civilians were killed. "The operation resulted in the killing and wounding of many elements of the terrorist organizations and the destruction of a number of their vehicles," Jordan's Armed Forces added. Jordan did not specify the locations of the attacks or provide casualty figures in its initial statement.

Damascus contradicted Amman's claim that the strikes were coordinated. In a statement, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs informed Jordan of its "complete reservation" over the attacks, which took place "without prior coordination or notification with the Syrian side". A Foreign Ministry official told Al Jazeera the strikes were a "treacherous attack", and Syria condemned the strikes as an "unacceptable violation". Syria said it had launched an investigation into the attacks.

The church attack that triggered the strikes took place on Friday, October 23, during a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in the village of Hammoud in Jordan's Karak governorate. Gunmen opened fire for several minutes, killing nine people and wounding at least 34. ISIL (ISIS) has claimed responsibility for the October 23 church attack. Earlier on Monday, Jordanian security forces arrested two brothers and their brother-in-law after a shootout in the village of Al-Jadeeda, near Karak. The three suspects were wounded and no security personnel were injured.

Such violence is rare in Jordan, a relatively stable US ally in the Middle East, and King Abdullah ordered security forces to respond with an iron fist after the church attack. The kingdom has largely been spared the sectarian and militant violence that has afflicted its neighbours, which is why the attack on a church funeral — and the military response across the border — has shaken the country.

The border closure was brief. Nassib, Jordan's only border crossing with Syria — known as Jaber on the Jordanian side — was closed on Tuesday, suspending all passenger and commercial traffic in both directions. The General Authority for Border Crossings and Customs announced the closure "due to prevailing circumstances and developments in Daraa province", saying it was intended to ensure the safety of travellers and staff at the crossing. But the crossing was due to reopen on Wednesday morning, with regular passenger and commercial traffic resuming, the state news agency SANA reported, and the two countries agreed to investigate the strikes together. Mourners in southern Syria carried the bodies of people killed in the strikes at a funeral in the village of al-Taf on October 27, even as the diplomatic contacts continued and the border prepared to reopen.
