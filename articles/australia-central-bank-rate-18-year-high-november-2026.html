Australia's Central Bank Lifts Rates to 18-Year High, Split Vote Points to Pause Ahead

business · 3 Nov 2026 · 7 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

The Reserve Bank of Australia lifted rates by 25 basis points to 4.85%, but removed a reference to raising rates further if needed.

Australia's central bank raised its cash rate to an 18-year high on Tuesday in its fifth hike of the year, though the vote was closer than many expected, with three of the nine board members wanting to stay on hold.

Wrapping up its November policy meeting, the Reserve Bank of Australia's board lifted rates by 25 basis points to 4.85% in a 6-3 vote and said it would continue to do what it considers necessary to bring inflation to target. However, it did remove a reference to increasing the cash rate further if needed. The decision was taken at the board's Melbourne Cup Day meeting, and the cash rate is now at its highest level since 2008.

The Aussie dollar slipped 0.3% to $0.6979 and three-year government bond futures fell 3 ticks to 95.14. Markets are wagering the Reserve Bank will be on hold in December after the split vote, and the bank's next decision is due on 8 December 2026. The split matters because a divided board suggests some members believe the 125 basis points of tightening delivered this year is already doing its job, and that the bar for a sixth rate hike is now considerably higher.

Thirty of 32 economists polled by Reuters expected a hike, while markets implied an 88% chance of a move after hot third-quarter inflation data and hawkish commentary from policymakers eclipsed concerns about unemployment climbing to a five-year high. All four major banks had forecast a hike. The probability of an increase rose in the latter part of October, particularly after Deputy Governor Andrew Hauser said on 21 October that the inflation outlook was materially worse than the Reserve Bank had previously forecast, citing higher energy costs, the AI investment boom and capacity constraints in the economy.

The data delivered the proof a week later. Underlying inflation, measured by the trimmed mean, accelerated to a two-year high of 3.7% in the third quarter, after spending more than five years above the central bank's 2% to 3% target band. The trimmed mean rose 1.0% over the September quarter, above the Reserve Bank's 0.8% forecast, taking the annual rate to 3.7%. Headline inflation rose to 4.3% over the 12 months to September, up from 4.0% in August, pushed up by a 9.4% jump in automotive fuel prices in the month alone, while housing-related inflation rose 5.2% over the year.

The Reserve Bank's updated forecasts, published in its November Statement on Monetary Policy, were materially worse than the August round. Headline and underlying inflation are now expected to stay above 3% until the end of 2027, reaching the target midpoint only in mid-2028, and inflation is not expected to return to the top of the 2-3% target range until late 2027, around three quarters later than previously forecast. The bank now expects headline inflation to hit 4.5% in the fourth quarter, and it revised its trimmed mean forecast for December 2026 up from 3.3% to 3.6%. Governor Michele Bullock told a Senate committee the week before the decision that inflation was tracking above the central bank's August forecast as some of the upside risks it had flagged materialised.

Given the revised inflation outlook and the continuing upside risks, the Board judged that a further tightening in financial conditions is warranted to support the return of inflation to target, the board said in a statement. Since the previous meeting, the data have confirmed that underlying inflation remains broadly based, and inflation is now expected to remain above 3% for the coming year with an increasing risk that higher inflation expectations will become embedded. Hauser also highlighted concerns that inflation expectations in the business sector were moving higher, describing higher inflation expectations as a central banker's nightmare, because prolonged above-target inflation erodes the credibility of the inflation target.

The Reserve Bank's 125 basis points of tightening this year has more than reversed the 75 basis points of policy easing from 2025, putting Australia at the hawkish end of developed market central banks. Elsewhere, the Federal Reserve stayed on hold this month while the Reserve Bank of New Zealand stunned markets by pausing in a razor-thin vote. The Reserve Bank had already lifted the cash rate four times this year, in February, March, May and September, taking it to 4.60% on 29 September in a unanimous vote, before the fifth rise in November.

Australian policymakers fear elevated oil prices could feed into broader price pressures in the economy and unmoor inflation expectations. Minutes of the November meeting, published later, revealed division on the board over the pace of interest-rate increases, but continued agreement on the need to be ready to tighten further if necessary. Members noted that inflation was still too high and forecast to remain so for another year, and still viewed the risks to the latest inflation forecast as being skewed to the upside. The board still senses that the economy is operating with excess demand, and inflation is not expected to return to its target of 2.5% until mid-2028.

The policy tightening has been felt most acutely in the housing market, which is on track for its biggest downturn in four decades. National home values fell another 1.2% in October, the seventh consecutive monthly decline, leaving values 6.7% below their March peak. Across the combined capital cities, values have dropped 8.1% in just seven months, the fastest fall over a comparable period in at least 40 years, and sales volumes are down almost 26% annually. Sydney, which still carries the deepest fall from its peak at 10.1%, slipped 1.6% in October and 4.7% over three months.

The single biggest driver of the downturn is the cost of money. Based on the average owner-occupier mortgage of almost $742,000, mortgage repayments are now around $600 a month, or 14%, higher than where they stood in January, and the borrowing capacity of the median-income household is around $88,000 lower, or 12%, than at the start of the year. Rate hikes have cut what a median-income household can borrow by more than the $63,400 drop in the national median value, so lower prices are not making homes more affordable. Combined with existing affordability constraints, cost-of-living pressures, pessimistic consumer confidence and the retreat of investors following May's Federal Budget, higher interest rates have driven potential buyers to the sidelines. The national vacancy rate is at 2.3%, its highest since January 2022, and national rents have climbed 52% over the past six years, adding about $245 a week to the median rent.

The labour market has also softened, with the unemployment rate hitting a five-year high of 4.7%, the highest since 2021, and annual jobs growth slowing to 1.2%, but the central bank still judges it to be a little tight and a source of inflationary pressures. Stephen Smith, a partner at Deloitte Access Economics, said the split 6-3 vote reflected the difficult trade-off facing policymakers as households cut spending and businesses grapple with softer demand, and Governor Bullock said it was unclear what the next move in interest rates would be, adding that the three dissenting members placed greater weight on downside risks as the housing market weakened and unemployment rose. Bullock said after five hikes it was probably a good time to take stock, while declining to rule out further interest rate increases, and she said the bank is not expecting the economy to go into a recession.

The challenge for the RBA is that inflation remains sticky just as growth momentum is beginning to slow, said Katherine Palmer, head of fixed income strategy at BlackRock Australia. With the full effect of this year's tightening still working its way through the economy, we expect the RBA to hold from here as it assesses incoming data and policy transmission. In her post-meeting press conference, Bullock acknowledged the difficulties higher interest rates create for households, noting that high inflation is particularly painful for those on low incomes, there is nowhere left to cut spending, and this is why getting inflation down matters so much, it affects everyone but the burden is not felt equally. Commonwealth Bank and National Australia Bank expect the Reserve Bank to pause after November, while Westpac sees a growing risk of another rise in February, when the board's next forecast round becomes the key checkpoint.
