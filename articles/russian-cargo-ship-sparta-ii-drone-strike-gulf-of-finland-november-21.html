Russian Cargo Ship Sparta II Hit by Drones in Gulf of Finland, Catches Fire as Finland Says Ukraine Likely Behind Attack

world · 21 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Russian cargo ship Sparta II, seen damaged and on fire in the Gulf of Finland on Saturday, 21 November 2026, after being struck by drones.

A Russian cargo ship with links to the military was hit by drones and caught fire in the Baltic Sea on Saturday, in what Finland said was likely an attack by Ukraine, and the vessel later drifted into Finnish waters. The incident, in the narrow Gulf of Finland between Finland and Estonia, is the latest sign of the war in Ukraine spilling beyond its borders — a day after Russian missiles and a drone landed on Polish soil during a massive strike on Ukraine.

The vessel is the Sparta II, a 121-metre (397-foot) Russian-flagged cargo ship owned by Oboronlogistika, a Russian shipping company that provides logistics services for the Russian Defence Ministry, according to the company's website. The ship is subject to United States sanctions, according to the US Treasury Department. It left St Petersburg on Friday, according to LSEG data, bound for Kaliningrad, the Russian exclave on the Baltic Sea that borders Poland and Lithuania.

Russian officials said the vessel was attacked by two drones in neutral waters in the Gulf of Finland, about three nautical miles from Russia's border. Aleksandr Drozdenko, the governor of the Leningrad region, said on Telegram that the crew was being evacuated and the extent of the damage was being assessed. He also said 80 drones had been shot down over the Leningrad region overnight, and lifted a drone threat warning for the region.

Finland's Defence Forces said the ship was damaged in international waters west of Russia's Gogland island, in the eastern Gulf of Finland, corroborating the earlier Russian account. The damaged vessel caught fire and drifted from international waters into Finnish territorial waters, where it anchored. The blaze was still ongoing late on Saturday, and Finland was leading the rescue operation, although Russian vessels may eventually tow the wreckage home, Finnish authorities said.

The 16-member crew of the Sparta II evacuated onto lifeboats and then onto a nearby Russian ship, and is on its way back to Russia, Finnish Border Guard Commander Ilja Iljin told public broadcaster Yle late on Saturday. The Finnish Border Guard said in a statement: "The situation poses no danger to other maritime traffic... Preparations for potential rescue operations are being made in cooperation with other authorities in Finland and internationally."

Helsinki said it had introduced temporary aviation and maritime restrictions in the eastern Gulf of Finland to protect civilians and allow the authorities to operate if drones needed to be intercepted. A Russian helicopter was suspected of briefly violating Finnish airspace during the rescue operation, said Mikko Simola, commander of the Gulf of Finland Coast Guard.

Finland's armed forces said it was likely that Ukraine was behind the attack. "It is likely that Ukraine ... is behind (the attack)," Finnish armed forces spokesperson Aki-Mauri Huhtinen told a press conference, adding that it was not clear why the vessel had been targeted. Ukraine's Foreign Ministry did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Russia's embassy in Helsinki did not immediately respond to requests for comment either.

What the ship was carrying remains unconfirmed. "At the moment, there is no confirmed information about what cargo is on board, but it cannot be ruled out that there is military materiel on board," Iljin of the Finnish Border Guard told Reuters. The governor of Kaliningrad, Alexei Besprozvannykh, said the incident posed no threat to supplies to the region, which relies on sea and land links through neighbouring states.

The reaction from the region's leaders was swift, and it framed the incident as part of a wider pattern rather than an isolated accident at sea. "The war is spilling over into our neighbouring areas. Finnish authorities are handling the situation in the Gulf of Finland, where a drone strike has hit a Russian merchant vessel. Ukraine has the right to fight against the aggressor," Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo wrote on X.

Estonia's Prime Minister Kristen Michal struck a similar note, writing on X: "The consequences of fighting Russia's aggression have reached the Baltic Sea. Another signal for the aggressor to stop. But nothing will stop us from supporting Ukraine." Estonia sent a navy vessel to assist in the rescue operation, including firefighting and pollution response, its government said.

Sweden was drawn in as well. Swedish combat aircraft, operating under Finnish command, took part in safeguarding Finland's and NATO's territorial integrity following the incident, Sweden's Defence Minister Pal Jonson said. Finland and Sweden are NATO's newest member states, joining the military alliance in 2023 and 2024 respectively, in response to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 — and the coordinated response on Saturday was an early test of how that new northern NATO flank works in practice.

The strike did not come out of nowhere. Ukraine has sent waves of military drones towards Russia's north-west Leningrad region this year, including some that strayed into the airspace of the Baltic region, leading to fears that the war was affecting NATO territory. Finland's military said last week it had detected a strike on a Russian warship in the same area during a wave of Ukrainian drone attacks on the Leningrad region. Saturday's attack on a merchant vessel extends that campaign from warships to the commercial shipping that sustains Russia's Baltic exclave.

That matters because of where the Sparta II was going, and what it represents. Kaliningrad, which lies between Poland and Lithuania, has periodically been a flashpoint between Russia and NATO and the European Union, and its supply lines — by sea through the Gulf of Finland and the Baltic, and by land through Lithuania — have been a recurring point of friction since the full-scale invasion began. A sanctioned vessel owned by a company that works for the Russian Defence Ministry, sailing a route that keeps the exclave supplied, is exactly the kind of target Ukraine's long-range drone campaign has been reaching for: economic and military logistics, far from the front line.

The episode also fits a pattern of escalation at sea and in the air that has accelerated sharply this week. On Thursday, Ukraine struck the Crimean Bridge for the third time in a week, in a drone attack that suspended rail services and damaged the road section. On Friday, Russia launched one of the largest missile and drone attacks of the war — more than 400 drones and over 110 missiles, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky — hitting bridges and energy infrastructure in Kyiv, and three Russian weapons, probably two missiles and a jet-powered drone, landed in Poland, prompting Warsaw to call the incursion an intentional provocation and to request additional NATO air defences. Saturday's burning ship in the Gulf of Finland is the third front of that same escalation, opening in the Baltic.

For Finland, the incident is a reminder of how close the war now sits to its own shores. The Gulf of Finland is a narrow arm of the Baltic Sea between Finland and Estonia that extends east to St Petersburg, and Gogland, the island near which the Sparta II was hit, lies in its eastern reaches. The temporary flight and shipping restrictions Helsinki imposed show how quickly a strike in international waters can become a domestic security operation, with a burning ship at anchor in Finnish waters, a suspected airspace violation by a Russian helicopter during the rescue, and allied aircraft scrambled under Finnish command.

Russia has previously described attacks on its commercial vessels outside its territorial waters as acts of terrorism and maritime piracy, and that framing is likely to shape Moscow's response. But no formal Russian statement on blame had been issued by late Saturday, and the question of who ordered the strike remains, officially, an assessment by Finland rather than a confirmed fact. What is confirmed is the damage: a sanctioned Russian cargo ship, on fire, at anchor in Finnish waters, its crew evacuated, and a Baltic Sea that is no longer a quiet backwater of the war.

Why it matters: the war in Ukraine has now produced, in the space of two days, Russian weapons landing on NATO soil in Poland, a third strike on the Crimean Bridge, and a burning Russian cargo ship drifting into the waters of a NATO member in the Baltic. Each incident on its own can be managed — no one was killed on the Sparta II, its crew got off safely, and Finland's rescue operation is under control. Together, they show a conflict that is widening geographically even as peace talks stall: Ukraine is taking the war to Russia's logistics far from the front, Russia is answering with mass strikes that keep spilling across borders, and the countries in between — Poland, Finland, Estonia, Sweden — are being pulled in, one incident at a time.
