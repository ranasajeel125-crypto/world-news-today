EU Energy Ministers to Meet as Fuel Prices Soar Ahead of Winter

European wholesale gas prices briefly touched €100/MWh on Wednesday, the first time since December 2022, as the EU enters the winter heating season with gas stocks at only 75% of available storage.

EU energy ministers met in Luxembourg on Thursday, 29 October, to discuss energy security ahead of the winter heating season as the wars in Iran, Ukraine and Yemen send oil and natural gas prices soaring. The meeting came as European wholesale gas prices briefly touched €100 per megawatt-hour on Wednesday, the first time since December 2022, when Europe was reeling from the loss of most of its Russian pipeline gas supply, and as the bloc enters winter with gas storage far below normal levels.

Fighting has damaged refineries across the Middle East and Russia, and hampered oil and gas flows through vital shipping lanes in the Black Sea, Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz. The war in the Middle East, which broke out on 28 February 2026, has disrupted global supply chains, with Gulf liquefied natural gas cargoes falling by more than 60 billion cubic metres between March and October compared with the same period in 2025, roughly four years of Belgium's total gas consumption. European gas prices, measured on the Dutch TTF benchmark, rose by 70 per cent over that period, exceeding €70 per megawatt-hour in August for the first time since January 2023, and are now trading at around triple pre-Iran war levels.

"We are in a very serious situation now. It does not look like we have a security of supply issue but I would warn against neglecting that," EU Energy Commissioner Dan Jorgensen told reporters on Wednesday. He said affordability was just as urgent an issue, arguing that available supply meant little to households and businesses that could not afford it. Jorgensen said nearly 50 million Europeans cannot adequately heat their homes and warned that number could rise as the bloc heads into what he called an even worse energy crisis than the one it faced four years ago. "We do not expect the security of supply issue as such. But for the citizens that are struggling to pay the bills, that might not matter," he said after the meeting.

The EU is entering the winter heating season with stocks at only 75% of available storage, versus 83% a year ago. The International Energy Agency said storage reservoirs were 75 per cent full at the end of October, well below the minimum target of 80 per cent fixed by the European Union and at the lowest level in more than a decade, the lowest since 2013. The amount of gas injected into storage this season was about 4 per cent below the average of the past five years, after buyers initially held off purchasing in the hope prices would fall. Deliveries stepped up in August, September and October, but not enough to make up for the delay. German gas storage facilities are on average only 61 per cent full, below a legally mandated target of 70 per cent by 1 November. Combined gas inventories across the European Union and the United Kingdom peaked at 853 terawatt-hours on 27 October, about 168 terawatt-hours below the ten-year seasonal average.

IEA executive director Fatih Birol warned on the sidelines of a conference in Berlin on 23 October that Europe is entering winter with historically low gas reserves and cannot withstand further supply shocks. "Normally before the winter, European countries have a lot of gas stocks, but the gas stocks we have while we are entering this winter are much lower than historical averages," he said, adding that Europe's gas is competing with that of Asian countries, which are even more dependent on the Middle East. He said the crisis shows how closely energy and geopolitics are now tied, and that the IEA does not expect real relief in liquefied natural gas supply before the end of the decade.

Diesel is a particular problem. The EU's ageing refineries have processed less crude in recent years, and the bloc faces shortages of imported refined products such as the diesel that powers its trucking, agricultural and industrial sectors. Jorgensen said a series of refinery closures in EU member states over the past 20 years was a mistake, as the diesel squeeze pushes prices to all-time highs. Birol said Ukraine's attacks on Russian refineries were also behind the looming diesel scarcity. The Commission began talks with European refineries on Wednesday to find ways to boost output, including through the possible easing of some technical specifications.

Western powers also agreed this month to accelerate the release of over 400 million barrels of emergency oil stocks after US pressure. The Group of Seven agreed in March to release 400 million barrels of crude and diesel from strategic reserves, an effort coordinated by Birol, his third emergency stock release since Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022. On 2 October, the G7 agreed to speed up the release of up to 100 million barrels over four months under IEA oversight, and Birol credited the move with a 20 dollar fall in oil prices. About 330 million barrels had been released by 15 October, and the EU still holds about 80 per cent of its emergency stocks.

At Thursday's meeting, a dozen European countries, including France, Germany and Italy, pledged to jointly release more than 42 million barrels of oil in the coming months, Jorgensen said, including 20.5 million barrels of diesel, to be released by the end of January 2027. France moves first, with 10 million barrels of diesel it had already announced reaching the market within days, sold at cost. The release works out to about 460,000 barrels per day, 230,000 of them diesel, and is part of the G7's October agreement rather than an additional round of stockpile draws.

EU countries will also discuss a Commission proposal to cut electricity bills through tax and grid fee reforms, such as by taxing electricity at a lower rate than natural gas. Lowering electricity bills offers short-term relief and encourages electrification, insulating Europe from soaring fossil fuel prices, and Jorgensen said any such help should be "targeted and temporary". But ministers at the summit broadly acknowledged that Brussels and national governments had few remaining options to contain prices. "We've exhausted most of our short-term options," one senior European energy official said. Ministers rejected calls to ask households to cut energy use, and Irish Energy Minister Darragh O'Brien, whose government holds the EU's six-month rotating presidency, said: "The EU and member states don't have a magic wand to make the price increases go away." The focus, he said, was on moving from short-term steps to "more sustainable measures". "There's no silver bullet, but when you add all of these things up, it can actually matter," Jorgensen said. Work continues on the review of the EU's emissions trading system and the post-2030 energy framework, and EU leaders meet again on 17-18 December.
