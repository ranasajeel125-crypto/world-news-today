Protests Sweep European Capitals as Anger Over Living Costs Boils Over

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

Thousands fill a European city square during Saturday's continent-wide day of demonstrations over economic policies, rising living costs and proposed welfare cuts.

Demonstrators battled police and set fires in cities across Europe on Saturday, in one of the widest days of unrest the continent has seen this year. Protests against government economic policies, rising living costs and proposed cuts to social welfare drew crowds in Belgium, Italy, Spain and Albania, and in several cities the marches ended in clashes.

In Brussels, protesters gathered at Albertine Square to voice opposition to the federal government's economic policies and planned budget cuts to social welfare programmes, before the demonstration was broken up by police intervention. In Italy, Milan was placed on particularly high alert, with Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni deploying 500 soldiers into the city alongside drone surveillance, railway station checks and rapid-response units.

Protests turned violent in Rome, where demonstrators threw bottles and launched fireworks at police officers, and street signs were torn down and defaced. In Turin, police deployed tear gas after protesters threw stones, bottles and smoke bombs, and there were further clashes in Florence. The unrest prompted the US Embassy and Consulates in Italy to urge Americans to avoid Milan's Piazza Duomo and major commercial streets.

In Spain, more than 25,000 people took to the streets of Madrid demanding pension system reform, while masked demonstrators in Barcelona smashed a glass kiosk and started fires. In Tirana, Albania, a protester was pictured firing fireworks during an anti-government protest by students. EuroNews reported that jihadist groups had called for attacks on demonstrators and police during demonstrations in Spain and Portugal, adding a security dimension to an already tense weekend.

Governments across the continent now face a common problem: electorates squeezed by years of high living costs are losing patience with austerity budgets, and the street is becoming the loudest opposition. Whether Saturday's unrest was a one-day release of anger or the start of a longer season of protest will become clear in the weeks ahead.
