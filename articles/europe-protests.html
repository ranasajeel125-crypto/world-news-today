Protests Sweep European Capitals as Anger Over Living Costs Boils Over

world · 25 Oct 2026 · 7 min read · By Maria Santos (Europe Correspondent)

Thousands fill a European city square during Saturday's continent-wide day of demonstrations over economic policies, rising living costs and proposed welfare cuts.

Demonstrators battled police and set fires in cities across Europe on Saturday, in one of the widest days of unrest the continent has seen this year. Protests against government economic policies, rising living costs and proposed cuts to social welfare drew crowds in Belgium, Italy, Spain and Albania, and in several cities the marches ended in clashes.

In Brussels, protesters gathered at Albertine Square to voice opposition to the federal government's economic policies and planned budget cuts to social welfare programmes, before the demonstration was broken up by police intervention. In Italy, Milan was placed on particularly high alert, with Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni deploying 500 soldiers into the city alongside drone surveillance, railway station checks and rapid-response units.

Protests turned violent in Rome, where demonstrators threw bottles and launched fireworks at police officers, and street signs were torn down and defaced. In Turin, police deployed tear gas after protesters threw stones, bottles and smoke bombs, and there were further clashes in Florence. The unrest prompted the US Embassy and Consulates in Italy to urge Americans to avoid Milan's Piazza Duomo and major commercial streets.

In Spain, more than 25,000 people took to the streets of Madrid demanding pension system reform, while masked demonstrators in Barcelona smashed a glass kiosk and started fires. In Tirana, Albania, a protester was pictured firing fireworks during an anti-government protest by students.

Governments across the continent now face a common problem: electorates squeezed by years of high living costs are losing patience with austerity budgets, and the street is becoming the loudest opposition. Whether Saturday's unrest was a one-day release of anger or the start of a longer season of protest will become clear in the weeks ahead.

Saturday, October 24, was the second weekend in a row of widespread protests across Europe. Hundreds of thousands of people took to the streets across France, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Albania and Belgium, and the demonstrations reflected a range of grievances: rising living costs, housing affordability, low wages, education, deteriorating public services and opposition to government policies. France reported nearly 1,000 arrests as protests continued across the country, while Italy deployed additional soldiers to Milan to prevent a repeat of the previous weekend's violence.

The protests were not a single coordinated movement. Analysts described them as a series of parallel movements arising from shared concerns about living costs, educational standards and economic and political dissatisfaction. The demands differed by country, but the reasons people marched looked alike, and the movements have started to watch and inspire one another: Italian demonstrators referred to the French protests in their slogans, the Spanish mobilisation for affordable housing found an echo in Portugal, and student organisations in different countries swapped experience.

In France, hundreds of thousands of people had been taking to the streets for several weeks as discontent spread from conditions in schools to the high cost of living. In Paris, the march on October 24 was mostly peaceful, but near its end masked groups threw objects and fireworks at police, who responded with tear gas and water cannon.

Belgium's protests were student-led. Students in Brussels and Liege demonstrated against rising education costs, including university registration fees, and police used tear gas and water cannon. On Saturday, October 24, hundreds of people gathered in Brussels to protest the federal government's economic policies, the cost of living and social cuts, and to call for better conditions in education. The Belgian student movement began in early October over rising education costs, overcrowded classrooms and reforms that students said could make universities less accessible, and it has since merged with wider protests against the austerity plans of Prime Minister Bart De Wever's government. In early October, around 30,000 people took part in a nationwide union demonstration in Brussels. Belgium's deficit is about 5 percent of gross domestic product, and its debt stands at 110.2 percent.

Italy had been building towards the weekend for more than a week. It began with "No Meloni Day" on October 16, when students demonstrated in Milan, Turin, Bologna and Rome, and clashes followed in Milan, Turin and Bologna on October 17. Demands that started with school conditions widened to low wages, prices and diesel costs, and road hauliers and farmers joined in. On October 20, Meloni approved tougher anti-riot measures, and after Milan police detained 45 people the previous week, 34 of them minors, she announced tougher penalties for underage offenders.

On October 24, thousands of people took part in marches in Italian cities including Milan, Rome, Turin, Bologna and Florence. Their demands included action on the cost of living, changes to government policies, and opposition to wars and rearmament. Milan was placed on heightened alert after looting and clashes had marred demonstrations the previous weekend, and hundreds of soldiers and thousands of police patrolled the city, with shopkeepers boarding up the windows of luxury boutiques. The march in Milan remained largely peaceful: demonstrators chanted slogans against Meloni and carried banners criticising the government, and some dumped manure outside Italy's energy regulator and burned a symbolic oversized electricity bill to draw attention to energy costs.

In Rome, however, demonstrations turned violent as police used tear gas and water cannon against protesters. Some demonstrators threw bottles and fireworks at officers, while street signs were damaged. In Turin, police used tear gas after demonstrators threw stones, bottles and smoke bombs at officers, and in Bologna bottles were thrown and police intervened. Italy's debt is 139.9 percent of GDP, the second-highest in the eurozone after Greece, though its deficit is improving at around 3 percent.

In Spain, more than 25,000 people marched peacefully in Madrid on Saturday to demand safeguards for the public pension system and oppose possible privatisation, voicing concerns about retirement security amid a wider debate over pension reform ahead of a snap election scheduled for the following month. Their demands included increases linked to inflation, a higher minimum pension and a reduction in the gap between the benefits received by men and women. In Barcelona, hooded demonstrators looted shops and set fire to street furniture around the central Placa de Catalunya, and approximately 100 people were arrested.

Portugal's issue was housing. On October 17, tens of thousands marched in Lisbon, led by the CGTP union, over living costs, low wages and the housing crisis, and set up a tent camp in Rossio Square. Portuguese house prices have more than doubled since 2017. On October 24, thousands of people marched in Lisbon and Porto to demand action against the high cost of homes and rents, opposing government measures intended to ease rent increases and make it easier to evict tenants who fail to pay.

In Tirana, thousands of people gathered on October 24 for an anti-government protest supported by students, teachers, artists and opposition parties. Students called for lower university costs, greater public investment in education and measures to combat corruption. In Athens, students pitched tents outside the finance ministry and parliament over student housing costs, while in Germany and Austria only early signs of organisation were reported. The same day, thousands of people filled the squares of Czech cities to protest against plans to abolish licence fees and out of concern for the future of public media.

Four shared pressures bind these countries. The first is debt: eurozone government debt rose to 88.8 percent of gross domestic product in the second quarter of 2026. The second is prices and housing, with rents and property prices rising faster than wages in many cities. The third is the strain on public services, from schools to pensions. And the fourth is a political mood in which electorates squeezed by years of high living costs are losing patience with austerity budgets, and the street is becoming the loudest opposition. Whether the October 24 unrest was a one-day release of anger or the start of a longer season of protest will become clear in the weeks ahead — but the weekend showed that the discontent is now continental in scale.
