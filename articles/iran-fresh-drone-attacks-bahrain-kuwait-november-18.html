Iran Launches Fresh Drone Attacks on Bahrain and Kuwait as Gulf Conflict Widens

world · 18 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Bahrain issued two threat alerts telling residents to seek shelter before announcing the all-clear, while Kuwait reported its air defences were engaging hostile drones for the first time in this round of fighting.

Bahrain’s air defences intercepted a fresh round of Iranian aerial attacks on Wednesday, a day after the United States and Iran traded strikes, and Kuwait said its air defences were also countering hostile drones — widening the Gulf conflict to a second state that hosts American forces, Reuters reported.

The Bahrain Defence Force said its air defence systems had repelled “a number of Iranian air attacks” on Wednesday, accusing Tehran of maintaining a “systematic hostile approach” of targeting the kingdom, which hosts the headquarters of the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet. Bahrain’s Interior Ministry issued two threat alerts during the morning, urging residents to move to the nearest safe location, before announcing an all-clear after each warning. No casualties or damage from Wednesday’s attacks had been reported.

Separately, Kuwait’s air defences were also countering hostile drone attacks early on Wednesday, the Kuwaiti army said in a statement, without providing further details. It was the first time Kuwait has reported coming under attack in this round of fighting, and it puts a second Gulf Arab state that hosts US forces on the receiving end of Iranian strikes.

Wednesday’s attacks came a day after the most direct exchange of the conflict so far. On Tuesday, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it had launched drones at a US military radar system at Bahrain’s Sheikh Isa Air Base, in response to what it described as continued violations of Iranian airspace by US military drones. Bahrain and the United States said the attack was intercepted, and hours later US fighter jets struck an Iranian missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, in what Washington described as self-defence.

The new round makes it four Iranian attacks on Bahrain in six days. The Revolutionary Guard said on Friday that it had targeted a US early-warning radar in the kingdom, and on Sunday that six of its drones had targeted the US air-defence radar at the al-Riffa helicopter base. Bahrain said its air defences intercepted both attacks, and sirens sounded across the capital, Manama, on Sunday. Tuesday’s strike on the Sheikh Isa radar was the third, and Wednesday’s attacks the fourth — each one answered, so far, only by interception or by a limited American strike on Qeshm.

The fighting is also spreading beyond the two Gulf states. Yemen’s Iran-aligned Houthis said on Tuesday they had launched five drones towards two pumping stations along Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, which Riyadh has used during the war to reroute oil to its Yanbu port on the Red Sea, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. The pipeline is one of the main alternatives to the strait, the world’s most important oil chokepoint, and any threat to it raises the stakes for global energy supplies.

Oil markets reflected the strain. Brent crude was trading at $92.98 a barrel and US West Texas Intermediate at $85.45 in early trading on Wednesday, Reuters reported, after both benchmarks settled higher on Tuesday as the US-Iran conflict intensified. Attacks on ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz have continued alongside the strikes on land: an LPG tanker was hit by a projectile while sailing through the strait off Oman on Monday, and its crew had to evacuate, and Iranian naval mines have struck tankers in the area in recent days.

The conflict erupted on 28 February after US and Israeli attacks on Iran, and has since widened to include Iranian strikes on Israel and on Gulf Arab states hosting US military bases. Talks between Iran and mediators Oman and Qatar were continuing, Reuters reported, but the political space for a deal has narrowed sharply: President Donald Trump said last week that the United States was no longer interested in a deal and ordered Vice-President JD Vance, who had been leading efforts to restart negotiations, to turn his attention to domestic politics instead.

What happens next will turn on whether Washington answers this latest round. No American response to Wednesday’s attacks on Bahrain and Kuwait had been reported by the time of publication, and the pattern so far has been limited: interception on the Gulf side, and a single retaliatory strike on a missile site on Qeshm. But each round has been wider than the last — from a radar in Bahrain, to a second Bahraini base, to a missile site in Iran, and now to Kuwait as well — and the alerts telling Bahraini civilians to take shelter show the fighting is no longer confined to military installations.

Why it matters: for two months the confrontation was fought mostly at sea, through mines, seizures and a blockade. This week it has become a daily exchange of drones and jets over Gulf capitals, and on Wednesday it crossed into a second country. The competing accounts — Tehran claiming radars targeted, Manama and Washington claiming every drone intercepted, and both sides giving different accounts of the damage — show how hard it has become to establish what is actually happening in a war fought increasingly through claims on Telegram and denials from military commands. Until independent evidence emerges, the one fact both sides agree on is the most important one: the drones are flying again, the jets are answering, and the map of the war is getting bigger.
