Kenyan Doomsday Cult Leader Paul Mackenzie Sentenced to 200 Years Over Shakahola Deaths

A Kenyan court has sentenced self-proclaimed pastor Paul Mackenzie to 200 years in prison for the murder of 191 people — 190 children and one adult — in the Shakahola starvation cult tragedy, handing down two 100-year terms to run consecutively. The sentence, delivered on Friday, 6 November, at the High Court in Mombasa, is one of the longest ever imposed by a Kenyan court, and it closes the main chapter of a case that has shocked the country for more than three years.

Justice Diana Kavedza of the High Court in Mombasa imposed 100 years for the murder of 183 people by starvation — 182 children and one adult — and a further 100 years for eight counts of murder by direct homicide involving child victims, including strangulation, suffocation and blunt-force injuries. The sentences in each category will run concurrently, but the two sets of sentences will run consecutively, giving an aggregate term of 200 years. Prosecutors had sought the death penalty, but Justice Kavedza said she did not impose it because it could strengthen the convicts' belief that dying would reunite them with Jesus, rather than deter them. Mackenzie, wearing a grey polo shirt and black trousers, smiled as he entered a tent set up outside the court where the hearing was held, and did not react as the judge read out his sentence.

Mackenzie, 54, was convicted on October 19 along with 28 co-accused on all 191 counts of murder. The court also sentenced the 28 co-accused, with their terms set according to their individual roles and levels of participation, ranging from 10 to 160 years. The deaths covered by the conviction took place between January 2021 and September 2023, and the trial heard testimony from around 120 prosecution witnesses. The judge said Mackenzie had won the trust of his victims through his charismatic media presence, broadcasting sermons on a television channel and a YouTube channel that had thousands of subscribers, and had then targeted defenceless, vulnerable followers, particularly children.

The court found that Mackenzie, leader of the Good News International Church, instructed followers to starve themselves and their children to death in the belief that they would "meet Jesus" before the end of the world. Justice Kavedza rejected the defence claim that the victims had fasted voluntarily. His teachings centred on doomsday predictions, and according to a Kenyan Senate report, he told followers the world would end in August 2023, announcing at one point that the apocalypse would arrive on 15 April 2023 and accelerating preparations for what became a mass-death campaign. The movement denounced formal education as "satanic", regarded modern medicine and hospitals as instruments of the devil, and required followers to cut ties with the outside world.

Mackenzie, a former taxi driver who described himself as a pastor, founded the Good News International Church in Malindi in 2003. As early as 2017, police raided his compound and rescued dozens of children, some of whom said they had abandoned their families and schooling under his influence. In 2019, authorities shut down his church in Malindi after allegations of the church's involvement in the deaths of children, and Mackenzie was arrested and released on bond. He then relocated the church to the remote Shakahola Forest, to an 800-acre property named the Chakama Ranch, where followers were persuaded to abandon their homes and move to the forest, promised that fasting there would bring them closer to God and prepare them for heaven.

The tragedy came to light in 2023, when investigators began recovering bodies from shallow graves in Shakahola Forest in Kilifi County, near the coastal town of Malindi. The case first came to attention in March 2023, when a man approached police and reported that his brother and sister-in-law had starved their children to death in the forest on Mackenzie's instructions. Mackenzie was detained that month over allegations that he had encouraged parents to kill their children through starvation and suffocation. On 14 April 2023, police raided the compound after receiving a tip-off, rescued 15 severely malnourished followers, four of whom died before reaching hospital, and began discovering the graves of dozens of others. Eight additional survivors died in the following month from the effects of extreme malnutrition.

More than 400 bodies have since been recovered from the forest, though the murder trial covered 191 deaths. The Kenya National Commission on Human Rights put the overall death toll at the Shakahola site at 429 in 2024, and the total has since risen further, with hundreds more reported missing. Many of the bodies were so badly decomposed that forensic teams could identify only 26 victims. The Kenyan Red Cross reported on 30 April 2023 that 410 individuals, including 227 minors, were missing. Among the bereaved was Stephen Mwiti, 48, the father of six of Mackenzie's child victims and the husband of one of his convicted accomplices, who said his wife had taken the children to Shakahola, where they were starved to death.

Mackenzie faces separate proceedings on terrorism-related and manslaughter charges, which he denies. Kenya has not carried out an execution since 1987. Prosecutors have also linked him to the deaths of 52 people whose bodies were discovered in Kwa Binzaro, a village about 30 kilometres from Shakahola, and additional charges were filed this year in connection with those deaths, so his legal troubles are far from over. The discovery of the graves in 2023 prompted intense criticism of law enforcement, administrative authorities and the regulatory framework governing religious organisations in Kenya, and a Senate committee documented allegations of coercion, intimidation, child deaths and the concealment of bodies. For the families of the victims, the 200-year sentence is a measure of justice, though, as one relative told reporters, no punishment could ever compensate for the loss of a child.
