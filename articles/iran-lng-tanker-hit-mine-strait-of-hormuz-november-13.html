Iran Says LNG Tanker Hit Mine and Suffered Explosion in Strait of Hormuz

world · 13 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Strait of Hormuz is a vital waterway for global shipping; Iranian media said four oil tankers were also hit by explosions there on Thursday.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards navy said a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas struck a naval mine and suffered an explosion in the Strait of Hormuz on Friday while using what they described as an "unauthorised and dangerous route," state media reported.

No details on the tanker were immediately available. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said the vessel had ignored warnings and attempted to navigate a route through the waterway that Iran had not authorised. Al Jazeera reported that the liquefied gas carrier caught fire after hitting naval mines, and Iranian state media broadcast footage, released by the Revolutionary Guards, showing a ship burning in the strait.

The incident came a day after Iranian media reported that four oil tankers were hit by explosions in the waterway on Thursday, adding that the vessels had ignored warnings and entered what Iran considers restricted areas of the strategic waterway. Tankers are still facing attacks across the strait, despite a rebound in traffic and expectations of a de-escalation in the US-Israeli war on Iran, expectations that have been reflected in falling oil prices.

At least 10 ships were hit in the first 10 days of November, after 37 attacks in October, which was the highest monthly tally since the conflict began in late February, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy. The pace of attacks in November suggests the danger to shipping has not eased, even as traffic through the strait has recovered and markets have begun to price in a lower risk of a wider war.

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the most important waterways in the world for energy. It is the narrow channel between Iran and Oman through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas must pass, connecting the oil-producing states of the Gulf with the open ocean. Roughly a fifth of the world's petroleum liquids pass through the strait, and for the gas-exporting states of the Gulf, above all Qatar, it is the only sea route out. Any sustained threat to shipping there raises insurance costs, pushes up freight rates and feeds directly into global energy prices.

Liquefied natural gas, or LNG, is natural gas that has been cooled to about minus 162 degrees Celsius until it becomes a liquid, shrinking its volume to roughly one six-hundredth of its size as a gas so that it can be carried in specially built tankers. Qatar is the world's largest LNG exporter, and its shipments, along with cargoes from other Gulf producers, pass through Hormuz on their way to buyers in Asia and Europe. Kpler data showed that exports of liquefied natural gas via the Strait of Hormuz last month reached the highest total since February, at 31 tankers, compared with 23 in September, a sign that exporters had been returning to normal operations despite the security risks.

Naval mines are among the oldest and cheapest weapons of sea warfare, and also among the hardest to defend against. A mine anchored to the seabed or floating just below the surface can wait silently for weeks or months before a passing ship triggers it, and clearing a mined waterway is slow, dangerous work that requires specialised minesweepers. The use of mines in a narrow, congested channel like Hormuz is particularly disruptive, because ships have little room to manoeuvre around a suspected minefield and because even an unconfirmed mine sighting can force a channel to close while it is investigated.

Iran has long threatened to close the Strait of Hormuz in response to attacks on its territory, and the waterway has been the scene of repeated confrontations. During the so-called tanker war of the 1980s, in the Iran-Iraq war, dozens of ships were attacked in the Gulf. In more recent years, vessels have been seized, sabotaged and struck by drones and missiles, and the United States and its allies have maintained a naval presence in the region to protect commercial shipping. The current round of attacks began in late February, when the US-Israeli war on Iran started, and it has made the strait the most dangerous stretch of water for merchant shipping anywhere in the world.

The pattern of the recent attacks is striking. The vessels hit have, according to Iranian media, been accused of ignoring warnings and entering areas Iran considers restricted, or of using routes Iran had not authorised. That framing suggests the attacks are not random but are aimed at enforcing Iranian control over which ships may use the waterway and on what terms. For shipowners and charterers, the message is that passage through Hormuz now depends on more than navigation: it depends on political calculations about which routes are safe and which flags, cargoes and destinations might draw attention.

For the shipping industry, the consequences are practical and immediate. War-risk insurance premiums for transits through the strait have risen sharply, and some owners have rerouted cargoes or delayed sailings. LNG carriers are particularly sensitive, because their cargo is carried at very low temperatures in specialised tanks, and a fire on board an LNG carrier is among the most feared events in shipping. The fact that an LNG carrier was hit at all, rather than an oil tanker, is likely to sharpen concern among gas buyers in Asia and Europe, who depend on Gulf supplies.

The attacks also complicate the diplomatic picture. Falling oil prices in recent weeks have reflected market expectations that the US-Israeli war on Iran might be de-escalating, and traffic through the strait had rebounded on that basis. A fresh round of attacks, including one on a gas carrier, tests that optimism. If the attacks continue, they could push prices back up and force governments to weigh whether to increase naval protection for shipping, to press for a ceasefire, or to accept a higher level of risk in one of the world's most important energy corridors.

What happens next will depend on whether the attacks continue and on whether any ship is lost or any crew is killed. So far, the reports have not given casualty figures for the vessels hit, and no details of the LNG tanker have been released. But the sheer number of incidents, at least 10 in the first 10 days of November after 37 in October, suggests a sustained campaign rather than isolated accidents. For the crews who sail through Hormuz, for the energy markets that depend on it, and for the governments watching from the Gulf, Washington and beyond, the strait remains the place where a regional war can most quickly become a global economic shock.
