Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Plant Loses External Power Again, Runs on Diesel Generators

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The International Atomic Energy Agency said the plant's fuel reserves can run a limited number of emergency diesel generators for about two weeks; Ukraine's nuclear operator warned a prolonged blackout risks a radiation accident.

Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia nuclear power plant lost all external electricity early on Sunday, leaving emergency diesel generators to cool its reactors and run other essential safety systems, the plant's management and the UN's nuclear watchdog said.

The 330 kV Ferosplavna-1 transmission line, the plant's only remaining connection to the grid, was disconnected at 02:36 on Sunday after protective systems shut it down because of damage, the Russian-installed management of the plant said. An attempt to reconnect the line automatically failed. Energoatom, Ukraine's state nuclear operator, said the line was switched back on at 17:41 the same day, so the plant ran on diesel for about 15 hours, while the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), which has a team stationed at the plant, said all off-site power was lost for more than 12 hours due to damage to the Ferosplavna-1 line. The IAEA confirmed about ten hours after the outage was first reported that power had been restored.

Backup diesel generators started as designed and are supplying power to the equipment required for safe operation of the station, the management said in a statement posted on the Russian state-backed messenger app MAX, adding that radiation levels at the site and in the surveillance zone were normal. Four emergency diesel generators started automatically, and operators later reduced the number running to two to conserve fuel. The IAEA said the cause and location of the latest disconnection are not yet known.

The agency said information given to its team showed that existing fuel reserves can run a limited number of emergency diesel generators for about two weeks. IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi called for the grid connection to be restored and for diesel stocks at the plant to be increased, stressing the urgency of keeping the plant's cooling systems running. The IAEA said on 6 November that it had not been told of any diesel delivery to the plant since March, and that fuel was still being consumed, though more slowly than usual.

Ukraine's nuclear operator, Energoatom, warned that operating in blackout mode creates a direct risk of failure of life-support systems and nuclear fuel cooling if the diesel generators break down or exhaust their fuel, which could lead to a radiation accident. European nuclear regulators warned on 16 November of escalating accident risks in Ukraine this winter, noting that the country's fourth plant, Zaporizhzhia, remains under Russian occupation and has not generated electricity since 2022. The IAEA also reported that a backup transformer near the adjacent Zaporizhzhia thermal power plant, previously repaired during a ceasefire negotiated by the agency, had again been destroyed by military activity.

It was the 27th complete loss of off-site power at the facility since the conflict began, the IAEA said, and the 16th since the start of 2026, according to Energoatom. "It is the 27th such event during the conflict, underlining the continued precarious nuclear safety situation," IAEA Director General Rafael Mariano Grossi said about the latest power cut. The agency said on 23 October that the plant's main Dniprovska power line remained unavailable. Before the Russian invasion in February 2022, the plant had ten power lines; it now relies solely on the Ferosplavna-1 line, its one remaining external connection, which it has depended on since late March. In early September, a local ceasefire brokered by the IAEA took effect to allow repairs to restore external power to Zaporizhzhia, which at that time had been without it since 20 August, and the IAEA has asked both governments to agree its ninth local ceasefire around the plant.

Zaporizhzhia, Europe's largest nuclear power plant, has been under Russian control since March 2022 and produces no electricity. All six of its reactors have been in cold shutdown since 2022, but the plant still needs external power to keep its nuclear fuel cool. The plant, which supplied about 30 per cent of Ukraine's electricity before Russia's invasion in February 2022, was seized by Russian forces in the first days of March 2022 and stopped generating electricity for the national grid in September 2022. Its six Soviet-designed VVER-1000 water-cooled, water-moderated reactors were all built in the 1980s, though the sixth only came online in the mid-1990s, after the collapse of the Soviet Union. The last unit was placed in cold shutdown in April 2024, meaning all six reactors are now in that state, which reduces the temperature and pressure of the reactors and provides an additional buffer in case of an accident.

Cooling fuel even in reactors in cold shutdown is necessary to prevent a nuclear meltdown, and pumped water is also used to cool down removed spent nuclear fuel from the reactors. Without enough water, or power to pump the water, the fuel could melt down and the zirconium cladding could release hydrogen, which can explode. Besides the reactors, there is also a dry spent fuel storage facility at the site for used nuclear fuel assemblies, and spent fuel pools at each reactor site that are used to cool down the used nuclear fuel. The IAEA has kept a permanent team at the site since 1 September 2022, when it established its Support and Assistance Mission to Zaporizhzhia to assess physical damage to the facilities and determine whether the main and back-up safety and security systems were functional.

At a 30 May 2023 Security Council briefing, Grossi presented five concrete principles needed to ensure nuclear safety and security at the plant, which he asked both parties to observe: avoiding the use of the plant as a launch site for attacks or as a target; ensuring that the plant is not used for storing heavy weapons or as a base for military personnel; safeguarding off-site power sources; protecting all crucial structures, systems and components from attacks or sabotage; and refraining from any actions that would compromise these principles. The agency has highlighted the ongoing hostilities jeopardising its seven indispensable pillars for ensuring nuclear safety and security in an armed conflict, which include physical integrity and reliable external power, and its governing body has adopted several resolutions calling on Russia to withdraw its military and other personnel from the plant.
