Trump Names Stephen Miller as New White House Chief of Staff, Replacing Susie Wiles

politics · 6 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Miller, 41, the architect of the president's hardline immigration agenda, takes over on Monday; Wiles, who resigned on Thursday, moves to Trump's political operation.

politics · 6 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

President Donald Trump said on Friday he was promoting deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller to become White House chief of staff, with current chief of staff Susie Wiles moving over to his political operation. The change takes effect on Monday, November 9.

In a post on Truth Social, Trump called Miller "one of my most trusted advisors" and praised Wiles for doing "an outstanding job". He wrote: "I am pleased to announce that Mr. Stephen Miller, the current Deputy Chief of Staff of the Trump Administration, and one of my most trusted advisors, will become the Chief of Staff starting Monday, November 9, 2026." He added that Wiles would be moving over to the Trump political operation and thanked her for her service as chief of staff.

Wiles submitted her resignation on Thursday evening, according to people familiar with the matter, and said in a statement on Friday that it was her last day as White House chief of staff. She was the first woman to hold the post and had served since the start of Trump's second term, after leading his successful 2024 presidential campaign. Wiles, 69, had previously worked on Trump's three campaigns before being tapped as chief of staff. She handed her resignation to Trump in the Oval Office on Thursday, and she had always said she would remain at the White House until the end of Trump's second term. As it became clear the president was unhappy with her, she decided to resign, believing he should be able to tap someone to lead his White House who had his confidence, according to a person familiar with her thinking.

Miller, 41, has served as deputy chief of staff for policy and Homeland Security adviser since January 2025, and has been the architect of the president's hardline immigration agenda, including the deportation policies at the centre of his second term. He is one of Trump's longest-serving and most trusted advisers, dating back to his first campaign. As deputy chief of staff he widened his portfolio over the past year to include wide swaths of domestic policy, and he has advocated for an expansive view of executive power. He is a staunch supporter of Israel and a firebrand policy hand, and his promotion puts one of the administration's most ideological figures in charge of running the White House.

The move is the first major personnel shuffle since Tuesday's midterm elections, in which Democrats won control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, dealing Republicans their worst midterm defeat since the Watergate era. Trump, who initially appeared unfazed by the results, has become angrier as he has watched coverage of the historic losses, according to people familiar with his response, and the president signalled on Friday that the shake-up would not be the last, telling CBS News "I do" when asked if he intended to fire more people. The change came after Trump voiced fury at the strategy that led to Republicans decisively losing majorities in the House and Senate.

Miller said on X that it had been "the privilege of my lifetime" to serve on Trump's team from day one, adding that he was "honored beyond words to serve as his White House Chief of Staff." His appointment was described by the Wall Street Journal as Trump doubling down and digging in: rather than moving to the centre after a midterm defeat, the president was putting his most loyal and most hardline adviser in charge of the White House.

The chief of staff is one of the most powerful jobs in American government. The holder controls access to the president, manages the flow of paper and people into the Oval Office, coordinates policy across departments, and enforces discipline among staff. In Trump's first term, the job changed hands repeatedly, and the turnover became a symbol of the chaos of that administration. In his second term, Wiles was credited with bringing a measure of order and discipline to the White House, drawing on her experience running the 2024 campaign. Her departure, and Miller's promotion, therefore marks a change in style as well as personnel: from a campaign manager who prized order to a policy ideologue who prizes loyalty and aggression.

Wiles is the latest in a series of prominent women to leave the administration, including Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, Attorney General Pam Bondi and Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer, and she is the latest high-ranking woman to be replaced by a man. Her departure also came months after she indicated she no longer showed evidence of breast cancer after months of treatment for the disease, a diagnosis she went public with in March 2026.

What Miller does with the job will shape the second half of Trump's second term. With Democrats now in control of both chambers of Congress, the administration faces investigations, subpoenas and a legislative blockade, and the White House will need to manage a far more hostile environment than it has faced so far. Miller's record suggests he will meet that challenge with confrontation rather than compromise, and his first days in the job will be watched closely for signs of how the administration intends to govern with a divided Congress.
