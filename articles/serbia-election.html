Serbia Votes in Tight Race Between Vučić and Student-Led Movement

politics · 25 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Elena Petrova (Balkans Correspondent)

The National Assembly in Belgrade. Serbia's vote is being watched closely across Europe.

Serbia voted on Sunday in a snap parliamentary election that has turned into one of the most consequential votes in the country's recent history, with longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic conceding defeat to a student-led anti-corruption movement that has shaken the country's politics.

Vucic said on Sunday evening that, based on a sample of votes, the Student List was expected to win between 135 and 140 seats in the 250-member National Assembly, compared with around 105 for his own electoral list. "It is clear that we have lost these elections, the Student List has won," he said in a televised speech, an announcement greeted with cheers, euphoria and tears at the Student List headquarters, according to the Belgrade-based FoNet news agency. Within moments of his announcement, the streets of Belgrade erupted in celebration, with tens of thousands of people thronging the city's main boulevards, honking horns and chanting.

The Republic Election Commission later announced that the Student List had won 52.54 percent of the vote, based on 8,077 processed polling stations out of 8,392, while the United Serbia list, led by Vucic's Serbian Progressive Party, had secured 39.29 percent. Voter turnout stood at 68.78 percent, a record high in more than 30 years, according to the commission's data. The figures mean the Student List will be able to form a majority in Serbia's 250-seat parliament on its own, with the bloc projected to win 140 seats, well ahead of the 126 mandates required for a majority, while United Serbia is expected to have 103.

The student movement grew out of months of protests demanding accountability and an end to corruption, and it has channelled the frustration of younger voters and urban professionals who feel shut out of a system dominated by Vucic's party for more than a decade. The protests began after a railway station canopy collapsed in the city of Novi Sad in November 2024, killing 16 people. What started as a demand for a transparent investigation into that disaster grew into the biggest protest movement in a generation, calling first for accountability and then for the depoliticisation of institutions and an end to systemic corruption.

Vucic, a populist and nationalist, has dominated Serbian politics as prime minister or president since 2014, and his Serbian Progressive Party has governed since 2012. He called the snap elections in September after almost two years of student-led demonstrations against perceived corruption and creeping authoritarianism, and he resigned from his second term as president to run again for prime minister, leading the United Serbia list himself. Few in the student movement dared believe he would concede so fast; he announced the outcome only around an hour and a half after polls closed.

During the campaign, the movement was led by Ilija Srdanovic, a professor at the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Novi Sad. The cardiologist is now seen as the most likely candidate to become the next prime minister, but it remains unclear who will take over key ministries, including those of internal affairs, finance and justice. Unlike United Serbia, which named Vucic as its candidate for prime minister before the election, the Student List made no such announcement during the campaign.

The Student List fielded a slate of 250 candidates drawn from civil society, including university professors, actors, farmers and athletes, many of whom have no political experience. The students' message during the campaign focused on a non-partisan effort to rebuild Serbia's economy and restore its democracy. In its electoral programme, presented in September, the movement said fighting corruption and organised crime were among its main priorities. After declaring victory on Sunday, the movement also said it would work to improve relations with neighbouring countries following a period of tension during Vucic's more than decade-long rule.

Vucic's campaign leaned on his record of stability, infrastructure building and Serbia's balancing act between the European Union, Russia and China. The opposition argues that stability has come at the price of captured institutions and a pliant media landscape. In conceding, Vucic blamed Serbia's independent media as well as alleged interference by unnamed European countries and an "American deep state" for the loss. He had been endorsed by Russian President Vladimir Putin during the campaign and had denounced the student movement as a foreign-led attempt to unseat him.

Voting to determine whether Vucic's party could retain power against a movement born from nationwide anticorruption protests ended at 8pm local time on Sunday. About 6.5 million people were eligible to vote for the 250 parliamentary seats, with 18 electoral lists competing. Lines snaked from polling stations in cities and towns across the country earlier in the day, and even Vucic waited for nearly two hours to vote in Belgrade. Pollsters and the election commission put turnout close to 70 percent, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992.

Sunday's voting was also marred by reports of intimidation and procedural problems. The Centre for Research, Transparency and Accountability, an independent election watchdog, said its observers recorded pressure on voters or election workers at almost a third of the 500 polling stations it monitored. The parliamentary campaign itself was marred by numerous irregularities, according to observers, including Vucic's overwhelming presence on television channels, manipulation of voter rolls, violence and illegal wiretapping targeting opposition figures.

European leaders and officials congratulated the Student List on its victory, among them the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, and the European Commissioner for Enlargement, Marta Kos. The result will be read closely in Brussels, where Serbia's path towards EU membership has stalled for years amid concerns about the rule of law, media freedom and the country's refusal to align fully with EU foreign policy, including sanctions on Russia. A new government that promises to fight corruption and restore democratic institutions could reopen that conversation, though the practical work of reform would take years.

The new National Assembly must be established within 30 days of the final results being announced, and a government must be formed within 90 days after that, but the Student List's clear majority removes the need for lengthy coalition talks. A separate question is the presidency: following Vucic's call for the snap parliamentary vote last month and his resignation as president on 27 September, the post has been held temporarily by Ana Brnabic, the president of the National Assembly. She is obliged to call an extraordinary presidential election, which must be held no later than three months after the end of Vucic's mandate, a deadline of 27 December.

Whatever the outcome of the government formation, the vote marks the moment Serbia's protest generation moved from the streets into the ballot box. The students' win marks a profound political shift in a country of about six million people, and it is reminiscent of the defeat of Hungary's Viktor Orban in April, another long-serving populist leader swept aside by a pro-European wave. European capitals will be reading the result closely, and so will governments in Moscow and Beijing, which have both cultivated close ties with Belgrade under Vucic. For Serbia itself, the hardest work begins now: turning a protest movement into a government, and turning promises of justice, freedom and dignity into institutions that can deliver them.
