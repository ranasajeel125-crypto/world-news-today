Russia Hits Two Grain Ships Near Odesa as Ukraine's Black Sea Exports Grind to a Halt

world · 26 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Grain shipments from Ukraine's Odesa ports have all but stopped since late July as Russian attacks on terminals and merchant vessels intensified.

Russia attacked two foreign cargo ships carrying more than 100,000 metric tons of grain overnight near Ukraine's Black Sea port of Odesa, Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergiy Koretskyi said on Thursday.

Koretskyi did not say where the grain originated, nor where the vessels had loaded their cargo, and the report gave no further details about the two ships. The strike is the latest in a campaign that has, in Kyiv's own account, all but shut down one of the world's most important grain export routes.

Ukraine has said grain shipments from its key Black Sea ports around Odesa have all but stopped since late July, after Russia sharply intensified attacks on grain terminals and merchant vessels. Russia says it does not attack civilian targets.

The scale of what has been lost is hard to overstate. Before the escalation, the three ports in the Odesa hub handled about 90 percent of Ukraine's agricultural and metallurgical exports. For a country whose economy and whose role in world food markets both depend on moving grain by sea, the near-closure of those ports is not a side effect of the war — it is one of its central fronts.

Kyiv has not formally banned port operations. It has not needed to. Shipowners have avoided the ports because of the security risks, while soaring insurance costs have deterred traders from booking cargoes through them at all. President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said earlier this year that shipments from the Odesa ports were taking place only intermittently, while Agriculture Minister Taras Vysotskiy has said no exports were moving through them.

That leaves Ukraine with a paradox that the overnight attack only sharpens: the grain keeps arriving at the coast, but the ships are not sailing. Analysts' data show that Ukraine's state railway continues to transport grain towards the Black Sea ports. Consultancy APK-Inform reported this week that 429 grain wagons were dispatched towards Odesa between November 12 and November 18 — four times the level recorded a week earlier. The harvest is moving; the export route is not.

Ukraine does not publish official data on shipments from its Black Sea ports, saying agricultural exports have instead been redirected to Danube River ports or moved by rail across land borders with eastern European countries. Those alternatives exist, and they have kept some grain moving, but they were never built to carry the volumes that Odesa handled. River barges and rail wagons move grain in thousands of tons at a time; the Odesa terminals moved it in millions.

The official export figures show the gap. Ukraine had exported 7.6 million tons of grain so far in the 2026/27 July-June season, according to official data, down from 11.5 million tons in the same period a year earlier — a fall of roughly a third, with the season still months from its end.

The attack also lands on a diplomatic track that was supposed to address exactly this. At the US-backed talks in Abu Dhabi earlier this month, Ukraine offered Russia a truce in the Black Sea and on energy infrastructure, tabling specific proposals rather than general ideas. On the energy and sea ceasefire, Zelenskiy said the Russian side said it had no mandate to respond, and the issues were passed to US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, who were due in Moscow on November 12 to put them to Putin directly. The two sides did agree in principle on a large-scale prisoner exchange, of 1,000-for-1,000 prisoners, perhaps 1,200. But on the sea, nothing was agreed — and the strikes on ships and terminals have continued.

That pattern — diplomacy in one room, escalation on the water — is now the backdrop to the warning issued only a day before this attack. On Wednesday, US President Donald Trump and NATO chief Mark Rutte warned Russia against any action, intentional or unintentional, that would expand the war in Ukraine, in a joint statement after their meeting at Mar-a-Lago in Florida. The war in Ukraine and the US-Israeli war on Iran were the primary subjects of the talks, the statement said, without saying what was discussed on Iran.

The warning came days after Trump met Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping in Asia. On Thursday 19 November, the three leaders held the first-ever trilateral meeting of the leaders of the world's three biggest nuclear powers, spending 90 minutes together at Kylin Villa, a secluded government retreat in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. That meeting ended with a four-point joint declaration, described by the leaders as a “peace declaration” — but it did not mention Ukraine at all, and announced no agreement on Ukraine or Iran.

Trump gave his first public assessment of the Putin talks the next day, Friday 20 November, at a White House news conference, saying he and Putin had held “very good talks” on Ukraine and that Putin had “clearly expressed his desire to see the war between Russia and Ukraine end by diplomatic means”. Hours after the Shenzhen peace declaration, Russia launched one of the largest missile and drone attacks of the war, hitting Kyiv's bridges over the Dnipro River and killing eight people. The same week, three Russian objects — a Kh-101 cruise missile, a second missile and what was most likely a jet-powered drone — landed in south-eastern Poland. Nobody was hurt and no damage to civilian property was reported, but Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said: “It seems we are dealing with an intentional provocation.”

The grain-ship attack fits that same sequence: a high-level statement of intent, followed within days by a strike that tests it. The difference here is the target. Bridges, power plants and airfields are military infrastructure by any measure. Grain ships carrying food are not, and the distinction is why attacks on them draw a different kind of attention — from the insurers who price the risk, from the governments of the countries that buy Ukrainian grain, and from the importers who have to decide whether the Black Sea route is worth the gamble at all.

For Ukraine, the stakes are immediate and practical. Every week the ports stay closed is a week in which grain that could be sold abroad sits in silos or moves slowly by rail and river, at higher cost and in smaller volumes. The 429 wagons dispatched towards Odesa in a single week show the supply is there. What is missing is a safe way to load it onto ships — and, after this latest attack, the insurers and shipowners who would have to take that risk have one more reason not to.

Why it matters: the Black Sea grain route is one of the few parts of this war with a direct, measurable effect on people far from the front line. Ukraine is one of the world's largest grain exporters, and the Odesa ports were its main outlet. A fall of a third in exports in a single season, and the effective closure of the ports that handled 90 percent of them, is a food-security story as much as a war story. The test now is the same one set by the Mar-a-Lago warning: whether the diplomacy of the past week — the trilateral in Shenzhen, the talks in Abu Dhabi, the warning from Trump and Rutte — can produce anything that actually protects a grain ship at sea. Until it does, the grain will keep arriving at Odesa by rail, and the ships will keep staying away.
