Steel Plant Explosion in Turkey Kills 16

world · 9 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Smoke rose over the HABAS plant in Aliaga after the blast, in footage shown on Turkish television; witnesses said the ground shook.

A huge explosion and fire at an iron and steelworks in western Turkey killed 16 people on Monday, officials said, in one of the country's deadliest industrial accidents in years.

The blast struck the HABAS plant in Aliaga, in Izmir province about 60 kilometres north of the port city of Izmir, at around noon local time (0900 GMT), the provincial governor's office said. Al Jazeera put the time of the blast at 11:46am local time (08:46 GMT). President Recep Tayyip Erdogan said 13 people were injured, two of them critically, in a televised address after a cabinet meeting.

Provincial Governor Suleyman Elban said an overhead crane fell into a furnace as it was replacing a boiler, causing an explosion in a nearby steel mill. According to initial findings, the incident occurred when a ceiling crane snapped and fell into an arc furnace during the liquid steel casting process. The blast destroyed part of the casting platform and damaged a natural gas line. About 200 workers were in the area instead of the usual 300 because production had been halted for the boiler switch, he said, with 100 more having stayed home because of the boiler replacement operation.

The death toll climbed through the day, from an initial three dead, 14 injured and 10 missing. The governor later put the toll at 10 dead and 15 injured, with four workers still missing, while Erdogan's figure of 16 dead and 13 injured has not been reconciled with the on-site count. Izmir Mayor Cemil Tugay said the final toll would only be known once the debris was cleared. Another body was later discovered by rescuers, bringing the death toll to 18, and on 13 November the body of Baris Guney, who was initially reported missing, was identified, raising the death toll to 19. According to deputy justice minister Can Tuncay, the victims' cause of death was severe burns.

"The ground shook. It was very intense," witness Nuri Ali Ulker was quoted as saying by the state-run Anadolu news agency. Another witness, Deniz Ulker, said the explosion "felt like the earth's crust was shifting beneath us. I thought it was an earthquake." Footage posted online showed a huge cloud of thick smoke pouring into the sky as flames appeared to engulf a tall structure within the plant, and smoke rose over the HABAS plant in Aliaga after the blast, in footage shown on Turkish television.

Interior Minister Mustafa Ciftci said emergency teams fought the blaze while searching for casualties, and the injured were taken to hospital. Justice Minister Akin Gurlek said four public prosecutors had been assigned to the case, and the private Demiroren News Agency reported that five people had been detained in relation to the explosion.

"Judicial and administrative investigations have been launched into the explosion," Erdogan said. "The reasons and circumstances of this tragic event, which has deeply saddened our nation, as well as whose negligence was involved and who is responsible, will be revealed."

The HABAS plant, established in 1987, can produce 4.5 million tons of steel a year, according to the company's website. HABAS is a privately owned group and one of Turkey's top steel producers, and its Aliağa operations also include port facilities. Aliaga, a large port and industrial district on Turkey's Aegean coast, is also known for shipbreaking.

The explosion raises questions about safety in Turkey's heavy industry. Steelworks are among the most dangerous workplaces in the world: molten steel is handled at temperatures of more than 1,500 degrees Celsius, and an electric arc furnace, which melts scrap steel with electric arcs, is a massive piece of equipment that must be operated with great care. A crane collapse into a furnace full of molten metal can cause a violent explosion, as water, gas or air trapped in the metal expands instantly, throwing molten steel across the plant. Workers in the area have little chance of escaping, and severe burns are the most common cause of death.

Turkey has a poor record on workplace safety. The country has suffered a series of deadly industrial accidents in recent years, including a mining disaster in 2014 that killed 301 people at Soma, in western Turkey, and a coal mine explosion in 2022 that killed 41 people at Amasra, on the Black Sea coast. Trade unions and opposition politicians have repeatedly accused the government and employers of failing to enforce safety regulations, and of prioritising production over the lives of workers. The government, for its part, has introduced new safety laws and inspection regimes, but critics say enforcement remains weak and that fines for violations are too low to deter employers.

The investigation into the Aliaga explosion will need to establish why the crane fell, whether it was properly maintained, whether the boiler replacement operation was properly planned and supervised, and whether workers should have been in the area at all while such a dangerous operation was under way. The fact that five people were detained suggests the authorities believe there may have been negligence, but the full findings of the judicial and administrative investigations have yet to be published.

For the families of the dead, the immediate concern is the identification of the victims and the return of their bodies, and for the injured, the long and painful process of recovery from severe burns. For the steel industry, the explosion is a reminder that a single failure in a plant that handles molten metal can kill in seconds, and that the cost of cutting corners on safety is measured in lives. The plant itself will face months of repair and investigation before it can return to full production, and the company will face questions about its safety record that will not be answered quickly.
