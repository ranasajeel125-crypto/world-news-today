NASA and Energy Department Sign Deal to Put Nuclear Power in Space

technology · 25 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By Tom Becker (Technology Editor)

Space Reactor-1 'Freedom' is planned as a fission-powered spacecraft demonstrating nuclear electric propulsion to Mars in 2028.

NASA and the US Department of Energy have signed a new space nuclear agreement, putting another piece of Washington's reactor ambitions on paper while private developers race to meet near-term goals. The memorandum of understanding — 'Accelerating American Leadership in Space Nuclear Power and Propulsion' — was signed by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman and Energy Secretary Chris Wright and takes effect on 1 November.

'We're getting America underway on nuclear power in space and ushering in the era of a Nuclear NASA,' Isaacman said. 'Fission power will help us travel farther, operate longer, and power more capable scientific instruments.'

The agreement covers three programmes. Space Reactor-1 'Freedom' is a fission-powered spacecraft that will demonstrate nuclear electric propulsion, slated to launch toward Mars in 2028. Lunar Reactor-1 is a fission surface power system meant to keep the lights on at NASA's planned Moon base, with a launch-ready unit due by 2030 under the December 2025 executive order 'Ensuring American Space Superiority'. A third strand covers research, testing, launch integration and fuel production.

The deal replaces the previous NASA–DOE memorandum, which reaches its ten-year expiry at the end of October. It also lands alongside a wider White House science push of more than $6 billion across AI, quantum computing and biomedical research, in which the 'Genesis Mission' alone attracted $2.4 billion in industry support — including a $1 billion pledge from Nvidia.

Nuclear power is attractive in space because sunlight weakens with distance and lunar nights last two weeks. A compact reactor can run instruments and life support continuously — if engineers can solve the shielding, cooling and launch-safety problems that have kept space reactors rare since the 1960s.
