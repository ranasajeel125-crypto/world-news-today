NASA and Energy Department Sign Deal to Put Nuclear Power in Space

technology · 25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By Tom Becker (Technology Editor)

Space Reactor-1 'Freedom' is planned as a fission-powered spacecraft demonstrating nuclear electric propulsion to Mars in 2028.

NASA and the US Department of Energy have signed a new space nuclear agreement, putting another piece of Washington's reactor ambitions on paper while private developers race to meet near-term goals. The memorandum of understanding, "Accelerating American Leadership in Space Nuclear Power and Propulsion", was signed by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman and Energy Secretary Chris Wright on 8 October during the Science: A Golden Age Summit, hosted by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy at the Donald J. Trump Institute of Peace in Washington, and takes effect on 1 November.

"We're getting America underway on nuclear power in space and ushering in the era of a Nuclear NASA," Isaacman said. "Fission power will help us travel farther, operate longer, and power more capable scientific instruments." In NASA's own announcement, he said the agency was entering the "Nuclear NASA era", which represents a major transformation for space exploration. Wright linked the partnership to the administration's broader nuclear policy agenda, citing what he called America's nuclear renaissance.

The agreement covers three programmes. Space Reactor-1 "Freedom" is a fission-powered spacecraft that will demonstrate nuclear electric propulsion, slated to launch toward Mars in December 2028. Lunar Reactor-1 is a fission surface power system meant to keep the lights on at NASA's planned Moon base, with a launch-ready unit due by 2030 under the December 2025 executive order "Ensuring American Space Superiority". A third strand covers research, testing, launch integration and fuel production. The memorandum establishes a framework for end-to-end collaboration across the full spectrum of space nuclear development, from advanced research and fuel production through rigorous testing, launch integration and operations.

Space Reactor-1 Freedom is the centrepiece. In December 2028, NASA plans to launch the first spacecraft to use a nuclear fission reactor for propulsion beyond Earth orbit, something no nation has accomplished in 60 years of spaceflight. After launch, the spacecraft escapes Earth's gravity and within 48 hours starts its nuclear fission reactor and powers electric thrusters. It will then navigate to Mars using nuclear electric propulsion, taking approximately one year to reach the planet, and deliver the Skyfall scientific payload: three Ingenuity-class helicopters equipped with cameras, ground-penetrating radar and radios to survey potential human landing sites, search for subsurface water and relay navigation data for future landers.

The reactor itself is deliberately modest in scope. It is an approximately 20-kilowatt-electric reactor using high-assay low-enriched uranium oxide fuel, with thermal transfer through heat pipes, a boron carbide radiation shield and an advanced closed Brayton cycle power conversion system to turn reactor heat into electricity. NASA's fission surface power programme executive, Steve Sinacore, has said the design limits its scope to using existing technology, with the reactor as the primary new system, an approach chosen to prioritise hitting the 2028 Mars launch window. The plan calls for significant design work to be complete and hardware development to begin in June 2026, with all spacecraft assembly and testing between January and October 2028.

The ambition is striking because the history is one of false starts. Over 60 years, NASA has spent more than 20 billion dollars on more than a dozen attempted flight programmes for space nuclear power, none of which reached orbit. The United States has flown a reactor in space exactly once, in 1965. Space Reactor-1 Freedom is designed to break that pattern by reusing hardware originally built for NASA's Gateway lunar station programme, which the agency is pausing in favour of focusing on a permanent Moon base, and bolting a reactor onto it.

Lunar Reactor-1 is the second step in the sequence, and it will be informed by the data and lessons from Space Reactor-1. It is a fission surface power system designed to keep the Moon base operating through periods of darkness and in locations where solar power alone cannot reach, and it would be the first fission nuclear reactor on the Moon. Development relies on public-private partnerships under NASA's Fission Surface Power project, managed by the Department of Energy's Idaho National Laboratory. NASA has been directed to develop a launch-ready lunar surface reactor by 2030 under the 2025 executive order, which also calls for the return of Americans to the Moon by 2028 and the establishment of lunar outpost elements by 2030.

Nuclear power is attractive in space because sunlight weakens with distance and lunar nights last two weeks. A compact reactor can run instruments and life support continuously, if engineers can solve the shielding, cooling and launch-safety problems that have kept space reactors rare since the 1960s. The same logic extends beyond the Moon and Mars: NASA's Dragonfly mission to Saturn's moon Titan, also scheduled for launch in 2028, will rely on a multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator and 24 lightweight radioisotope heater units to power and warm the car-sized rotorcraft, and NASA and the Energy Department plan to provide 24 similar heater units to the European Space Agency's Rosalind Franklin Mars rover.

The deal replaces the previous NASA-Department of Energy memorandum, which reaches its ten-year expiry at the end of October. Together, Space Reactor-1 and Lunar Reactor-1 are described by NASA as the early beginnings of a domestic nuclear-space industrial base that scales to power permanent lunar outposts and secures American leadership in space for decades to come. The December 2028 launch window, which forces decisions that decades of study never did, is the discipline behind the schedule, and the competition with China, which plans to land astronauts on the Moon before 2030, is the pressure behind it.
