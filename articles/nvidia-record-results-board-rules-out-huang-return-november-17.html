Nvidia Posts Record Results as Board Rules Out Huang's Return

business · 17 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Business Desk)

Nvidia chair Harvey Jones told investors the board had unanimously accepted Jensen Huang's resignation and would not reinstate him, even as the chipmaker reported record sales.

Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of $115.8 billion, more than double a year earlier, in its first results since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive — and its board used the same day to say it does not intend to bring him back. Shares in the company rose about 4% in extended trading after it forecast fourth-quarter revenue above Wall Street targets, Reuters reported.

The numbers, for the fiscal third quarter ended 25 October, were emphatic. Revenue of $115.84 billion rose 103% from the same period last year and beat analysts' average estimate of $109.11 billion, according to LSEG data — the 17th straight quarter in which Nvidia has topped Wall Street's revenue expectations. Profit more than doubled to $65 billion, adjusted earnings reached $2.68 a share against expectations of $2.47, and adjusted gross margin rose to 74.9% from 73.5% a year earlier, also beating estimates, despite soaring memory prices and production ramps. Data-centre revenue, the heart of the business, more than doubled to $107.6 billion.

The outlook was strong too. Nvidia said it expects fourth-quarter revenue of $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, above analysts' average estimate of $125.25 billion, with adjusted gross margin of 71% to 72%. The forecast assumes no data-centre chip sales to China, and includes an additional week in the reporting period. The company also raised its forecast for revenue growth in the coming fiscal year to 75%, from 70% previously.

But the results were almost beside the point on the earnings call. Board chairman Harvey Jones opened the call with investors by saying the board would not rehire Huang as chief executive and would not take questions about his departure. “The board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO,” Jones said. The board has hired executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles to help find a permanent chief executive, and is considering internal and external candidates. “We want to install the CEO, the permanent CEO, as quickly as possible, but we won’t expedite the CEO before finding exactly the right person,” Jones added.

Huang, widely regarded as the public face of the AI boom, resigned on 12 November after revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with Chinese businesswoman Alice Huang and two with Nvidia employees, ending 33 years of founder-led control over the company's chip roadmap, customer deals and Washington ties. On Tuesday he said in his first public statement since stepping down that he would be “honoured” to return if the board deemed it best. A public “Stand With Jensen” campaign to bring him back has gathered more than 180,000 signatures, according to Reuters, and US President Donald Trump has said he would not object to his reinstatement. Amazon executive chair Jeff Bezos, speaking at a conference in Silicon Valley on Tuesday, said the board should keep its focus within the company: “If you don’t bring Jensen back, I would not hire an outsider. Nvidia is too complicated,” he said.

In the meantime the company is being run by interim chief executive Colette Kress, Nvidia's longtime chief financial officer, who was joined on the call by Jones and by Ian Buck, vice president of hyperscale and high-performance computing. Kress defended her team's work since Huang's departure. “Our leadership has stood up so strongly over the last week,” she said. “We’ve got this.” Leadership from Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co, which makes Nvidia's chips, was in the office and “fully engaged in helping us,” she said, and the company's latest flagship AI chip, Vera Rubin, was off to an “explosive start”. The call, the first without Huang, ended in under an hour — the first time in nearly 28 years, since the company's stock-market listing, that he had not been at the helm for a quarterly earnings call.

The board's firm line does not settle the succession question, and in some ways it complicates it. Huang can nominate two directors, and the campaign to reinstate him has put the board under public pressure even as it insists it is focused on its review of his conduct. Prediction market Kalshi put the odds at 25% that Rene Haas, chief executive of chip company Arm and a former Nvidia executive, would become chief executive, 17% for Ian Buck, who joined Kress on the call, and 9% for Huang himself. Until a permanent chief executive is named, Nvidia — and by extension much of the AI industry — is, in the words of Reuters Breakingviews, stuck in purgatory.

The other half of Tuesday's news was governance. Earlier in the day, Nvidia's board created a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, its first major governance move since Huang's departure, as scrutiny grows over the chipmaker's national security role. On Monday, the Trump administration said it would further curb China's access to AI chips located in neighbouring countries, tightening licensing rules for chip shipments to Southeast Asia and overseas data centres serving Chinese firms. Lawmakers are preparing to question Huang, who had pushed for greater chip exports to China, and he has agreed to meet US lawmakers on 1 December to answer questions about chips and national security, AFP reported.

The investigations around the departure are also widening. US intelligence agencies are trying to determine the extent of Alice Huang's ties to China's Ministry of State Security and what information she may have passed to Beijing, according to two people familiar with the matter. China has rejected allegations of an espionage operation targeting Jensen Huang. The US Securities and Exchange Commission is probing Nvidia after Huang's resignation, the Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday afternoon; the SEC declined to comment. The US Justice and Commerce departments are also investigating Megaspeed, the Singaporean operator at the centre of the affair, and an internal inquiry at Nvidia is under way.

Tuesday's results also offered a first read on Vera Rubin, Nvidia's newest generation of AI processors and the successor to Grace Blackwell. Nvidia said in August that Vera Rubin would account for about a fifth of data-centre revenue in the October quarter. Sales to the upstart operators known as neoclouds — companies such as CoreWeave that rent out computing power — are now Nvidia's fastest growing, and Huang's complicated deals to backstop or support these clients were a key part of that rise. Kress chose to highlight that the amount and timing of commitments to neoclouds depend on these companies securing financing and deploying computing power in a timely manner. Shares of CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN and Applied Digital extended gains after she told analysts Nvidia had made no changes to how it backs its neocloud commitments.

All of this lands at an anxious moment for the industry. Nvidia is valued at $3.8 trillion, but that is down about $2 trillion from an early October high. Investors were already uneasy about AI spending after the Democrats' sweep of the US midterm elections fuelled fears of tougher scrutiny and slower data-centre expansion. Fund managers surveyed by Bank of America say the top risk facing investors is an AI bubble, and more than half expect a so-called hyperscaler like Microsoft to cut back on capital spending in the next 12 months. Analysts said Huang's exit made execution a bigger risk than near-term AI demand — the company can sell every chip it makes, but the deals, the diplomacy and the strategy now have to be carried by someone else.

The market's first verdict was relief. “It’s no surprise that they’re doing very well, and there’ll be people who will breathe a sigh of relief about it,” said Rick Meckler, partner at Cherry Lane Investments. “But the story here is very complicated, and it’s hard not to factor that in as an additional risk for people buying stock.” Anshel Sag, principal analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, called the outlook “quite positive, albeit not as ambitious as in previous quarters”, and added: “I don’t think anything near-term will be affected by Jensen’s departure. His executive leadership team is capable and has followed his leadership and executed effectively for years, reaching this point.”

What happens next will be watched in three places at once. The chief-executive search, inside and outside the company, will decide whether Nvidia stays founder-shaped without its founder. Huang's appearance before US lawmakers on 1 December will test how much of the China and export-control story becomes public. And the Vera Rubin ramp, together with next quarter's results, will show whether the record numbers are a one-off or the new normal for a company selling the picks and shovels of the AI boom. Until then, the world's most valuable chipmaker has record sales, a rising forecast, no permanent chief executive — and a board that has said, plainly, that its old boss is not coming back.

Why it matters: Nvidia is the bellwether of the artificial-intelligence boom, the company whose results are read as a verdict on whether the trillions being poured into data centres are paying off. A record quarter answers that question for now. But the board's decision to rule out Huang's return, the widening investigations, and the tightening export controls around China are a reminder that the AI trade is no longer just a bet on demand. It is a bet on who runs the company that supplies it, on how Washington polices its most strategic technology, and on whether the industry's fastest-growing customers can keep paying for the chips they are buying. Tuesday's numbers were astonishingly good. The questions around them are not going away.
