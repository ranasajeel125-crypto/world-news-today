Anti-Houthi Forces Retreat From Bab el-Mandeb as Iran Pressures US and Gulf on Two Fronts

world · 15 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The Bab el-Mandeb Strait controls access to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal; Yemeni government forces announced on Sunday they were pulling back to defend the temporary capital, Aden.

Saudi-backed forces fighting for Yemen’s internationally recognised government said on Sunday they were withdrawing from the area overlooking the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, leaving the Iran-aligned Houthis in control of one of the world’s most important shipping chokepoints. The announcement came on the same day Iran fired drones at US bases in Bahrain, in a coordinated squeeze on the United States and its Gulf allies on two fronts.

The Southern Armed Forces, the main military force fighting for Yemen’s government in the south of the country, said in a statement that it was carrying out “a redeployment operation for its forces from Dhubab Airport and the Bab el-Mandeb area, west of Taiz Governorate”. It said the step was taken “based on the priority of securing the capital, Aden, and the neighbouring governorates, as well as strengthening the front lines and addressing existing threats”. There was no immediate response from the Houthis, or from Saudi Arabia.

The retreat signalled the apparent end of a six-week government counteroffensive to drive the Houthis from the mouth of the Red Sea and secure a key oil route. The Houthis had made rapid gains in September, capturing the coast along the Red Sea and the strait that controls access to it. A Saudi-backed government offensive last month retook key positions around the strait but failed to drive the Houthis entirely away from the area or to retake the city of al-Mocha further up the coast. Sunday’s announcement raises the prospect of new Houthi advances towards Aden, the base of the internationally recognised government since the Houthis drove it from the capital, Sanaa, more than a decade ago.

Yemeni military sources said government forces around the Bab el-Mandeb had redeployed to al-Suqyah, 150 kilometres (95 miles) west of Aden, though they were still trying to push the Houthis from coastal areas nearby. In the highlands north of Aden, the Houthis are now positioned just outside the district of Tur al-Bahah, about 80 kilometres (50 miles) from the city of Taiz, the sources said. Taiz, Yemen’s third-largest city, remains besieged, and humanitarian conditions there are worsening: the city hosts about half of the 300,000 people displaced across Yemen by the latest fighting, aid supplies are unable to enter and civilians are unable to leave.

The Houthis have responded to the government offensive by intensifying drone and missile attacks on Saudi Arabia, grounding flights with strikes on its main airports and setting global oil markets on edge by targeting energy facilities. The escalation in fighting broke a 2022 UN-brokered truce and has become another volatile front in the wider US-Israeli war on Iran, with the Houthis continuing to launch attacks on Saudi Arabia, a key US ally and the world’s biggest crude exporter. Earlier this month a Houthi strike hit the Aramco tower in Riyadh, and the group has struck Saudi ports and airports repeatedly in recent weeks.

The second front opened before dawn on Sunday in Bahrain, home to the headquarters of the US Fifth Fleet. Bahrain said it intercepted and destroyed several Iranian aerial attacks, the first such interceptions by its air defences since September. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it had attacked a US military base in Bahrain with six drones in retaliation for “hostile US actions”, Iran’s Fars news agency reported. During a brief alert, Bahrain urged citizens and residents to remain calm and proceed to the nearest safe location. It was the second Iranian drone attack on Bahrain in three days: two days earlier the Revolutionary Guards said they had attacked a US early-warning radar facility in Bahrain, a claim the US military denied, saying it had intercepted two Iranian drones without any damage.

The twin pressures are likely to put new pressure on US President Donald Trump to resolve the war he launched nearly nine months ago. Since the war contributed to a defeat for his Republicans in the midterm elections this month, Trump has played down the need for a diplomatic breakthrough. He has said in recent days that he might hold off from attacking Iran if the US faces no new provocations from either Tehran or the Houthis in Yemen, although he would maintain a US naval blockade on Iran, Reuters reported on Saturday. Major warfare between the US and Iran has calmed since the early phase of the conflict, but with diplomacy stalled there is little immediate prospect of a resolution either to the wider conflict or the war in Yemen.

The stakes run through the world’s shipping lanes. The Bab el-Mandeb Strait is one of the world’s most important and busiest routes for global seaborne commodity and goods shipments, particularly from Asia to Europe via Egypt’s Suez Canal. With the Houthis now in control of the area overlooking it, they hold a potent pressure point over global trade. Crude oil has been flowing out of the Gulf in recent weeks at near pre-war levels, Reuters reported, but attacks on ships have become a daily hazard, keeping markets jittery and prices still elevated. The Revolutionary Guards said two tankers were on fire on Sunday after striking naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, though Reuters could not independently verify the report. The Guards typically do not acknowledge attacking ships in the strait but say it is unsafe to transit it without their permission.

The wider war began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, and the two sides have been locked in a stand-off over the Strait of Hormuz, the world’s most important oil chokepoint, ever since. A preliminary agreement in June to end the war and reopen the strait collapsed within weeks after Iranian attacks on shipping resumed and the US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports. October recorded 37 vessel attacks, the highest monthly total of the conflict, according to Lloyd’s List. Diplomacy has stalled since then: Iran’s negotiators had been discussing a proposal to reopen the strait if the US met a list of conditions, but President Trump said this week the United States no longer needed a deal and pulled Vice-President JD Vance, who had been leading the talks, off the negotiations.

What happens next will be watched in three capitals at once. In Aden, the question is whether the government can hold its temporary capital now that the Houthis are pressing towards it from the coast and from the highlands near Taiz, and whether Saudi Arabia answers the retreat with new air support or accepts a redrawn front line. In Washington, the question is whether the Bahrain strikes count as the provocation Trump warned about, or whether he holds to his conditional pause and keeps the naval blockade as his main lever. And in the Gulf, Iran’s Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi will on Tuesday visit Oman, which holds the southern coast of the Strait of Hormuz and has played a role as mediator in the crisis, for talks with his counterpart, according to Iranian state media — the only diplomatic channel still visibly open.

Why it matters: Sunday was the day the Yemen war and the US-Iran war fused into a single front. The Houthis now control the ground overlooking the strait through which a large share of Asia-Europe trade passes, giving Tehran’s closest regional ally direct leverage over global shipping at the same moment Iran itself is striking at the heart of American naval power in the Gulf. The retreat also closes a chapter that began with optimism: the six-week government counteroffensive was meant to reverse the Houthis’ September gains and reopen the Red Sea coast; instead it ended with government forces falling back 150 kilometres to defend Aden. With the 2022 truce broken, Taiz besieged, 300,000 people newly displaced and no talks scheduled, the war in Yemen is no longer a separate conflict running alongside the one in the Gulf — it is the same war, fought on two fronts, with the world’s busiest shipping lanes caught in the middle.
