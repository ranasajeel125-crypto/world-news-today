Ukraine Fires Nearly 1,200 Drones at Russia in One of Its Biggest Attacks as US-Led Talks Weigh Wider European Security

world · 13 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

A Russian strike on Zaporizhzhia wounded 30 people, including six children, Ukrainian officials said.

Ukraine fired almost 1,200 drones at Russian targets overnight in one of its biggest aerial attacks since Moscow's full-scale invasion nearly five years ago, as American-led efforts to end the war entered a delicate new phase — with the wider European security order, in place since the end of the Cold War, now on the negotiating table.

Russia's Defence Ministry said its air defences shot down 1,198 Ukrainian long-range drones between late Thursday and early Friday. But the strikes that got through hit three Russian regions, killing seven people, including a 12-year-old child, and wounding 17, according to local officials.

Ukraine's General Staff said its drones struck a major oil refinery in the Russian city of Volgograd, starting a fire. The refinery has a capacity of about 110 million barrels a year, it said — the latest blow in Kyiv's sustained campaign against the fuel infrastructure that keeps Russia's war machine running.

Russia answered with its own overnight barrage, launching 117 drones against Ukraine and killing at least two people, including a 17-year-old, Ukrainian authorities said. A single Russian attack on Friday on the southern city of Zaporizhzhia hit two five-storey apartment blocks and wounded 30 people, including six children.

The scale of the exchange shows how completely drones have come to define this war. Kyiv's forces are using drones and ground robots to help defend against Russia's army along the roughly 1,200-kilometre front line snaking down eastern and south-eastern Ukraine, while Moscow, its troops largely bogged down on the battlefield, has used strategic bombardment of civilian areas to hurt Ukraine.

Ukraine has repeatedly struck Russian refineries, claiming last month that it had neutralised more than half of Russia's refining capacity. The massive Ukrainian barrage appeared designed to show that Kyiv is holding its own in the war, countering Moscow's insistence that a Russian victory is inevitable.

The human cost of the night's exchanges was felt on both sides. The war has killed hundreds of thousands of soldiers and thousands of civilians, analysts and humanitarian organisations say — and Friday's strikes added to that toll in a single night, with children among the dead and wounded in both countries.

The energy war escalated on Friday as well. Russian attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure caused widespread power outages across about half of the country's regions, First Deputy Minister of Energy Artem Nekrasov said. Moscow has battered Ukraine's power grid each winter since the war began, hoping to weaken public morale as the cold sets in.

Meanwhile, the diplomacy moved just as fast. After two days of talks with Russian and Ukrainian delegations in Abu Dhabi this week, US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner — President Donald Trump's son-in-law — went in person to brief Russian President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who was meeting European leaders in Dublin. More talks are planned as negotiators seek a breakthrough, the envoys said.

The negotiators have raised the stakes by including for discussion the wider European security arrangements that have been in place since the end of the Cold War. That change, long sought by Moscow, has alarmed European leaders and placed a question mark over NATO's future role.

Kushner said Russia has agreed to include Europe in upcoming talks, after Moscow's long insistence that the continent's governments should be excluded because they supported Ukraine. Europe's presence at the negotiating table will allow negotiators “to address the broader postwar European security arrangements alongside the territorial issues between Ukraine and Russia,” Kushner said on X. A team of diplomats from the UK, France, Germany, Italy, Poland and the European Union will be sent to the talks, European Council President Antonio Costa said after a meeting in Dublin.

Europe's leaders, however, are wary of what Moscow is really after. “Russia wants to talk about questions of European security architecture. We don't need to talk about that. There is one,” German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said, referring to the NATO military alliance. The EU's top diplomat, Kaja Kallas, said Europe must insist on a ceasefire before taking the negotiations further — the opposite of what Putin demands. “If we start to talk about what Russia is asking regarding the security architecture, we actually walk into the traps that Russia is setting us,” she said in Dublin.

“We cannot let neither the Americans nor the Russians decide about our future,” Luxembourg Prime Minister Luc Frieden insisted. The Institute for the Study of War said in an assessment on Thursday that Moscow is aiming “to dismantle NATO and the post-Cold War European security architecture.” Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov reaffirmed that Russia is open for contacts with the Europeans, but said a decision on talks in an expanded format would come only once Moscow understands Europe's “true intentions and their readiness to facilitate the peace process.”

Zelenskyy also raised a practical problem for Ukraine's drone war on Friday, repeating his complaints about drone speed limits on Starshield, the US military-grade version of SpaceX's Starlink satellite internet service. The restrictions are limiting Ukrainian drones to 161 kph (100 mph) on the entire front line and in Russian-occupied territory, he said, making it impossible for Kyiv to deploy its interceptors effectively against Moscow's fast-moving jet-powered drones. “We still have no clear explanations,” he said, after first raising the issue on Thursday. Ukrainian officials are asking the US State Department, the Pentagon and SpaceX to clarify and resolve the situation. Last month, similar SpaceX limits on Ukrainian drone speeds severely disrupted operations, and Elon Musk acknowledged in October that his company had throttled the Starlink service in Ukraine for about a week, saying the system could not be used for offensive strikes against Russia.

Why it matters: the battlefield and the negotiating table are now moving in lockstep. Kyiv's record-scale drone barrage and its refinery campaign are leverage — a demonstration, as Washington says the two sides are working towards the largest prisoner exchange of the war, that Ukraine can still impose costs deep inside Russia. But the widening of the talks to European security is the larger gamble: for Moscow, a chance to rewrite the continent's post-Cold War order; for Europe, a trap to be avoided at all costs. What happens next will be decided both by the next round of talks and by the next night of drones.
