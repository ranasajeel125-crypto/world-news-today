At Least Seven Killed and 112 Injured in Egypt Train Collision

world · 8 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The crash happened in al-Ayyat district, Giza province, about 76 km south of Cairo; the health ministry said the death toll could still rise.

At least seven people have been killed and 112 injured after two passenger trains collided south of Cairo on Sunday, Egypt's health ministry said.

The state railway authority said a train travelling from Giza to Fayoum hit the back of another heading from Cairo to Aswan, derailing several carriages. The collision happened in al-Ayyat district, Giza province, about 76 kilometres south of the capital, between Kafr Ammar and Al-Raqqa stations. The Aswan-bound train had stopped when the Fayoum-bound train crashed into it, according to a witness who was sitting at a nearby cafe when the collision occurred.

At least 52 ambulances raced to the scene, the health ministry said, and the train's conductor was among those killed, according to the chief prosecutor's office. Authorities said the death toll could still rise as rescuers searched the wreckage. Later reports put the injured figure higher, at 125, with 71 injured passengers discharged after treatment and 43 remaining in hospital under medical observation. According to Egyptian National Railways, train number 134, travelling from Giza to Fayoum, struck the rear of train number 2010, which was operating between Cairo and Aswan.

President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi ordered a swift investigation and said anyone found negligent should be held accountable. Egypt's public prosecutor said investigations were under way to determine the technical reasons behind the crash. Emergency crews were clearing the wreckage to restore services on the Cairo to Upper Egypt line, the railway authority said, without saying how long that would take.

Al-Ayyat district was also the scene of Egypt's worst-ever rail disaster. In February 2002, a fire swept through an overcrowded train near al-Ayyat, killing more than 370 people, a disaster that remains one of the deadliest rail accidents in the world. The fact that Sunday's crash happened in the same district has sharpened the public reaction, and the collision has already ignited parliamentary and political demands for accountability and for a review of rail upgrades.

Egypt's railway system is the oldest in Africa and the Middle East, and it carries millions of passengers every day on a network that links Cairo with the Nile Delta, the Red Sea coast and the cities of Upper Egypt as far south as Aswan. The Cairo to Aswan line is one of the busiest in the country, carrying commuters, students and workers as well as long-distance travellers, and any disruption on it is felt across the whole network. Trains are the main form of long-distance travel for most Egyptians, and the system is chronically overcrowded, with ageing rolling stock and signalling that has been upgraded only in patches.

Deadly accidents have happened repeatedly on the network, and each one revives the same debate about investment, maintenance and management. In 2019, a locomotive crashed into a barrier at Cairo's Ramses station and exploded, killing at least 25 people. In 2021, two trains collided in the southern province of Sohag, killing 32 people and injuring more than 100, and a separate derailment near Cairo killed 11 people the same year. The government has announced large programmes of modernisation, including new signalling, new locomotives and the renewal of track, but critics say the work has been slow and uneven, and that safety culture inside the railway authority has not kept pace.

Rear-end collisions like Sunday's are among the most preventable kinds of rail accident. Modern signalling systems are designed to keep trains a safe distance apart, and automatic train protection can stop a train that passes a red signal or approaches another train too fast. When such a collision happens, investigators look first at the signalling, at whether the driver received the correct warnings, at the condition of the brakes, and at whether the timetable left enough margin between the two trains. The public prosecutor's investigation will need to establish whether the Fayoum-bound train was travelling too fast, whether it was given a clear signal in error, or whether a mechanical failure played a part.

For the injured, the immediate aftermath was a race to hospital. The health ministry said the injured were being treated in hospitals around the Giza area, and the scale of the ambulance response, more than 70 ambulances according to later reports, shows how seriously the authorities treated the crash. Families gathered at hospitals and at the scene, and local residents were among the first to help, pulling passengers from the wreckage before the emergency services arrived.

The political fallout is already under way. Members of parliament have demanded that the transport minister and the head of the railway authority explain what went wrong, and opposition figures have argued that repeated accidents show that the modernisation programme is not working. The government, for its part, points to the investment it has made and to the difficulty of overhauling a network that cannot be shut down while the work is done. President Sisi's order for a swift investigation and his warning that anyone found negligent will be held accountable are a familiar response after such accidents, but accountability in past cases has often been limited to low-level staff rather than senior managers.

For the passengers on the two trains, the crash was a sudden and terrifying interruption to an ordinary Sunday journey. Many were travelling for work or to visit family, and the Fayoum and Aswan routes are used heavily at weekends. The sight of carriages derailed onto a countryside road, with people carrying the bodies of victims away from the wreckage, was carried on television and social media across Egypt within hours.

What happens next depends on the investigation. If it finds a signalling failure or a breach of safety procedures, the railway authority will face pressure to accelerate the rollout of automatic train protection on the Cairo to Upper Egypt line. If it finds a human error, the debate will turn to training, working hours and supervision. Either way, the crash in al-Ayyat is a reminder that for all the talk of modernisation, Egypt's railways remain a system where a single failure can kill, and where the passengers who depend on them most have the least protection when it does.
