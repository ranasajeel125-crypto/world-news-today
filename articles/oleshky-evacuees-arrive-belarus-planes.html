Oleshky Evacuees Arrive in Belarus After Buses, Flights in Rare Joint Operation

world · 31 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

The evacuees travelled by bus via Crimea to Anapa in Russia before flying to Belarus, Belta said.

Three planes carrying residents evacuated from the Russian-controlled Ukrainian town of Oleshky landed at Gomel airport in Belarus, the Belta state news agency reported on Saturday.

The evacuees, wishing to leave for Ukraine, travelled by bus via the Crimean peninsula to the Russian town of Anapa before flying to Belarus, and will proceed to Ukraine via a border crossing, Belta said. By Saturday afternoon, the evacuees had reached Ukrainian-controlled territory, where tearful reunions with family members were filmed at the Ukraine-Belarus border. Many of the returnees were elderly or immobile, and ambulances were present at the crossing. Hundreds of people — and many pets — arrived back in Ukraine after being evacuated from the frontline town.

Starving and exhausted residents were evacuated by bus on Friday as part of a joint relief operation by the opposing sides, the first of its kind in four years and eight months of war, Reuters reported. The operation was made possible by a temporary ceasefire in Ukraine's southern Kherson region, allowing humanitarian aid deliveries and civilian evacuations. The six-day localised ceasefire, which began at 01:00 GMT on Tuesday, 27 October, was due to last through Saturday and end on Sunday evening. Russia suspended strikes on the Kherson region as the pause took effect.

The evacuation began on Friday under a deal that Reuters reported US first lady Melania Trump was involved in brokering, with the additional involvement of Pope Leo XIV. Both Moscow and Kyiv are trying to use the operation to show US President Donald Trump they are serious about his efforts to secure an overall peace settlement, Reuters reported. Announcing the ceasefire on 23 October, Melania Trump said the situation in the town was "dire" and that it was on the "brink of disaster". "The people of Oleshky have limited electricity, live amid mined roads, and endure regular military attacks. It was abundantly clear that without my intervention, the possibility to bring humanitarian aid into the city and to evacuate the elderly would be impossible," she said in a statement issued by the White House. She said her representatives had worked closely with Russian and Ukrainian leadership to define the terms of the ceasefire, and that civilian leaders from both countries would visit residents in their homes to determine the level of assistance they required. "I anticipate some will want to go to Russia, and some to Ukraine," she said.

Pope Leo XIV personally appealed to the Russian leader to allow humanitarian assistance and civilian evacuations, and the Vatican said the operation followed diplomatic efforts involving both the pope and the first lady. On Sunday, speaking during his Angelus address in St Peter's Square, the pope recalled the extraordinary operation and said he hoped the cooperation between the parties, which made this necessary gesture of humanity possible, would foster a greater willingness to engage in dialogue and lead to new concrete initiatives for peace. The competing accounts of who brokered the truce — the White House insisting on Melania Trump's mediation, the Vatican pointing to the pope's phone calls with both presidents — became a small diplomatic contest of their own, though both sides agreed the ceasefire itself was real.

Oleshky, a town in the Kherson region in southern Ukraine, was taken by Russia in the early days of the invasion in February 2022 and has been under Russian control ever since. Before the war it had a population of about 24,000; by the time of the evacuation, only about 1,700 people remained, living with acute shortages of food, medicine and other basic supplies. The town sits on the front line, and residents described artillery hitting courtyards, craters in gardens and life lived constantly on the brink of death. One evacuee, Lyubov Stanislavovna, said her house remained intact but staying had become too hard, and that in Belarus "here drones don't fly, they don't fall" and "shells don't fly".

The operation was difficult and dangerous. Once a two-day demining operation was completed on Thursday, people who wanted to leave gathered on Friday and travelled by bus through Crimea to Anapa in Russia's Krasnodar region, a journey of 25 buses, before flying to Gomel. Russian officials reported that two people, including a military demining specialist, were killed while clearing roads for the humanitarian convoy. Ukraine's human rights commissioner, Dmytro Lubinets, travelled to Oleshky to personally escort the evacuees, becoming the first high-profile Ukrainian official to openly travel to occupied areas under Russian control and Russia since the start of the war. He coordinated the mission with his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova, and thanked Melania Trump for her involvement. Speaking after crossing the border, Lubinets said the operation was extremely difficult but he hoped it could serve as an inspiration for the future. "Now we have a unique positive experience how to negotiate with the Russian side with the coordination role of Melania Trump," he said.

The number of residents leaving Oleshky remains unclear: Kyiv's prime minister, Sergiy Koretskyi, said on Friday that there were no exact figures at the moment, retracting an earlier remark to parliament that 527 people were planning to leave. He had said the evacuation route would lead through Russia to Belarus, from where residents would enter Ukraine through the Kyiv and Chernihiv regions. The exact number evacuated has not been disclosed for security reasons, although Melania Trump indicated that more than 750 people could be evacuated. Some residents did not want to go to Ukraine and decided to stay in Oleshky, even though the ceasefire was set to expire on Sunday, and some travelled to Russian-held areas instead.

The International Committee of the Red Cross said on Friday its teams had not been asked to provide support but stood ready to help within its mandate if requested, while the UN humanitarian office said it had not received a request to help but was closely monitoring the situation. Ukraine's ombudsman said he hoped the operation could set a framework for future missions, but warned the ceasefire was set to end in days, raising uncertainty for those who remain in Oleshky. After the ceasefire ended on 1 November, Lubinets said in a statement that "both sides honored their commitments" throughout, and Melania Trump called the ceasefire a "success" in a social media post on Sunday. "On behalf of America, I commend Russia and Ukraine for their leadership and cooperation surrounding the Oleshky ceasefire," she said. "I hope Oleshky serves as a foundation for lasting peace between both nations." The ceasefire also created an opportunity to bring other Ukrainians to safety: on Sunday, the Ukrainian Army said it had evacuated 167 people from parts of the Kherson region "that had been under daily shelling" by Russian forces. For the evacuees themselves, the journey ended a separation of 1,711 days since the town fell — and for those left behind, the question is whether the first humanitarian truce of its kind in more than four years of war can be repeated.
