At Least 24 Dead as Boat Carrying Funeral Mourners Capsizes in Nigeria's Bayelsa State

world · 22 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

A boat carrying mourners to a burial ceremony capsized at a river junction in Bayelsa State, southern Nigeria, on Friday, killing at least 24 people, with search and rescue operations still under way on Saturday.

A boat carrying people to a funeral has capsized in southern Nigeria, killing at least 24 people, emergency services said on Saturday. The boat was taking passengers from one community to another for a burial ceremony when it sank on Friday in Bayelsa State, in the country's South South region, Nigeria's National Emergency Management Agency said in a statement.

Agency spokesman Manzo Ezekiel said 24 bodies had been recovered and that search and rescue operations were continuing, without specifying how many people were missing. Police spokesman Musa Mohammed said on Friday that another 24 people had been rescued and sought medical assistance. Authorities have not said how many passengers were believed to have been on board, though residents told Reuters they believed at least 300 people were on the vessel.

The boat was travelling between the villages of Ayama and Ukubie for a burial ceremony when it capsized at about 1000 GMT on Friday, at a river junction between the Oweikorogha and Otuan communities in the Southern Ijaw Local Government Area, according to local police. Two children were among the four bodies initially recovered from the water on Friday, police said at the time, and 24 survivors were rescued — 19 treated at a local clinic and five taken to hospital in Yenagoa, the state capital.

Preliminary reports suggested that the boat suffered a mechanical problem, Mohammed said. Passengers asked the boat operator to steer toward land, but he allegedly declined, according to Mohammed. The boat operator then reportedly fled the scene, while the manager of the boat was taken into custody.

The Bayelsa state government responded by urging boat operators and passengers to adhere to safety regulations. "Overloading, speeding, recklessness and traveling without life jackets must stop. No journey is worth a life," Bayelsa Commissioner for Information Ebiuwou Koku-Obiyai said in a statement on Saturday.

President Bola Tinubu said the victims did not deserve to die that way, and directed that a presidential campaign rally scheduled to be held in Ibadan, Oyo State, on Saturday be observed without much fanfare in honour of the victims and in sympathy with their families.

Boat tragedies are common in Nigeria. A lack of enforcement of safety regulations means faulty boats without life jackets sometimes operate in the country, and overcrowded vessels ply the rivers of the Niger Delta and the north, where roads are poor and waterways are the main route between communities.

The Bayelsa disaster follows a string of deadly capsizes across Nigeria this year. In the north-west, a boat capsized on a river in Sokoto state, killing dozens, with officials withholding an official death toll as rescues continued. The repeated tragedies have drawn criticism of regulators and operators, but enforcement has remained patchy, and many boats continue to sail overloaded and without life jackets.

For the families in Ayama and Ukubie, the loss is measured in empty seats at a burial that was itself meant to honour the dead. The mourners had set out to pay their respects to one person; instead, rescue teams spent Saturday pulling bodies from the river, and the search continued for those still unaccounted for, with the final toll yet to be confirmed.

Why it matters: the Bayelsa capsize is the latest reminder that Nigeria's waterways, which carry millions of people every year, remain dangerously unregulated. Twenty-four people are confirmed dead, more may still be missing, and the early account — a mechanical failure, an operator who allegedly refused to head for shore and then fled — points to the same failures that have caused disaster after disaster: overloading, no life jackets, and no one held to account until after the bodies are counted. Until those rules are enforced, every funeral, market trip and school run by boat carries the same risk.
