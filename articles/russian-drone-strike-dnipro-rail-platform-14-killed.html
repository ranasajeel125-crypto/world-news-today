Russian Drone Strike Kills at Least 14 on Crowded Dnipro Rail Platform

world · 29 Oct 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (Europe Desk)

A jet-powered drone struck a platform at Dnipro's main station where a commuter train was preparing to depart for Zaporizhzhia, state railway Ukrzaliznytsia said.

A Russian drone strike killed at least 14 people on a crowded rail platform in the Ukrainian city of Dnipro on Thursday and five in daytime strikes on other cities, officials said, even as a local truce in Kherson raised cautious hopes for a resumption of diplomacy, Reuters reported.

The state rail company Ukrzaliznytsia said a jet-powered drone had struck a platform at Dnipro's main station where a commuter train was preparing to depart for the nearby city of Zaporizhzhia. Regional governor Oleksandr Hanzha said at least five more people were wounded, and Dnipro Mayor Borys Filatov wrote on Telegram: "Many people were killed. Lots were injured."

Images posted by President Volodymyr Zelenskiy showed bodies strewn across the ground as rescuers rushed the wounded away on stretchers. "Russia is deliberately hunting civilians: on trains, at stations, in residential buildings," Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergiy Koretskyi said on Telegram. "There is no justification for this terror, and there never can be."

Two teenagers were killed on a central street in Chernihiv in northern Ukraine, and three people were killed in a strike on an apartment building in the central city of Kremenchuk. In Chernihiv, 23 people were also wounded in a drone attack on a busy downtown street.

Moscow had made clear that a six-day humanitarian truce brokered with US help applied only to south Ukraine's Kherson region, to enable humanitarian relief to reach the Russian-held town of Oleshky, marooned in a war zone since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in early 2022.

Elsewhere, Russian drone and missile attacks on critical infrastructure including the rail network have been making civilian life increasingly fraught as well as burdening the economy, exploiting an acute shortage of air defence missiles. Like Kyiv, Moscow denies deliberately targeting civilians.

The strike hit at around 11:00 in the morning, when passengers were waiting on the platform to board a train heading for Zaporizhzhia. State-owned Ukrzaliznytsia said a Russian jet-powered unmanned aerial vehicle struck a railway station platform where train No. 866, travelling from Dnipro to Zaporizhzhia, was standing at the time. A regular suburban commuter train and a scheduled passenger train were both at the platform when the drone hit.

The casualty figures rose through the day as rescuers worked at the scene. Governor Hanzha initially reported 13 deaths and at least five people injured, then updated the number of wounded to 10, then 13, then 14, before confirming that the death toll had risen to 14. By Thursday afternoon he said 14 people had been killed and 14 injured, 13 of whom had been hospitalised. Six of the wounded were in critical condition, including an 11-year-old boy. All had sustained multiple shrapnel wounds, and a 40-year-old man had had both legs amputated.

President Zelenskiy called the attack "horrific" and demanded action by Kyiv's allies, including tougher sanctions. Images he posted showed bodies strewn across the ground as rescuers rushed the wounded away on stretchers, and emergency service photographs showed rescuers carrying an injured person on a stretcher after the attack. Dnipro's mayor, Borys Filatov, wrote on Telegram: "Many people were killed. Lots were injured."

The Dnipro strike was the deadliest attack on a Ukrainian railway station since 2022, and it came on a day when at least 19 people were killed across the country — 14 in Dnipro, two teenagers on a central street in Chernihiv, and three people in a strike on an apartment building in Kremenchuk. In Chernihiv, 23 people were also wounded in a drone attack on a busy downtown street. By the end of the day, local officials put the toll at at least 20 killed and more than 70 injured in strikes across Ukraine.

The attack came even as a fragile local ceasefire held in the south. The six-day humanitarian truce, brokered with US help, applied only to the Kherson region, to enable humanitarian relief to reach the Russian-held town of Oleshky, which has been marooned in a war zone since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in early 2022. The town, under Russian occupation since 2022, has faced months of food, water and medicine shortages, and the truce held into a third day even as the strikes continued elsewhere. Moscow had made clear the truce applied only to that region, and the Dnipro attack showed that the fighting elsewhere had not paused.

The strike fits a wider pattern in the war. Russian drone and missile attacks on critical infrastructure, including the rail network, have been making civilian life increasingly fraught as well as burdening the economy, exploiting an acute shortage of air defence missiles. Ukraine's railways are a lifeline: they carry civilians between cities, move freight and supplies, and have been central to evacuations since the invasion began. Attacks on stations and trains strike at that lifeline and at the daily routines of ordinary Ukrainians.

Prime Minister Koretskyi's words captured the Ukrainian government's framing of the attack: "Russia is deliberately hunting civilians: on trains, at stations, in residential buildings. There is no justification for this terror, and there never can be." Moscow denies deliberately targeting civilians, as does Kyiv when it is accused of the same. But the images from the Dnipro platform — bodies on the ground, rescuers carrying the wounded on stretchers, a commuter train standing at the platform where families had been waiting to board — left little doubt about who had borne the cost of the strike.
