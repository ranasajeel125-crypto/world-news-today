US Carries Out 'Defensive' Strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island on Eve of Midterms

world · 3 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

US officials described the strikes as limited and defensive; no damage or casualty figures have been confirmed.

The United States carried out limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz late on Monday, US officials told US media, describing the action as defensive in nature and meant to protect US assets in the region. The Wall Street Journal first reported the strikes, citing US officials, and CNN, citing three US officials, and the Associated Press and Reuters, citing US officials, subsequently reported the same operation.

Iran's state-owned IRNA news agency reported several explosions on Qeshm Island in rapid succession late on Monday, with witnesses saying they saw two aircraft over the island. The semi-official Fars news agency said explosions were also heard in the port city of Bandar Lengeh, coming from the direction of the sea.

One US official told CNN the attack came in response to Iran firing at a US aircraft, while an official cited by the Associated Press said the targets were Iranians using surface-to-air missiles against US assets. Officials described the strikes as limited and said they did not mark the start of expanded operations or a return to major combat. The Pentagon referred questions to US Central Command, which had not publicly commented at the time of the reports.

The Wall Street Journal reported that the strikes were the latest in a series of smaller-scale strikes the United States has carried out in recent weeks without publicising them, with officials saying the operations were intended to restrict Iran's ability to attack commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and were kept quiet so as not to suggest a resumption of major combat operations. A source told the Journal that the US has conducted limited airstrikes against Iran over recent weeks, and the officials explained that the attack was a defensive measure to prevent Iran from targeting commercial vessels passing through the strait.

The strikes came on the eve of the US midterm elections on Tuesday. President Donald Trump said on Monday that a deal with Tehran could happen and, when asked whether he was considering striking Iran after the elections, said "no". Vice President JD Vance said on Monday that the United States and Iran had made some progress toward a negotiated settlement.

Earlier on Monday, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed it had captured two US reconnaissance drones south of Qeshm and destroyed an MQ-9 drone east of the Strait of Hormuz. Those claims have not been independently verified, and no damage or casualty figures from the strikes themselves have been confirmed. The IRGC Navy also announced that an oil tanker deviating from its route in the eastern Strait of Hormuz had collided with a mine and caught fire, an incident that was later reported as an LNG tanker striking a mine in the strait.

The Strait of Hormuz is a key route for oil shipments, and Reuters reported on Tuesday that oil prices edged up after the report of the strikes, raising concerns about an escalation of the conflict that began in late February with US and Israeli attacks on Iran. The strait is the narrow channel between Iran and Oman through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas must pass, and any sustained threat to shipping there raises insurance costs, pushes up freight rates and feeds directly into global energy prices.

Qeshm Island is the largest island in the Persian Gulf, and it sits in the Strait of Hormuz near the Iranian port of Bandar Abbas. Its position makes it strategically important: from Qeshm, Iran can monitor and, if it chooses, threaten the shipping that passes through the strait, and the island hosts military facilities, including missile sites and air defence systems, that are part of Iran's defences in the Gulf. The United States has targeted the island before, and it did so again on 17 November, when US fighter jets struck an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island, hours after Iranian drones were launched at Bahrain. That strike used precision munitions and was described by a US military official as self-defence, in response to IRGC aggression targeting American personnel and assets. Iranian media reported that an air-defence missile launcher in the island's Nariman area was targeted, though the extent of damage to military facilities was unclear, and a provincial official said the attack caused no damage to residential, commercial or infrastructural areas.

The pattern of the strikes, limited, defensive and largely unpublicised, reflects the awkward position the United States finds itself in. The war with Iran began in late February with US and Israeli attacks, and it has continued through the year with attacks on shipping, strikes on military targets and a diplomatic effort to reach a negotiated settlement. The administration has sought to keep the conflict contained, avoiding a return to major combat operations, while at the same time responding to Iranian attacks on its forces and on commercial shipping. The strikes on Qeshm are part of that pattern: a limited response to a specific threat, intended to deter further attacks without escalating the war.

For Iran, the strikes are a reminder that the United States retains the ability to hit its military facilities at will, and that its attempts to pressure American forces and Gulf shipping carry a cost. For the Gulf states, the strikes are a source of anxiety, because they raise the risk of a wider conflict that could draw in their own territory, as the drone attack on Bahrain showed. Washington's Gulf allies remained on alert hours after the United States struck Qeshm Island, and the region's governments have been pressing both sides to avoid an escalation.

For the markets, the strikes are a reminder that the war in the Gulf is not over, and that oil prices can rise sharply on any sign of escalation. The edging up of oil prices after the report of the strikes was a modest reaction, but it showed that traders are watching the strait closely, and that a more serious incident, a ship sunk, a crew killed, or a closure of the strait, could send prices much higher.

What happens next depends on whether the diplomatic track can produce a deal. Trump has said a deal with Tehran could happen, and Vance has said the two sides have made some progress toward a negotiated settlement, but the strikes on Qeshm show that the military track is still active, and that the two tracks are running in parallel. Until one of them produces a result, the Strait of Hormuz will remain one of the most dangerous stretches of water in the world.
