320,000 Protest Across France as Clashes Erupt in Paris on Third Weekend of Unrest

Hundreds of thousands of mostly young protesters took to the streets across France on Saturday in a third consecutive weekend of demonstrations over living costs and education, the Associated Press reported. France's government estimated that 320,000 people protested nationwide on Saturday, fewer than in previous protests but still a significant turnout, while a coalition of youth organisations and unions representing high school and university students put the figure at nearly half a million, including 200,000 in Paris. More than 560 arrests were reported by night.

About 70,000 police and gendarmes were deployed across the country, and Paris marches were rerouted to the capital's edge. Youth-led demonstrations were also reported in Germany and Italy, and in Portugal and the Netherlands, where housing affordability and demands for better public services featured prominently. French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez warned of a very high risk of disorder, especially after dark on Halloween. Police had arrested thousands of people following riots and looting that marred the largely peaceful protest marches the previous two Saturdays.

Mass protests on Oct. 17 that deteriorated into some of France's worst rioting in years drew more than 500,000 people around France, and demonstrations on Oct. 24 drew 410,000, according to government estimates. Organisers' turnout estimates are much higher. On 17 October, the Interior Ministry estimated 550,000 people took part nationwide, including 110,000 in Paris, while organisers put the figure at 800,000. Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said 1,200 people were arrested that day and 150 police officers were injured. Masked groups and looters turned the day into the worst urban violence France has seen in years: the historic town hall in Limoges burned to a shell, the tourist office in Lyon was set on fire, and rioters targeted boutiques, supermarkets and public buildings in several cities. A day later, between 410,000 and 800,000 people protested again nationwide, in some cases defying local protest bans. President Emmanuel Macron condemned the violence and the actions of those "who sought to bring down the Republic", and the day after the riots he went on television to address the country. The interior minister offered to resign on 19 October; Macron refused, telling him to stay, to find new ways of dealing with looters, and to ensure the authorities were better prepared. Macron also ordered an inquiry into why the authorities had so badly underestimated the risk.

The Interior Ministry told reporters that of the 320,000 protesting on Saturday, 70,000 people are estimated to have turned out for the main protest in Paris, which police moved outside the city centre to reduce the risk of unrest around government buildings and tourist sites after a last-minute court battle. At Porte de Bagnolet, on the eastern edge of Paris, thousands gathered behind a line of students who led the march hand in hand. The crowd streamed along the Boulevards des Maréchaux, the ring of avenues that skirts the edge of Paris, in a march that was joyful for hours, the Associated Press reported, with whimsical Halloween costumes lightening the mood. In the main Paris march, each group carried its own banner: high school students, climate activists and people angry about the cost of living.

Later, clashes broke out near Porte de Charenton, where the march ended. Protesters hurled glass bottles and fireworks at police, and officers responded with volleys of stun grenades and tear gas. Many peaceful marchers turned back, coughing, their eyes streaming with tears, and left the march, some scattering into the nearby Bois de Vincennes. The Paris police prefecture said a hard core of several hundred rioters at the rear of the march attacked officers with projectiles, including a Molotov cocktail, and dozens of masked people dressed in black used hammers to pry up paving stones and hurl them at police, with at least one incendiary device thrown. Officers responded with tear gas and several baton charges, and a car was set on fire during the protests. In Lyon, tensions emerged after some demonstrators tried to leave the authorised route near Place Jean Mace and move towards the city centre, prompting police to use tear gas.

The rallies started last month as a movement of high school students protesting neglected buildings, teacher shortages and a university admissions system seen as unfair to disadvantaged students. Labour unions and others angry at President Emmanuel Macron joined in with a variety of demands. The movement began on 17 September, when teachers at the Antoine de Saint-Exupéry High School in Créteil, a working-class suburb southeast of Paris, went on strike to draw attention to staff shortages. Students soon joined them, and the protest spread to high schools in Lyon, Marseille, Paris and other major cities, with students barricading schools over the lack of teachers, overcrowded classrooms, overpacked schedules and crumbling infrastructure. By 1 October, government figures indicated that more than 1,000 of France's approximately 3,700 high schools had been affected, and the protests have continued throughout October. Students also called for an overhaul of Parcoursup, the university admissions platform, saying it is too complex and opaque and disadvantages pupils from less affluent backgrounds. Their immediate demands are smaller classes, enough teachers, better buildings and less exhausting timetables, and the movement has brought together students with trade unions such as the General Confederation of Labor and the Yellow Vests, the grassroots movement that led anti-government protests in 2018.

Macron said that Saturday's marches would stay out of city centres to protect shops. Police also barred people from entering Place de la Republique and Les Halles from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m. on Saturday and Sunday, and unaccompanied minors faced a separate 9 p.m. curfew around the Champs-Elysees. On 26 October, the government proposed additional measures, including making changes to Parcoursup by 2027 and fully replacing it by 2028, and the education minister promised 3,000 substitute teachers for the worst-hit schools. But the unrest occurs against the backdrop of France's upcoming presidential election in April 2027, a debt crisis and a government accused of doing little to address the problems facing people. Macron cannot run for office again, and after the protests he is seen as a lame duck who has lost control of his country and connection to his people, according to the Council on Foreign Relations. The big unions have called a strike for 5 November, and the movement shows no sign of ending: what began as a dispute over broken schools has become a wider expression of anger at deteriorating public services, overstretched household budgets and a political system that many of the young protesters feel offers little hope of change.
