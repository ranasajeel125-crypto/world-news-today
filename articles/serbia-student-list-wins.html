Vučić Concedes Defeat as Serbia's Student List Wins Parliamentary Election

politics · 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Elena Petrova (Balkans Correspondent)

Crowds celebrated in Belgrade on Sunday night after Vučić conceded defeat to the student-backed list.

Serbia's Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat to the student-led protest movement in parliamentary elections on Sunday, ending a long grip on power that began when he first became prime minister in 2014 and then president in 2017.

“We still do not have final results, on the basis of a sample ... it is clear that we have lost these elections, the Student List has won, they will have between 135 and 140 seats and we will have 105, no one else has entered parliament,” Vučić said.

Vučić, who resigned as president on September 27 to lead his coalition's list, had called the snap elections in September. They came after almost two years of student-led demonstrations against perceived corruption and creeping authoritarianism, triggered by the deaths of 16 people when a train station canopy collapsed in the city of Novi Sad in 2024.

In Belgrade on Sunday night, tens of thousands of people filled the city's main boulevards to celebrate, after a movement that began as street protests turned into the greatest threat to Vučić's rule since he first took power.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the Student List on its victory, saying the Commission stood ready to support reforms on the rule of law, media freedom and the fight against corruption and to work together to advance Serbia's future in the European Union.

The students' win marks a profound political shift in the country of some 6 million people, and the disparate group of 250 student-backed candidates must now turn from protest and campaigning to governing.

The concession came strikingly fast. Polls closed at 8pm local time on Sunday, October 25, and Vučić conceded defeat at 9:30pm, an hour and a half later, in a televised speech. Few in the student movement had dared to believe he would concede so quickly. He told supporters at his party's election-night event that his party projected the Students' List would take around 2.2 million votes, against around 1.7 million for his own list, and blamed Serbia's independent media as well as alleged interference by unnamed European countries and an "American deep state" for the loss.

Partial results put the Student List, the main opposition movement, in the lead with 140 of 250 seats in the National Assembly, a clear absolute majority, while Vučić's coalition secured only 104 seats. A government needs 126 seats, so the students were on course to clear that bar with room to spare. No other list entered parliament, according to Vučić's own figures.

Turnout helped drive the result. The State Election Commission said that by 6pm, voter turnout had reached 64.9 percent, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992. Long queues had formed at polling stations during the day, and the high turnout was widely seen as favouring the opposition.

The celebrations began within minutes of the concession. In Belgrade late on Sunday, drivers in cars draped in Serbian flags honked their horns through the streets as fireworks lit up the sky and crowds flooded central squares and the area around parliament. Hundreds of thousands of Serbians took to the streets to celebrate, and people in Novi Sad — the city where the movement began — also celebrated in the streets. "I feel as though I am finally living in a free country," student Mila Martic told reporters, as people raised three fingers, the Student List ballot symbol and a traditional Serbian greeting. Supporters of the Student List drove around Belgrade in cars, honking horns, singing, shouting and chanting: "Aleksandar is gone."

At the Student List headquarters, supporters hugged, cried and chanted, "Victory, victory!" Candidate Radomir Saicic, a university professor, declared: "Serbia has won as we promised. Never again a tyrant in this country," while urging peaceful, dignified celebrations and "serious, responsible hard work" from Monday.

The movement behind the victory began with a disaster. In November 2024, a concrete canopy at the newly renovated railway station in Novi Sad collapsed, killing 16 people. The tragedy was widely blamed on corruption and cronyism in public construction contracts, and students began demonstrating to demand accountability. Over almost two years, the protests grew into some of Serbia's largest since the 2000 overthrow of strongman Slobodan Milosevic, spreading from university campuses to towns across the country and drawing in teachers, farmers, doctors and pensioners.

The Student List itself is unlike most political groups. It formally entered the race on September 14, when supporters marched through central Belgrade to hand in their papers. Its list of 250 candidates includes university professors, actors, farmers, athletes and doctors, and is led by cardiologist Ilija Srdanovic, who was in tears after Vučić's announcement. Notably, no students actually appear on the election list; the candidates are little-known experts and professionals chosen to carry the movement's programme into parliament. It is still unclear who will be prime minister.

Vučić had dominated Serbian politics for 14 years, as prime minister or president since 2014, with his Serbian Progressive Party governing since 2012. He had been endorsed by Russian President Vladimir Putin and had denounced the student movement as a foreign-led attempt to unseat him. His defeat ends that long grip on power and opens a new political era in the Balkan country.

The reaction from Brussels came quickly. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the Student List on its victory, saying the Commission stood ready to support reforms on the rule of law, media freedom and the fight against corruption, and to work together to advance Serbia's future in the European Union. Serbia has been an EU candidate country for years, but its accession talks have stalled amid concerns about democratic backsliding, media freedom and the rule of law — the very issues the student movement campaigned on.

The hard part now begins. The disparate group of 250 student-backed candidates, most with no political experience, must turn from protest and campaigning to governing a country of about 6.4 million voters, forming a government, passing a budget and delivering the reforms they promised. Their supporters' message on election night was that the victory was only the start: as Saicic put it, the work ahead demands serious and responsible effort from Monday onwards.
