Newsom Commutes All 548 California Death Sentences in Largest Mass Clemency in US History

California Governor Gavin Newsom announced on Thursday, 5 November, he is commuting the death sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row to life in prison without the possibility of parole, in the largest mass commutation in American history, the Death Penalty Information Center said. The move effectively clears out California's death row, although the state has not executed a prisoner for 20 years, and those on death row will instead serve life sentences without the possibility of parole.

California has the largest death row in the Western Hemisphere, with 529 men and 19 women currently sentenced to death, according to the governor's office. The move cuts the total number of people sentenced to death across the United States by nearly a third. According to the Death Penalty Information Center, 1,388 people now remain sentenced to death throughout the country, the lowest number since 1984. By granting clemency to 548 people, Newsom has more than doubled the total number of clemencies granted in capital cases in the last 50 years, and his announcement marks the eighth time in modern history that a governor has granted clemency to all condemned inmates in their state, though none at this scale. "Though other governors have granted universal clemencies, none have been at this scale," said Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, who called the announcement "a decisive moment in the history of the death penalty in the United States".

The commutations do not repeal the death penalty, which California voters chose to keep in 2012 and 2016, and prosecutors can still seek death sentences in new cases. Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions shortly after taking office in 2019, and California has not carried out an execution since 2006. The commutations change sentences without overturning convictions, granting pardons or making prisoners eligible for release, and restitution orders will remain in place, according to an official fact sheet. Those whose death sentences were changed will remain incarcerated for life without the possibility of parole.

About a third of the commutations take effect immediately. The remaining two-thirds — cases involving people convicted of more than one felony, about 382 people — require approval from the California Supreme Court under the state constitution, and Newsom submitted those cases to the court on Thursday. Under the California Constitution, governors cannot grant clemency to repeat felony offenders without the court's approval, meaning the fate of nearly 70 per cent of the inmates rests with the state Supreme Court. In his letter to the court, Newsom asked it to recommend a path for the 382 inmates by the end of the year so he can act before his term ends. If the court does not act before Newsom leaves office, the requests would not roll over to the next governor. Newsom can grant a blanket commutation to the other 166 inmates with the stroke of a pen.

In an open letter to Californians, Newsom wrote that the death penalty was unjust and error-prone. "My decision today comes after nearly three decades of openly opposing state-sanctioned executions as an elected official," he wrote. "But as Governor, I have concluded the state cannot morally kill them," he wrote, adding that offenders could now die in obscurity while their victims are remembered and honoured. He said life without parole was the most severe punishment he could lawfully impose. Catholic advocates for abolishing the death penalty praised the decision, with Krisanne Vaillancourt Murphy, executive director of the Catholic Mobilizing Network, saying it "demonstrates how justice can be rooted in mercy and restoration instead of death and retribution" and "advances human dignity".

Among those on death row are serial killers Randy Kraft and Charles Ng, and Richard Allen Davis, who kidnapped and murdered 12-year-old Polly Klaas in 1993, CNN reported. The announcement comes as the death penalty faces renewed scrutiny following Tennessee's failed execution of Christa Pike in late September.

California's death penalty has a long and troubled history. The state has executed 13 people since 1976, the last on 17 January 2006, when Clarence Ray Allen, 76, was put to death by lethal injection at San Quentin State Prison, making him the second-oldest person put to death in the United States since capital punishment resumed nationwide in 1976. Executions stopped after that because a federal judge halted the scheduled execution of Michael Angelo Morales in February 2006, finding that the way California administered its three-drug lethal injection protocol created too great a risk that an inmate would suffer extreme pain, and the state could neither have a licensed medical professional administer the injection nor fix its procedures. On 13 March 2019, Newsom signed an executive order declaring a moratorium on executions, calling California's death penalty system "unfair, unjust, wasteful" and "protracted", repealing the state's lethal injection protocol and closing the execution chamber at San Quentin. At the time there were 737 prisoners on death row. In 2022, he ordered the dismantlement of California's death row and the relocation of its population to other state prisons, and in 2020 he signed the Racial Justice Act, which gives death-sentenced prisoners a process to seek relief from convictions or sentences obtained on the basis of race, ethnicity or national origin.

California voters have twice rejected ballot measures to abolish capital punishment. In 2012, Proposition 34, which would have replaced the death penalty with life in prison, failed after garnering support from 48 per cent of voters. In 2016, voters faced two death penalty measures at once: Proposition 62, which would have replaced the death penalty with a strict life sentence, failed with 46 per cent, while Proposition 66, which retains the death penalty and expedites state capital appeals, passed with 51 per cent. In 2017, the California Supreme Court upheld Proposition 66 in Briggs v. Brown but severely limited its core provisions, concluding that its five-year deadline for completing appeals was a non-binding exhortation rather than a mandatory rule. The death row population has fallen steadily, to 563 inmates in October 2026, the lowest since 2011, because of suicides, deaths from other causes, fewer juries willing to sentence people to death, and resentencings by newly elected district attorneys.

Newsom, a Democrat who is expected to seek his party's presidential nomination in 2028, is nearing the end of his second and final term as governor, which ends in January, Reuters reported. His successor, Governor-elect Xavier Becerra, who is also Catholic, has committed to maintaining a moratorium on capital punishment. "By bookending his time as governor first with a moratorium and now this mass clemency, Governor Newsom has irrevocably changed California's relationship with the death penalty," Maher said. Considered a potential candidate for the 2028 presidential election, Newsom's move could endear him to liberal voters in a Democratic primary, though it could raise hurdles in a general election.
