Denmark Ratifies Trump Greenland Security Deal Unanimously, Ending Months of Arctic Standoff

world · 19 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Denmark's parliament voted 109-0 to ratify the Greenland security agreement, the final step before the deal with the United States enters into force.

Denmark's parliament on Thursday unanimously ratified a security agreement with the United States covering Greenland, hoping to end a standoff over the Arctic island's future that has rattled transatlantic relations.

The deal was backed by 109 Danish lawmakers and none voted against, the parliamentary tally showed. All 109 members present in the 179-seat parliament, known as the Folketing, voted in favour of the text, which was adopted without opposition or abstention after a second reading that went through without any debate.

The United States, Denmark and Greenland signed the agreement in September to resolve tensions after US President Donald Trump repeatedly threatened to take control of the mineral-rich island, a self-governing Danish territory that is home to 57,000 people. The agreement was signed in New York on September 22 by Trump, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen.

The agreement is an executive deal and does not require a vote by the US Senate. It will formally enter into force once Denmark's foreign ministry informs the United States in a diplomatic note that the necessary procedures have been completed.

The deal expands the US military's role in Greenland, including permission to establish a "Golden Dome" missile defence system, while reaffirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlanders' right to self-determination. It gives the United States the right to establish two new bases on the territory and a say over foreign military activity and sensitive investments, and it supplements a 1951 accord governing the American military presence on the island.

The signatories will now start work on a new Greenland law to screen outside investors on security grounds and on plans for the two new US military bases, as outlined in the agreement, Denmark's Defence Minister Jeppe Bruus told Reuters. "I expect that the US will now also start to be more concrete on the purposes and the arrangement around these two areas," he said.

Greenland's parliament ratified the deal earlier this month, in a vote of 16 in favour, none against and seven abstentions. Denmark's parliament passed the text at a first reading last week with backing from all parties, before Thursday's final vote completed the parliamentary process on both sides.

The vote closes a chapter that began in January, when Trump's refusal to rule out military or economic force to acquire the island triggered a diplomatic crisis within NATO and months of intensive negotiations. The agreement capped months of secret negotiations, ending a bitter dispute over the semi-autonomous Danish territory that in January had escalated into a major diplomatic crisis between members of the Western alliance.

For Greenlanders, the relief is real but the wounds are fresh. Naaja Nathanielsen, a Greenlandic member of the Danish parliament, said Trump's pressure had led to distrust in Greenland towards the US administration. "It's been really difficult for Greenlanders these past two years. It's been seeping into every aspect of our lives," Nathanielsen told Reuters. She hoped the agreement would open a path to dialogue after hurtful rhetoric within the Western alliance.

The US ambassador to Denmark, Kenneth Howery, welcomed the vote result, calling the agreement "a tremendous accomplishment" that bolsters the security of all sides, including NATO. Danish lawmakers expressed relief and hope that the deal will normalise the situation in Greenland.

Not everyone in Copenhagen is convinced the deal settles the question. Mikkel Vedby Rasmussen, a political science professor at the University of Copenhagen, said the deal was more likely a beginning than an end. "With the treaty in hand, the US will be able to demand greater influence on decisions that have security policy implications and on part of the extraction of strategic resources," he said.

Other experts say the new agreement does not add much US military access to Greenland in practice. The northernmost US base is already in Greenland, which lies on the shortest route from Europe to North America, and the island's strategic value to Washington has long been established. The question, in their view, is how far the new text turns that long-standing presence into a broader say over Greenland's future.

Resources remain the most sensitive part of the story. Trump said last month that "massive oil, gas and mineral exploration" was set to begin on Greenland, a contentious statement on a territory that has banned new drilling for crude. Denmark has said any resource extraction must respect local law, and the new investment-screening law the signatories are now to draft will be the first test of how that promise works in practice.

What happens next is procedural but not trivial: once Copenhagen sends its diplomatic note to Washington, the agreement enters into force, and the work begins on the investor-screening law and the two new bases. The relief in the Danish parliament was genuine, and the unanimous vote, with support from every party, is a measure of how badly Copenhagen wanted this dispute closed.

Why it matters: the Greenland crisis was the sharpest test of the transatlantic alliance in years, with an American president openly refusing to rule out force against the territory of a NATO ally. A unanimous ratification does not erase the distrust the episode created in Greenland, or the expectation in Washington that the deal buys greater influence over Arctic security and resources. What it does is replace threats with a written agreement, and give all three sides a framework to argue inside rather than a crisis to manage from the outside.
