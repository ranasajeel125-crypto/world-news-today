Two Italians Killed in Iranian Drone Attack in Kuwait as the Gulf War Widens

world · 26 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Kuwait said its military detected and dealt with several drones in its airspace. The attack caused material damage and left two more people injured.

Two Italians were killed in an Iranian drone attack in Kuwait on Thursday, Italy's foreign minister said, adding that the victims were technicians working for the Italian unit of German defence group Rheinmetall.

The attack marked the second reported Iranian attack on the US-allied Gulf state this month, and one that killed two foreign civilian technicians working on a Gulf ally's air defences. In Rome, Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani told the Senate that the two Rheinmetall Italia technicians were working at an air-defence surveillance station in Kuwait when it was targeted by Iranian drones.

“Following a powerful explosion, both of our compatriots, according to information coming from Kuwait, have died. Two other people were injured: a Pakistani national and a captain in the Kuwaiti army,” Tajani said. “I would like to express my deepest sympathy and solidarity to the families of the Italian victims at this time of profound grief,” he added.

Kuwait's Defence Ministry confirmed that the two Italians had died of their injuries, after initially reporting that four people had been wounded by falling debris when a ‘vital facility’ was targeted in what it said was an Iranian attack. It confirmed that the other two people injured were a Kuwaiti and a Pakistani national. The ministry said the country's military had detected and dealt with several drones within Kuwaiti airspace, adding that the attack had also caused material damage.

The two accounts agree on the core of what happened — a drone attack on a sensitive site, two Italians dead, two more people hurt — but they leave the most operational questions unanswered. Neither side said how many drones were involved, how many were intercepted, or whether the explosion that killed the technicians came from a drone that got through the defences or from debris falling after an interception. The exact location of the air-defence surveillance station was not disclosed, and the victims were not named.

Iran's Foreign Ministry did not immediately respond to a Reuters request for comment on the incident. Rheinmetall declined to comment. That silence, on both sides, is itself part of the story: Tehran has in the past been quick to claim attacks it carried out on US-linked targets in the Gulf, and its failure to comment on this one leaves open whether the strike was meant as a message, a miscalculation, or something in between.

What is not in doubt is the pattern. Earlier in November, Iran said it had targeted US drone and missile sites in Kuwait after the United States struck Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. That was the first reported Iranian attack on Kuwait this month; Thursday's strike on an air-defence station is the second. The target matters: a surveillance station is the eyes of a country's air defence, and hitting it is a direct challenge to the Gulf states' ability to see and stop the next attack coming.

The Kuwait strike is the latest in a chain of attacks that has spread across the Gulf in the space of a fortnight. Iran carried out drone attacks on US bases in Bahrain on 15, 17 and 18 November, and missile and drone strikes on US bases in Bahrain again on 21 November. On 23 November, Tehran vowed to confront the US naval blockade. Two days later, on 25 November, Yemen's Houthis claimed attacks on Saudi Arabia's largest oil refinery at Ras Tanura and on the Riyadh financial district, and two oil tankers hit mines and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz.

Taken together, those incidents describe a war that is no longer confined to a single front. The fighting now touches Bahrain, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and the Strait of Hormuz — the waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil moves — and it is being fought with drones, mines and missiles against refineries, ports, air bases and now air-defence stations. The weapons are cheap and the targets are spread across thousands of kilometres of coastline, which is exactly what makes the campaign so hard to stop.

The human cost is also changing shape. Until now, the Gulf attacks had produced damage and disruption but, as far as the public record shows, few deaths. The killing of two European civilian technicians — men who were in Kuwait to work on its defences, not to fight — crosses a line that the earlier strikes had not. Italy is a NATO ally, and Rheinmetall is one of Europe's largest defence companies. The deaths give a European government, and a European defence industry, a direct stake in a war that many in Europe had treated as an American and Gulf affair.

That stake is likely to force questions in Rome that go beyond condolences. Tajani announced the deaths in the Senate, the most formal setting available to a foreign minister, which suggests the Italian government expects the matter to become a political one — about the safety of Italian nationals working on Gulf defence contracts, about Italy's role in the region, and about what, if anything, Europe does when its citizens are killed in a war it is not formally part of. None of those questions has been answered yet, and none was answered on Thursday.

The attack also lands in the middle of a diplomatic moment that was supposed to be about containing the war. On Wednesday, a day before the Kuwait strike, US President Donald Trump and NATO chief Mark Rutte met at Mar-a-Lago in Florida, where the war in Ukraine and the US-Israeli war on Iran were the primary subjects of their talks. The joint statement they issued said nothing about what was discussed on Iran — and within 24 hours, Iranian drones had killed two Europeans in Kuwait. Whatever was said in Florida, it did not stop the next attack.

Kuwait now faces the same problem as its neighbours: how to defend sites that are both vital and widely spread. Its Defence Ministry said its military had detected and dealt with several drones, which means its systems worked well enough to see the attack coming. But detection is not the same as prevention, and two men are dead and a facility is damaged. The Gulf states have spent heavily on air defence in recent years, including on the very kind of surveillance stations that were targeted on Thursday, and the question the attack poses is whether that spending is keeping pace with the cheap, expendable drones being used against it.

The regional response is already taking shape on a different track. Turkiye has ratified the Mecca defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, a sign that the Gulf states are looking beyond Washington for security guarantees as the war widens. Whether that pact, or any other arrangement, can protect a surveillance station in Kuwait from a drone launched hundreds of kilometres away is a question no treaty can answer on its own.

Why it matters: the Kuwait attack is the moment the Gulf war stopped being a story about ships, refineries and bases and became a story about people — two Italian technicians who went to work on an air-defence station and did not come home. It is the second Iranian attack on Kuwait this month, it follows a fortnight of strikes across Bahrain, Saudi Arabia and the Strait of Hormuz, and it happened the day after the US president and the NATO chief met to discuss the war on Iran without saying publicly what they had decided. The test now is whether the deaths of two Europeans change the calculation — in Rome, in Washington, in Kuwait City and in Tehran — or whether, like the strikes that came before them, they are absorbed into a war that keeps widening by one attack at a time.
