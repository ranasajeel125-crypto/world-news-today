Taiwan Accuses China of Harassing Shipping With East Coast Patrol

world · 1 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Taiwan said two Chinese ships were involved in the latest east coast patrol, and that China also sent a ship close to the Taiwan-controlled Pratas Islands.

China's Coast Guard is harassing shipping with its latest patrol off Taiwan's east coast and trying to exert a fictional jurisdiction, Taiwan's Coast Guard said, adding that it had sent its own ships to keep watch, Reuters reported.

Responding late on Saturday to China's announcement of its latest east coast patrol, Taiwan's Coast Guard said two Chinese ships were involved and that it had sent its own forces to monitor. The Coast Guard Administration said it dispatched ships, including the Ji An, to monitor the area, and that China's patrols included the Chinese coast guard vessels Hushi (CCG 1308) and Meishan (CCG 2303). Chinese state media showed video of one of its ships broadcasting a radio message that it was carrying out law enforcement and offering help should any be needed. It did not say who the message was broadcast at, but showed footage of an unidentified fishing trawler.

Taiwan's Coast Guard said China's attempts to harass ships at sea through radio communications were a clear expansion of power that violate international law, undermine regional peace and stability, and deviate from reality. "Our country holds sovereign rights and jurisdiction over the exclusive economic zone east of Taiwan, and China has absolutely no jurisdiction over any of Taiwan's waters," it added.

China, which views Taiwan as its own territory, has stepped up activity off the island's east coast since June with regular coast guard patrols. Officials in Taiwan, which rejects Beijing's sovereignty claims, say they believe China is practising for a potential maritime blockade using overlapping military, paramilitary and civilian assets. Taipei regards the area off its east coast as critical to wartime supply routes from allies in the event of a blockade or conflict. China recognises no claims of sovereignty by Taiwan.

The east coast matters because it is Taiwan's open side. The island's west coast faces mainland China across the Taiwan Strait, a narrow waterway about 130 kilometres wide at its narrowest, where Chinese military aircraft and ships operate daily. The east coast faces the open Pacific, and in a conflict it would be the route by which supplies and reinforcements from allies such as the United States and Japan could reach the island. A Chinese presence there, even in the form of coast guard patrols rather than warships, signals that Beijing can operate on both sides of the island and can practise the skills a blockade would require: tracking ships, broadcasting instructions to them, and establishing a pattern of presence that normalises Chinese authority in waters Taiwan claims.

The tactic fits a pattern that analysts call grey-zone pressure: actions that fall short of open war but steadily change the status quo. Coast guard vessels, rather than naval warships, are the instrument of choice, because they allow Beijing to present its activity as law enforcement rather than military coercion, while still demonstrating that it can put ships where it wants them. The radio broadcasts are part of the same approach. By hailing passing vessels and announcing that it is carrying out law enforcement, the Chinese Coast Guard asserts a jurisdiction that Taiwan says does not exist, and forces every ship that hears the message to decide whether to answer, ignore it, or report it. Over time, repeated patrols and broadcasts can make an extraordinary claim feel routine — which is why Taiwan's Coast Guard chose the phrase "fictional jurisdiction" to describe it.

The patrols are part of a broader escalation that has been building for years. China has run large military exercises around Taiwan, including the Joint Sword series, and its aircraft cross the median line of the Taiwan Strait — the informal boundary both sides once respected — on a near-daily basis. Coast guard patrols around Taiwan's offshore islands of Kinmen and Matsu, which sit close to the Chinese mainland, have also become routine. The east coast patrols, which began in June, extend that pressure to the side of the island that was once considered relatively safe, and they have continued at regular intervals since.

China on Saturday also sent another Coast Guard ship close to the Taiwan-controlled Pratas Islands, which are strategically located at the top of the South China Sea, Taiwan's Coast Guard said separately. Taiwan has so far this year expelled Chinese Coast Guard vessels from Pratas waters 13 times, it added. The Pratas, known in Chinese as the Dongsha Islands, are a small atoll with no permanent civilian population, lying roughly between southern Taiwan and Hong Kong. They are garrisoned by Taiwan's Coast Guard and are strategically located at the northern entrance to the South China Sea, one of the world's busiest shipping lanes. Security experts see them as vulnerable to Chinese attack due to their distance — more than 400 km (250 miles) — from Taiwan. Their isolation makes them hard to reinforce and hard to defend, and they have long been discussed as a possible first target in a crisis, precisely because seizing them would be a major political blow without requiring an invasion of Taiwan itself.

The dispute over the waters around Taiwan turns on competing readings of international law. Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, a coastal state has sovereign rights over the resources in its exclusive economic zone, which extends 200 nautical miles from its coast, and jurisdiction over certain activities there, though foreign ships retain freedom of navigation. Taiwan says it holds sovereign rights and jurisdiction over the exclusive economic zone east of the island. China, which claims Taiwan as part of its territory, says its coast guard is operating in its own waters and enforcing its own laws. There is no court that can settle the question while the underlying sovereignty dispute remains unresolved, so the argument is fought at sea, patrol by patrol, broadcast by broadcast.

For Taiwan, the response has been to shadow the Chinese ships, document their movements and publicise them. Taiwan's Coast Guard said it sent its own ships to keep watch on the latest patrol, and it has expelled Chinese vessels from Pratas waters 13 times this year. The island's government has also invested in its own maritime monitoring, with officials saying Taiwan must be the master of its own maritime information. The strategy is to make sure that every Chinese patrol is seen, recorded and contested, so that presence does not quietly become acceptance. Whether that is enough to hold the line on the east coast, as the patrols become more regular, is the question that will shape the next phase of the cross-strait standoff.
