EU and China Urged to Join Forces on a Carbon Border Levy

climate · 24 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

climate · 24 Oct 2026 · 3 min read · By Elena Petrova (Climate Correspondent)

China and Europe should jointly introduce a levy on carbon-intensive imports like the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), Adair Turner, co-chair of the Energy Transitions Commission, told the Bund Summit in Shanghai on Thursday.

Turner said the two should commit to carbon prices high enough to drive decarbonisation of heavy industries such as cement and steel, and argued that China would eventually need to raise its carbon price to around $100 per tonne to fully decarbonise iron production by 2050 or 2060. China's national carbon market closed at RMB 96.66 per tonne (about $15) on Thursday, according to the Shanghai Environment and Energy Exchange, a fraction of the level Turner said would be needed.

Turner's central proposal was for a climate club. He said the biggest single thing he would love China to do is to say it wants to join with Europe in a climate club, with the two economies jointly applying a system like CBAM against the rest of the world. The idea is that if the world's two largest trading powers, and two of its largest emitters, agree on a common carbon price and a common border levy, other countries would find it very hard to stay outside the system. The levy would apply to carbon-intensive imports, accounting for the carbon costs that domestic producers already face, and would remove the advantage enjoyed by producers in countries with no carbon price at all.

Jeromin Zettelmeyer, director of the Bruegel think tank, made a similar argument at the same panel. He said a joint EU-China pricing commitment would create a market too large for other developing countries to ignore, effectively setting a global floor price for industrial carbon whether Washington joins or not. He pointed to the Open Coalition on Compliance Carbon Markets, launched by the EU, China and Brazil, which he described as a multilateral extension of Europe's CBAM. Turner, who is also the former chairman of the UK's Financial Services Authority, was sharply critical of the current US administration's approach to shared global problems, describing it as a disaster for dealing with issues like climate change.

The proposal comes at a moment when the EU's CBAM is moving from design to reality. The mechanism entered its definitive phase in January 2026, requiring importers of covered carbon-intensive goods to pay for the emissions embedded in those goods, under the bloc's carbon pricing system. During the earlier transitional phase, importers had to report their emissions but did not yet have to pay. Now the levy is real, and many Chinese exporters are struggling with compliance, having to measure, verify and report the carbon content of their products in ways they never had to before.

CBAM works by preventing what climate policy calls carbon leakage. If European producers have to pay for their carbon emissions under the EU's emissions trading system, but importers can bring in the same goods from countries with no carbon price, European industry is put at a disadvantage and emissions simply move abroad rather than falling. CBAM closes that gap by requiring importers to buy certificates covering the embedded emissions of the goods they bring in, aligning import costs with those faced by EU producers. The goods covered include iron and steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen, the heavy industries where carbon costs matter most.

The idea of China joining such a system is ambitious, and the obstacles are obvious. Trade tensions between Brussels and Beijing remain high, with disputes over subsidies for Chinese electric vehicles and solar panels, and over market access in both directions. Many developing economies see carbon border taxes as green protectionism, a way for rich countries to impose their climate rules on poorer ones, and China itself once said the EU's planned carbon border tax violated trade principles and would hurt prospects for economic growth. As the world's top manufacturer of industrial raw materials such as steel and cement, China could suffer the most from border tariffs of this kind, according to researchers at Tsinghua University.

Yet the landscape has shifted since those early objections. China now runs the world's largest national carbon market, and the EU has begun to recognise it. In November 2026 the European Commission published Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498, detailing how carbon payments made in other countries can reduce the number of certificates importers must purchase under CBAM. The regulation recognises China's national carbon market for border levy deductions, although free emissions allowances will not qualify for deductions. That is a practical step towards the kind of linkage Turner and Zettelmeyer are calling for, even if a full joint system remains distant.

The gap between the two carbon prices remains enormous. China's market closed at about $15 a tonne on the day of the Bund Summit, while Turner's $100 figure for decarbonising iron production points to how far Chinese prices would have to rise. Closing that gap would mean higher costs for Chinese heavy industry, and difficult choices about how fast to move. But Turner argued that the direction of travel is clear: without a carbon price high enough to make clean steel and cement competitive, the technologies needed to decarbonise heavy industry will not be deployed at scale.

For now, the idea of linked carbon markets is moving from conference talk to policy agenda. The EU's CBAM is in its definitive phase, China is expanding its own carbon market, and the two sides, along with Brazil, have launched a coalition on compliance carbon markets. Whether that becomes the climate club Turner envisages will depend on politics as much as economics, and on whether Beijing and Brussels can set aside their trade disputes long enough to agree on a shared price for carbon. If they do, the effect would reach far beyond Europe and China: a joint carbon price set by the world's two largest trading powers would, in Zettelmeyer's words, be a market too large for anyone else to ignore.
