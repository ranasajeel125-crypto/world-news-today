Taiwan Local Elections: KMT Holds Taipei as DPP Takes Taichung, and Voters Back a Return to Nuclear Power

politics · 28 Nov 2026 · 7 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Taiwan's opposition KMT kept Taipei, New Taipei and Taoyuan in Saturday's local elections, but the governing DPP took Taichung and rose from five to nine of the 22 mayor and magistrate posts, while voters backed a referendum to revive nuclear power. Results remain unofficial until the Central Election Commission confirms them on 4 December.

Taiwan's main opposition party, the Kuomintang (KMT), kept control of the capital and most of the island's local governments in the nine-in-one local elections held on Saturday, 28 November 2026, but the governing Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) of President Lai Ching-te recovered strongly from its 2022 rout and took the central city of Taichung. On the same day, voters approved a national referendum calling for the government to use nuclear power and drop the long-standing nuclear-free homeland energy policy, according to Central Election Commission (CEC) counts.

The scoreboard, on unofficial tallies reported by Taiwan's Central News Agency (CNA), was 12 mayor and county magistrate posts for the KMT, nine for the DPP and one for an independent. That is a sharp narrowing from 2022, when the KMT won 14 of the 22 races, the DPP only five, the Taiwan People's Party (TPP) one and independents two. The DPP's recovery was broad rather than concentrated in one place: it held its southern strongholds, flipped Taichung, and picked up counties across the centre and north of the island.

In Taipei, the most closely watched race of the night, KMT Mayor Chiang Wan-an secured a second term with about 53.7 percent of the vote against 45.7 percent for the DPP's Puma Shen, with nearly all ballots counted. Shen, a first-term DPP legislator whose energetic campaign had been dubbed the Yang Wave, conceded by telephone during the evening and congratulated Chiang. Nikkei Asia reported that more than 100,000 votes separated the two men in the end. Chiang's win matters beyond the capital: he had once been tipped as a likely KMT presidential candidate for 2028, although Nikkei noted he drew criticism for repeated stumbles during the race.

In New Taipei, Taiwan's most populous city, the KMT's Lee Shu-chuan beat DPP lawmaker Su Chiao-hui with roughly 51.2 percent of the vote to 48.3 percent, and Su conceded on Saturday night. In Taoyuan, KMT Mayor Chang San-cheng won re-election with about 55.7 percent. Together with Taipei, those two results meant the KMT held all three of the northern special municipalities it went into the election controlling.

The night's biggest upset came in Taichung, Taiwan's second-largest city, where DPP legislator Ho Hsin-chun took 50.7 percent of the vote against 48.4 percent for the KMT's Johnny Chiang, ending eight years of KMT rule in the city. Chiang, a party heavyweight and former KMT chair, had been expected to cruise to victory on the strength of his local network and his reputation with middle-of-the-road voters. Instead he conceded on Saturday night, in what CNA described as likely the biggest city to flip in the DPP's favour. Nikkei Asia noted that the defeat in Taichung could hurt the presidential prospects of the KMT's outgoing Taichung mayor, Lu Shiow-yen, who had been tipped as a possible 2028 contender.

The DPP comfortably held its southern strongholds. In Tainan, Chen Ting-fei will become the city's first woman mayor, and in Kaohsiung Lai Jui-lung declared victory over the KMT's Ko Chih-en. Elsewhere the DPP took Changhua, Yilan, Chiayi City and Hsinchu County from the KMT. The Hsinchu County win was especially striking: CNA noted that the traditional KMT bastion had not elected a DPP magistrate since 1997. The KMT, in turn, captured Penghu from the DPP. Among the six special municipalities, the two parties now split three apiece, against a 4-2 KMT advantage in 2022.

Two races were still too close to call late on Saturday night, and both may end in recounts. In Yunlin County, the KMT's Chang Chia-chun declared victory with a lead of just 148 votes over the DPP's Liu Chien-kuo — 175,795 to 175,647 — with 632 of 638 polling stations reporting. In Hualien, the KMT's Yu Shu-chen declared victory while leading independent county council speaker Chang Chun by fewer than 400 votes, and Chang said he would request a recount, noting that the number of invalid ballots exceeded 2,500. Both margins fall within the statutory 0.3 percent threshold for requesting a recount, and the CEC is due to confirm all results on 4 December.

The nuclear referendum was the other story of the night. National Referendum No. 22 asked voters whether they support the government using nuclear power to boost the economy and thereby abolishing the nuclear-free homeland policy. As of 9 p.m., CEC data showed 7,122,278 yes votes against 4,382,307 no votes, easily clearing the threshold of 5,009,095 affirmative votes — one quarter of all eligible voters — required for a proposal to pass. With about 98.6 percent of the count in, the yes side stood at roughly 7.35 million votes, about 62 percent, against 4.51 million no votes.

The vote was Taiwan's fourth national referendum on nuclear power since 2018, and it comes after the island completed its nuclear power phase-out in May 2025, when its last reactor was shut. The referendum does not by itself restart any reactor or set a timetable, and the result only becomes official when the CEC announces it — but it is a clear public instruction to a government that was already exploring plans to restart shuttered atomic plants. Turnout for the referendum was 46.07 percent, according to early CEC figures.

The scale of the exercise was enormous. Around 19.58 million people — about 85 percent of Taiwan's population — were eligible to vote in the local elections, and about 20 million in the referendum, which has a lower voting age of 18 compared with 20 for the local races. More than 11,000 local government positions were contested across 8,906 electoral districts, with 18,244 polling stations set up and more than 300,000 election workers mobilised. Polls ran from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., and votes were counted polling station by polling station through the evening.

Local elections in Taiwan are primarily about domestic governance and personalities rather than foreign policy, and Saturday's vote was no exception: the Associated Press reported that the campaign focused on local concerns such as social welfare benefits and livelihood issues. But the results will shape the dynamics and competition within Taiwan's political class, and in turn they could sway decisions on security reforms, defence spending and diplomatic priorities ahead of the 2028 presidential election.

That is why the vote was watched so closely, both in Taipei and in Beijing. The DPP has now held the presidency for a 10-year stretch, an unprecedented run since direct presidential elections began in 1996, winning three straight presidential contests. The KMT, which advocates friendlier ties with China, has long been stronger at the local level, holding 15 of the 22 local governments going into Saturday's vote. A poor showing would have cast doubt on the party's relevance after a decade out of power; Saturday's result lets it keep its local base while the DPP can claim a genuine recovery.

The backdrop to the vote was tense. Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense reported about 33 sorties by Chinese warplanes, including bombers and fighter jets, toward the island between Friday and Saturday. Reuters reported earlier in November that Taiwan had flagged about 2,700 China-linked accounts to Meta ahead of the vote, amid warnings of Chinese cyberattacks and disinformation campaigns. President Lai campaigned personally for DPP candidates in the final days, appearing on Thursday night to support the party's mayoral candidate in Taoyuan.

The result leaves both parties with something to claim and something to worry about. The KMT kept the capital and the north, and held on to most of the island's local governments despite losing ground. The DPP more than recovered from its 2022 low, taking a major industrial city and a string of counties it had not held for years. The Taiwan People's Party, which won Hsinchu City four years ago, has no mayor or magistrate seat this time: Hsinchu Mayor Kao Hung-an kept her post, but ran as an independent.

Why it matters: Saturday's vote was the first island-wide test of Taiwan's political balance since the DPP's third straight presidential win, and it produced a split verdict rather than a landslide for either side. The KMT remains the dominant force in local government, but its grip has loosened; the DPP has rebuilt its local base, but it still could not win the capital it has not held since 1994. Add a referendum that reverses a decade of nuclear-free energy policy, and the night reshapes the starting positions for the 2028 presidential race — with two razor-thin county races still to be settled by recounts, and the official results still to be confirmed on 4 December.
