Iran Strikes US Bases in Bahrain With Missiles and Drones; Bahrain Says Civilian Facility Hit

world · 22 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (World Desk)

Explosions were heard over Bahrain on Saturday night as Iran said its forces launched missile and drone strikes on US military positions in the kingdom; Bahrain said a civilian industrial facility was hit and caught fire, with no injuries reported.

Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said on Saturday night it had launched a combined missile-and-drone attack on American military positions in Bahrain, claiming it struck the radars of two US Patriot air defence systems — while Bahrain said an Iranian attack hit a civilian industrial facility, causing a fire but no injuries, and that its air defences intercepted a number of the incoming attacks.

The Revolutionary Guard said its Aerospace Force carried out the attack on US positions at the Al-Riffa and Awali bases in Bahrain, in a statement carried by Iranian state news agency IRNA and reported by Reuters. The Guards said the operation was retaliation for what it called continued aggression and repeated violations of Iranian airspace, and said it had hit the radars of two American Patriot air defence systems. The Guards said two American drones — an MQ-1 Predator and an MQ-9 Reaper — had violated Iranian airspace earlier on Saturday, and pointed to an attack on a radar installation on Iran's Hengam Island in the Gulf. Iranian state television broadcast video it said showed the two drones being shot down over the Strait of Hormuz, and footage it described as the missile-and-drone strike on the Bahrain bases.

Bahrain told a very different story about what the night's explosions had actually struck. The kingdom's Interior Ministry said in a post on X that an Iranian attack had targeted a civilian industrial facility, causing a fire at the site. "As a result of the heinous Iranian aggression, a civilian industrial facility was targeted, causing a fire at the site," the ministry said, adding that civil defence teams were working to bring the fire under control and that preliminary reports indicated material damage, with no injuries. The ministry gave no details about the location of the facility or the nature of the site.

Before the fire was reported, Bahrain's Interior Ministry had issued an alert urging residents to move to the nearest safe location because of a potential threat, without giving details. It later issued an all-clear, and then sent a second alert as the night's attacks continued. Bahrain's Defence Force said its air defences had intercepted a number of Iranian aerial attacks, and that shrapnel from the interceptions had caused material damage on the ground, but no casualties. It said Tehran was continuing its "systematic hostile approach" towards the kingdom.

The gap between the two accounts is the central fact of the night. Iran says it destroyed American military hardware at two bases. Bahrain says the only confirmed damage is to a civilian site and to property hit by falling debris. By Sunday morning there was no independent confirmation that any American equipment had been damaged: US Central Command did not immediately respond to a request for comment from Reuters, and a US official separately told CBS News that the latest Iranian attack attempts had not affected American forces or military assets in the region.

Even the Iranian accounts disagree with each other about the targets. The Revolutionary Guard's official statement, carried by IRNA, named the Al-Riffa and Awali Sakhir bases. But minutes before that statement was released, the semi-official Fars news agency reported that the Guard had attacked the Shaikh Isa and Al Juffair air bases in Bahrain. The Guard's statement did not address the discrepancy, and neither account has been independently verified.

The attack is the latest in a series of Iranian strikes on Bahrain that has run through November, and the first in that series in which Iran has claimed to use missiles as well as drones. The Guard said on Friday that it had targeted a US early-warning radar in the kingdom, and on Sunday 15 November that six of its drones had targeted the US air-defence radar at the al-Riffa helicopter base, with sirens sounding across the capital, Manama. Further attacks followed in the days after, each answered so far by interception. The wider war began on 28 February, when the United States and Israel attacked Iran, and Gulf Arab states have been drawn repeatedly into the fighting since.

Bahrain's position makes it a central target in that war. The island kingdom sits less than 300 kilometres from Iran's southern coast and hosts the headquarters of the US Navy's Fifth Fleet, one of the most important American military installations in the Gulf. Hours before the Bahrain strikes, the US military's Central Command had denied an Iranian claim that a surface-to-air missile had damaged an American F-16 fighter jet over the Gulf on Friday evening, saying its forces had shot down two Iranian drones and that there were no injuries or damage.

The confrontation spread further on the same night. The Saudi-led coalition said it had intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile early on Sunday, and Saudi Arabia briefly issued a danger alert for Mecca province before lifting it moments later. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said there was "no threat to Mecca", as attacks by the Iran-backed group on Saudi Arabia continued into another night. The Houthis have been firing missiles and drones at Saudi Arabia throughout the war, and the coalition has reported intercepting them repeatedly.

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes, was also hit again on Saturday. The Revolutionary Guard's navy said two oil tankers struck sea mines while trying to pass through the strait, according to Al Jazeera, which reported that the number of tankers hitting mines has been rising. The Guard said the strait remained closed, and claimed that US efforts to escort ships through the waterway had failed. The strait has been at the centre of the war since February, with shipping disrupted and insurance costs rising as attacks on vessels have continued.

Diplomacy has not stopped, even as the missiles fly. Iran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, said on Saturday that Tehran's two-day plan to cease hostilities and reopen the Strait of Hormuz was still on the table, calling it the sole proposal Iran had put forward. Iran's parliamentary speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, has told Al Jazeera that Tehran wanted to move beyond a state of "no war, no peace". But with each side now striking the other's positions in the Gulf, the space for a deal keeps narrowing, and the immediate question is whether Washington answers the Bahrain strikes directly.

Why it matters: the Bahrain strikes are the sharpest escalation yet of the open confrontation between Tehran and Washington in the Gulf, and the first time Iran has claimed to fire missiles — not just drones — at American positions in the kingdom that hosts the Fifth Fleet. The competing accounts also matter: if the Guards' claim about the Patriot radars is true, the United States has lost air-defence capability at two bases in a single night; if Bahrain's account is right, the damage was limited to a civilian site and falling debris, and the attack failed in its military purpose. Until either side produces evidence, the truth sits between two statements that cannot both be right — and the next move belongs to Washington, which has so far answered Iranian attacks with interception and denial rather than retaliation. With tankers hitting mines in the Hormuz strait and Houthi missiles flying at Saudi Arabia on the same night, the war is widening on every front at once.
