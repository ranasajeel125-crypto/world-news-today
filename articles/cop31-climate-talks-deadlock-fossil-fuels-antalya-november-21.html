COP31 Climate Talks in Deadlock as Fossil Fuel Fight Pushes Summit Into Overtime

climate · 21 Nov 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Climate Desk)

Delegates at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, where two weeks of talks have run into overtime over fossil fuels and climate finance.

The COP31 climate conference slipped into overtime on Friday as negotiators from nearly 200 countries struggled to bridge deep divisions over fossil fuels and climate finance, with talks in the Turkish Mediterranean city of Antalya running through the night and a new and potentially final agreement expected on Saturday. The summit, which opened on 9 November, was due to end on Friday, but efforts to secure consensus at climate summits have regularly blown past that deadline by more than 24 hours in recent years — and this year the divisions look deep enough to test that pattern.

The biggest points of tension centre on whether a final deal should mention the fossil fuels that are the primary driver of global warming at all, and whether it should address financial support to help poorer countries adjust to a hotter world. Climate negotiators stripped out all references to ending the use of fossil fuels in a slimmed-down draft agreement published on Thursday, following objections from Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers. An earlier draft published on Monday had contained two references to fossil fuels, including language on limiting their use. Both were removed.

The same draft also dropped mention of a $120 billion target for finance to help developing countries adapt to climate change — a figure earlier versions of the text had included, with the goal of ramping up adaptation money to $120 billion by 2035. That second cut, on money, is as important to many delegations as the first, on fossil fuels, because for the countries most exposed to rising seas, floods and drought, adaptation finance is the difference between a deal that helps them cope and one that does not.

The response from the countries pushing for a stronger text came late on Friday, in an intervention led by France and Panama. Ministers from island states, Panama, and a strong contingent of European Union nations — including Spain, France, Belgium, Sweden and Finland — said they were pushing to get a commitment to move away from oil, gas and coal back into the final agreement, after fossil fuel producers led by Saudi Arabia pushed for its removal.

"It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover document that does not even mention fossil fuels, and we simply will not put up with it," Panamanian Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro told a press conference. France's Minister of Ecological Transition, Monique Barbut, put the same point in terms of the science: "We cannot be announcing to the world that we are now getting to 1.5 increase in the temperature and do business like nothing was happening."

That reference to 1.5 degrees is at the heart of why this summit matters. The draft deal now under discussion acknowledges the projected overshoot of the Paris Agreement's temperature limit, and would mark the first time a COP cover decision has explicitly acknowledged that global warming is projected to exceed the 1.5 degrees Celsius threshold that governments agreed under the 2015 Paris Agreement to pursue efforts to respect. A UN report last month found the world is set to exceed 1.5 degrees of warming above pre-industrial levels. The agreement, known officially as the Antalya Accords, would be the first COP text to acknowledge that the goal of keeping global temperature rise below 1.5C has been missed, and that the world is entering a period scientists call "overshoot" — warming past the limit, with the hope of bringing temperatures back down later.

The draft tries to square that admission with a technological answer. It encourages the accelerated deployment of technologies that can remove greenhouse gases from the atmosphere to help eventually bring temperatures back down. But several country delegations have criticised the mention of carbon removal in the draft, saying the technologies are expensive and unproven at scale, and that including them in a deal opens the door to continued oil and gas development. "It allows developed countries to continue producing oil and gas with a claim that CCUS (carbon capture, utilisation and storage) will fix it, while we will have a bigger role to triple renewables, which is not balanced at all," said Bob Natifu, Uganda's commissioner of climate change and carbon markets.

The mood in Antalya, by the accounts of those on the ground, is tired and frustrated. Environmental groups tracking the two-week conference heaped pressure on host Turkey and lead negotiator Australia, saying the meeting was on track to be the "worst COP ever" because it lacked ambition to address the worsening climate crisis. Mohamed Adow, director of the climate think tank Power Shift Africa, was downbeat: "The COP presidents want to tell us this has been a great COP. Well, I can tell you without any doubt that this has been the worst COP ever." Ralph Regenvanu, climate minister of the South Pacific island nation of Vanuatu, which is threatened by sea level rise and worsening storms, said: "We can see where it's going, and it's not going to be a high-ambition text."

Chris Bowen, Australia's climate change and energy minister and the president of the negotiations, told reporters he remained hopeful of a deal everyone could get behind. "Things worth doing, in my experience, don't come easy," he said. "People have strong views, of course. I'm not stepping away from that, but no one has said to me 'Let's give up and go home'." Bowen has argued that this COP was about implementation, and should focus on the move towards clean energy sources rather than relitigating the fossil fuel language.

That implementation argument is where the one area of apparent progress sits: electrification. Host Turkey has pushed for countries to join a voluntary pledge to increase the global share of final energy demand met by electricity to 35 percent by 2035, an initiative backed by Britain and the European Union. The draft deal kept a goal of raising electricity's share of global final energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, but weakened the obligation on individual countries to act. Some countries are concerned the text as written does not explicitly require that extra electricity be powered by clean energy and not coal or gas. One proposal under discussion, according to the BBC, is to keep fossil fuels off the table in exchange for Saudi Arabia and other petro-states signing up to the 35 percent electricity pledge. Investors and business groups also staged a late intervention, with 123 signatories to a business statement urging an ambitious deal to accelerate electrification of the world economy.

The deadlock is not only about fossil fuels and finance. A fight over climate science has also emerged at COP31, over the timing of the next set of reports by the UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Dozens of countries want the IPCC's next assessments available before a global review of carbon-cutting efforts in 2028. And the age-old fight over money has yet to be resolved: rich nations have resisted calls to increase support for countries struggling to adapt to climate change, citing budget constraints and insisting that big emerging economies such as China also contribute.

The wider context has made agreement harder. This year's talks have been further complicated by war and rising living costs, which are competing for the attention of policymakers and the public, and the war in Iran and the oil price shock have added another layer of complexity, sharpening divisions over energy security and the future of fossil fuels. The talks also come with a series of stark reminders of the urgency of climate action, from heat waves in Europe and devastating floods in Nepal to a record-breaking El Nino wreaking more havoc around the world.

One of the world's largest emitters is not in the room at all. The United States, the world's largest historical greenhouse gas emitter, is absent from the talks for the second year in a row after President Donald Trump ended cooperation on the climate, calling global warming a hoax. The Trump administration has withdrawn from the Paris Agreement, so it sent no delegation to Antalya. OPEC members Saudi Arabia and Venezuela, meanwhile, have both made a case for a future role for fossil fuels in their official speeches to the conference, and delegates have pointed at Saudi Arabia and Russia as the countries opposing any mention of transitioning away from fossil fuels.

What happens next depends on the text expected on Saturday, and on a rule that gives every delegation a veto. The COP system operates on consensus, so any one country can object and block the whole deal — which is why a disagreement over a single phrase, whether the final text mentions fossil fuels, can hold up an agreement involving nearly 200 nations. Nations agreed at COP28 in Dubai in 2023 to transition away from fossil fuels, without setting targets or timelines, and oil-producing countries have fiercely resisted any attempt to bring the issue back to the table. Subsequent COP meetings have not made that target more specific or ambitious, and the question in Antalya is whether COP31 will even repeat it.

For the small island states, that question is not abstract. Jess Gasper, a delegate for the Pacific island nation of the Marshall Islands, told NPR: "Transitioning away from fossil fuels for the Marshall Islands and the Pacific Islands is not a political choice. It is a matter of survival." That is the divide the negotiators must close before the summit can end: between countries for whom the energy transition is a matter of economic planning, and countries for whom it is a matter of whether their homes remain above water.

Why it matters: COP31 was supposed to be the summit that turned the promises of Paris and Dubai into delivery, with a deal on clean electricity, adaptation money and a renewed commitment to move away from fossil fuels. Instead, with the talks in overtime, the draft text concedes the central temperature goal has been missed while dropping the language on fossil fuels and the money for adaptation that developing countries say they need. If the final text expected on Saturday restores either, the summit can still claim progress. If it does not, COP31 will be remembered as the conference where the world formally admitted it had overshot 1.5 degrees — and agreed, for the first time, to a climate deal that does not even name the fuels causing the warming.
