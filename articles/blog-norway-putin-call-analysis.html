What Norway's Putin Call Really Changes — and What It Does Not

blog · 28 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By World News Today (Blog Desk)

Analysis based on Reuters reporting of the 28 October 2026 call between Vladimir Putin, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian premier Jonas Gahr Stoere.

The most important thing about Tuesday's call is not what was agreed, because nothing was agreed. It is that the call happened at all. For years, Western European leaders have largely shunned direct contact with Vladimir Putin since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Norway and Finland broke that pattern with a 90-minute, three-way conversation, and the break itself is the news.

Why do it now? Timing gives the first clue. The call came ahead of expected face-to-face talks between Ukrainian and Russian officials in Abu Dhabi next month, described as the first such meeting since February. Norway's premier said the point was to make sure Putin heard European views as well as American ones, and to press practical de-escalation steps: stopping attacks on energy infrastructure, halting strikes on ships carrying Ukrainian grain, and prisoner-of-war exchanges.

The second clue is the reaction. Denmark's prime minister was among allies who criticised the call, which tells us this was not a united European move. It was a Norwegian-Finnish initiative, and it exposed a real tension: some European governments fear being sidelined in a US-led process, while others fear that talking to Putin without a united front rewards him. Both worries can be true at once, and neither is settled by one phone call.

What the call does not change is the hard core of the war. Russia still controls nearly a fifth of Ukrainian territory, and Ukraine, in Norway's premier's own framing, will not sign a deal recognising that loss, even if the current front line might become a starting point for talks. The other unresolved question is security guarantees for Ukraine after any deal — the issue Putin himself is reported to recognise as central. A call can put positions on the table; it cannot answer those two questions.

So treat this as a signal, not a breakthrough. Russia's foreign minister said the call offered nothing new, which is exactly what you would expect from a first contact after a long silence. The test is not the call, but what happens in Abu Dhabi: whether de-escalation steps are actually taken, whether European views shape the agenda, and whether the next conversation is easier than this one was. Until then, the honest summary is modest — a taboo broken, a voice asserted, and the war's hardest questions untouched.
