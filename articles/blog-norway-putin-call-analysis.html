What Norway's Putin Call Really Changes — and What It Does Not

blog · 28 Oct 2026 · 6 min read · By World News Today (Blog Desk)

Analysis based on Reuters reporting of the 28 October 2026 call between Vladimir Putin, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian premier Jonas Gahr Stoere.

Analysis based on Reuters reporting of the 28 October 2026 call between Vladimir Putin, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian premier Jonas Gahr Stoere.

The most important thing about Tuesday's call is not what was agreed, because nothing was agreed. It is that the call happened at all. For years, Western European leaders have largely shunned direct contact with Vladimir Putin since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Norway and Finland broke that pattern with a 90-minute, three-way conversation, and the break itself is the news.

Why do it now? Timing gives the first clue. The call came ahead of expected face-to-face talks between Ukrainian and Russian officials in Abu Dhabi next month, described as the first such meeting since February. Norway's premier said the point was to make sure Putin heard European views as well as American ones, and to press practical de-escalation steps: stopping attacks on energy infrastructure, halting strikes on ships carrying Ukrainian grain, and prisoner-of-war exchanges. The call was coordinated with Kyiv, and the two Nordic leaders briefed President Volodymyr Zelenskyy straight after it. It also followed an October 20 meeting in Oslo involving Nordic leaders, Zelenskyy and US special envoy Steve Witkoff, so the call was not a lone initiative sprung on allies, but part of a sequence of European diplomatic preparation for the Abu Dhabi round.

The call itself was longer than planned. It was originally scheduled for less than 30 minutes but lasted 90, and Stubb, who described the conversation as direct and businesslike, said it was his first conversation with Putin in two and a half years as president. That gap captures the taboo in miniature: Finland shares a roughly 1,340-kilometre border with Russia, yet its head of state had not spoken to the Russian president for two and a half years. Finland joined NATO in April 2023, doubling the alliance's land border with Russia, and Norway is a founding member of the alliance, so both leaders speak from countries that are firmly inside the Western security architecture, not from its margins. The Kremlin's account of the call was that Putin used much of it to lecture about medieval Slavic history and the "initial reasons" for the conflict, rather than discuss a ceasefire, and Ukraine's main demand for an immediate ceasefire was barely discussed. It was also the first contact between Putin and Ukraine's European allies since July.

The second clue is the reaction. Denmark's prime minister was among allies who criticised the call, which tells us this was not a united European move. It was a Norwegian-Finnish initiative, and it exposed a real tension: some European governments fear being sidelined in a US-led process, while others fear that talking to Putin without a united front rewards him. Both worries can be true at once, and neither is settled by one phone call. The fear of being sidelined was not hypothetical. Russia later ruled out European participation in the Abu Dhabi talks, saying the negotiations would follow an agreed trilateral format involving Russia, Ukraine and the United States as mediator, and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the format had been agreed without European countries. That made the Nordic call look, in retrospect, like an attempt to get European views heard before the door closed, rather than a step towards a seat at the table.

What the call does not change is the hard core of the war. Russia still controls nearly a fifth of Ukrainian territory, and Ukraine, in Norway's premier's own framing, will not sign a deal recognising that loss, even if the current front line might become a starting point for talks. The other unresolved question is security guarantees for Ukraine after any deal — the issue Putin himself is reported to recognise as central. A call can put positions on the table; it cannot answer those two questions. The structure of the disagreement is a sequencing problem as much as a territorial one. Ukraine wants the fighting to stop first and the settlement to follow, while Moscow says security guarantees must be agreed before a settlement, and Russia has insisted that any guarantees "can only be mutual". That deadlock is why partial, technical truces became the focus of the diplomacy that followed.

What happened next in Abu Dhabi shows both the value and the limits of that approach. The trilateral meeting took place on 10–11 November 2026, mediated by the United States, with Ukraine's priorities — as set out by Foreign Minister Andriy Sybiha — being security guarantees, de-escalation in the Black Sea, an energy truce, the return of children and prisoner exchanges. Zelenskyy set out four priorities: a ceasefire covering energy facilities and critical infrastructure, the lifting of restrictions on Black Sea shipping to resume grain exports, more frequent prisoner exchanges under an "all-for-all" formula, and preparations for a trilateral meeting at the leaders' level. Ukraine offered Russia a truce in the Black Sea and on energy infrastructure, and Kyiv tabled specific proposals rather than general ideas. The second and final day ended with one concrete result: Russia and Ukraine agreed in principle on a large-scale prisoner exchange, with the sides discussing a swap of 1,000-for-1,000 prisoners, perhaps 1,200, to take place in the next two to three weeks once its procedure had been agreed. But on the energy and sea ceasefire, Zelenskyy said the Russian side said it had no mandate to respond, and the issues were passed to US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, who were due in Moscow on 12 November to put them to Putin directly. Attacks on energy infrastructure by both sides continued through the talks, with more than 20 targeted strikes on Ukrainian energy facilities in one 24-hour period forcing emergency power cuts in several regions, including Kyiv.

So treat this as a signal, not a breakthrough. Russia's foreign minister said the call offered nothing new, which is exactly what you would expect from a first contact after a long silence. The test is not the call, but what happens in Abu Dhabi: whether de-escalation steps are actually taken, whether European views shape the agenda, and whether the next conversation is easier than this one was. On the first two tests, the answer so far is mixed at best: a prisoner exchange was agreed in principle, but the energy and sea truce was not, and Europe was kept out of the room. Zelenskyy's response was to demand that Europe be an equal participant next time rather than on the sidelines, which is precisely the position the Nordic call was trying to stake out. Until then, the honest summary is modest — a taboo broken, a voice asserted, and the war's hardest questions untouched.
